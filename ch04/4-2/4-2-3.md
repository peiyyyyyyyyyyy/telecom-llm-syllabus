# 4.2.3 實體層處理流程

所屬：[4.2 基地台軟體](../4-2.md)

## 正文

The physical (PHY) layer in a 5G‑NR base station converts higher‑layer bits into radio‑frequency (RF) waveforms and performs the inverse operation on received signals. This chain is deterministic: *scrambling* randomises the bit pattern, *modulation mapping* converts groups of bits into complex symbols, and *sequence generation* provides reference‑signal patterns needed for channel estimation. Correct implementation is essential because any deviation changes the constellation seen by the UE, leading to decoding failures or increased error rate. The flow described below follows the procedures defined in **3GPP TS 38.211** for both uplink (PUSCH/PUCCH) and downlink (PDSCH/PDCCH) channels [TS 38.211 §5], [TS 38.211 §6], [TS 38.211 §7].

### Scrambling of transport‑block bits
Before modulation each codeword $q\in\{0,1\}$ is scrambled with a binary pseudo‑random sequence $c^{(q)}(i)$. The scrambled bits are  

$$
\widetilde{b}^{(q)}(i)=\bigl(b^{(q)}(i)+c^{(q)}(i)\bigr)\bmod 2 ,
$$

where $b^{(q)}(i)$ are the original bits. The sequence generator is a length‑31 Gold sequence (clause 5.2.1) whose initial state $c_{\text{init}}$ depends on the transmission context.  

*Uplink (PUSCH) non‑msgA*  

$$
c_{\text{init}} = n_{\text{RNTI}}\cdot2^{15}+q\cdot2^{14}+n_{\text{ID}} .
$$

*Downlink (PDSCH)* uses the same expression (see §7.3.1.1). The identifiers $n_{\text{RNTI}}$, $q$, and $n_{\text{ID}}$ are defined by higher‑layer parameters (RNTI, codeword index, scrambling identity) [TS 38.211 §6.3.1.1], [TS 38.211 §7.3.1.1] [TS 38.211 §6.3.1.1].

### Modulation mapping
After scrambling, groups of bits are mapped to complex symbols by the **modulation mapper** (clause 5.1). The mapper supports several constellations; the most demanding is 1024‑QAM, which maps **10 bits** (a *hextuplet*) to one symbol $d(i)$. The mapping formula is  

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(i)=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\Bigl(1-2b(10i+0)\Bigr)
\Bigl[16-(1-2b(10i+2))\bigl[8-(1-2b(10i+4))\\
&\qquad\bigl[4-(1-2b(10i+6))\bigl[2-(1-2b(10i+8))\bigr]\bigr]\bigr]\bigr] \\
&+j\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\Bigl(1-2b(10i+1)\Bigr)
\Bigl[16-(1-2b(10i+3))\bigl[8-(1-2b(10i+5))\\
&\qquad\bigl[4-(1-2b(10i+7))\bigl[2-(1-2b(10i+9))\bigr]\bigr]\bigr]\bigr] .
\end{aligned}
$$

All other supported schemes (π/2‑BPSK, BPSK, QPSK, 16‑QAM, 64‑QAM, 256‑QAM) follow analogous but shorter bit‑groupings [TS 38.211 §5.1.7].

### Sequence generation for reference signals
Reference signals (DM‑RS, PT‑RS, SRS, etc.) are generated by the pseudo‑random sequence described in clause 5.2.1. The sequence length is  

$$
N_{\text{C}} = 1600 ,
$$

and the first m‑sequence is initialised as specified in the clause [TS 38.211 §5.2.1]. Low‑PAPR Zadoff‑Chu sequences (clause 5.2.2) are derived from a cyclic shift of a base sequence, but their generation follows the same deterministic rules.

### Antenna‑port allocation
The PHY maps the generated symbols onto *antenna ports* defined separately for uplink and downlink.  

*Uplink* – ports 0 (DM‑RS for PUSCH), 1000 (SRS, PUSCH), 2000 (PUCCH), 4000 (PRACH) [TS 38.211 §6.2].  

*Downlink* – ports 1000 (PDSCH), 2000 (PDCCH), 3000 (CSI‑RS), 4000 (SS/PBCH block), 5000 (positioning reference) [TS 38.211 §7.2].

The UE may infer the channel of a data symbol from the associated reference‑signal symbol only when they share the same antenna port and belong to the same resource window (see clauses 6.3.1.1 and 7.3.1.1).

### Worked example: 1024‑QAM mapping
Assume the scrambled bit sequence for a single symbol is  

$$
\begin{array}{c}
b(0)=0,\; b(1)=1,\; b(2)=0,\; b(3)=0,\; b(4)=1,\\
b(5)=1,\; b(6)=0,\; b(7)=1,\; b(8)=0,\; b(9)=1 .
\end{array}
$$

Compute the real part:  

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Re\{d(0)\}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}(1-2\cdot0)
\bigl[16-(1-2\cdot0)[8-(1-2\cdot1)[4-(1-2\cdot1)[2-(1-2\cdot0)]]]\bigr] \\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\times1\times[16-1[8-(-1)[4-(-1)[2-1]]]]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\times[16- (8+4-2)] =\frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\times10 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly for the imaginary part (using $b(1),b(3),\dots$) we obtain  

$$
\Im\{d(0)\}= \frac{1}{\sqrt{682}}\times(-1)\times[16- (8- (4- (2-1)))] = -\frac{8}{\sqrt{682}} .
$$

Thus the complex symbol is  

$$
d(0)=\frac{10}{\sqrt{682}}-j\frac{8}{\sqrt{682}} .
$$

The symbol is then placed on the appropriate antenna port (e.g., port 1000 for downlink PDSCH) and transmitted over the allocated resource elements.

### Common pitfalls and practical notes
* **Incorrect $c_{\text{init}}$** – Using the wrong $n_{\text{ID}}$ or forgetting the $q$ term leads to a mismatched scrambling sequence and a burst of bit errors.  
* **Wrong bit grouping** – 1024‑QAM requires exactly ten bits per symbol; any off‑by‑one error produces an undefined mapping.  
* **Antenna‑port mismatch** – Assigning a downlink PDSCH symbol to an uplink‑only port (e.g., 0) violates the defined port ranges and prevents the UE from locating the reference signal.  
* **Sequence length confusion** – The pseudo‑random sequence length is fixed at $N_{\text{C}}=1600$; using a different length breaks the Gold‑sequence properties required for DM‑RS.  

Adhering strictly to the formulas and initialisation values defined in TS 38.211 guarantees interoperability between open‑source base‑station implementations and commercial UE devices.
