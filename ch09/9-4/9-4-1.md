# 9.4.1 影響範圍

所屬：[9.4 變更前檢查](../9-4.md)

## 正文

In a PLMN, any change to a network element (NE) or network resource (NR) can affect the service that customers experience. The *impact scope* of a change describes **what parts of the network (physical equipment, executable software/firmware, and logical data) are touched, and how those parts may influence traffic, capacity, or the state of other NEs/NRs**. Understanding the impact scope is the first step in the pre‑change check because it determines the level of precaution (e.g., taking resources out of service) that must be applied before the modification proceeds. The 3GPP specification explicitly groups the possible impacts into three management aspects: physical, executable, and logical/functional [TS 32.600 §6.1].

### Physical, executable, and logical dimensions

The reference defines the three aspects as follows:

- **Physical aspect** – the actual equipment (antennas, radio units, power supplies).  
- **Executable aspect** – the software (SW) and firmware (FW) that run on the equipment.  
- **Logical/functional aspect** – the configuration data that drives the behavior of the equipment (e.g., neighbour‑cell lists, timers).  

These are enumerated in the standard:  

> “There are three aspects of NE and NR management, which can be distinguished: 1) Management of the physical aspect (equipment); 2) Management of the executable aspect (SW and FW); and 3) Management of the logical/ functional aspect (data).” [TS 32.600 §6.1]

A change may affect one, two, or all three aspects. For instance, creating a new cell typically **affects equipment (physical antenna deployment), software (new radio configuration) and data (cell‑ID, frequency, neighbour relations)**, as the specification states:  

> “The creation can affect equipment, SW and data.” [TS 32.600 §6.1.1]

### Quantifying impact scope

While the standard does not prescribe a numerical formula, engineers often express the scope as a sum of the three dimensions:  

$$
\text{ImpactScope} = P + E + L
$$  

where  

* $P$ = number of physical resources (e.g., racks, antennas) that will be altered,  
* $E$ = number of executable items (e.g., SW modules, FW versions) that will be changed,  
* $L$ = number of logical data elements (e.g., neighbour‑cell entries, timers) that will be modified.  

All three terms are dimensionless counts; the resulting $\text{ImpactScope}$ is a simple indicator of how widespread a modification is. A higher value suggests a larger disturbance potential and therefore a stricter pre‑change verification.

### Worked example: Adding a new 4G cell

Assume an operator wants to add a new LTE cell in an existing site. The planned actions are:

| Aspect | Items affected | Count |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| Physical ($P$) | New antenna panel, cabling | 2 |
| Executable ($E$) | Radio firmware version update on the eNodeB | 1 |
| Logical ($L$) | Cell ID, EARFCN, neighbour‑cell list entries | 3 |

The impact scope is  

$$
\text{ImpactScope} = 2 + 1 + 3 = 6
$$  

Because the scope is moderate (6), the operator decides to **lock the MO/NR, perform the modification, then unlock**, following the conditioning sequence recommended by the specification:  

> “When an MO/NR is to be modified the following actions shall be performed: − Locking or logical removal of the MO/NR …; − Required modification (physical and/or logical); and − Unlocking or logical re‑installation of the MO/NR.” [TS 32.600 §6.1.3]

Before proceeding, the operator checks the three guiding questions from the standard:

1. Influence on ongoing traffic? – The new cell will initially carry no traffic, so impact is minimal.  
2. Impact on capacity? – Adds capacity in the target area; no reduction elsewhere.  
3. Difficulty and time‑consumption? – Physical installation requires a crew for 2 hours; software and data changes take 30 minutes.

Since the impact scope does not require taking the whole site out of service, only the specific eNodeB is temporarily locked, satisfying the *minimum disturbance* requirement:  

> “minimum disturbance of the network by taking the affected resources out of service if needed;” [TS 32.600 §6.1]

### Practical notes and common pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | Correct practice |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| **Assuming logical changes are always safe** | Operators may forget that data modifications can affect throughput (e.g., changing a timer that controls handover). | Verify “controlling influence” of data changes: “A modification to data, which has a controlling influence on some resources, could influence the resource throughput … during the modification time.” [TS 32.600 §6.1.3] |
| **Skipping the out‑of‑service step** | Belief that a small logical change does not need locking. | Follow the rule: “taking the affected NEs or NRs out of service prior to their removal” protects the network (deletion case) and “locking … before modification” protects conditioning. |
| **Changing physical and logical order arbitrarily** | Operators may think the order is irrelevant. | The specification allows flexible order **only** if the resource has already been taken out of service: “The sequence of logical and physical removal will not matter if the affected NEs are taken out of service prior to their removal.” [TS 32.600 §6.1.2] |
| **Ignoring the three‑aspect classification** | Overlooking that a change may span multiple aspects leads to incomplete impact analysis. | Explicitly list $P$, $E$, $L$ as in the example and compute $\text{ImpactScope}$. |
| **Neglecting data‑consistency checks** | Assuming the modification will succeed automatically. | Perform the mandatory checks: “data consistency checks shall be performed as described in subclause 4.2.1.3.” [TS 32.600 §6.1] |

By rigorously assessing the impact scope across physical, executable, and logical dimensions, operators can decide whether a change can be performed live, needs a scheduled outage, or must be postponed until capacity is available. This disciplined approach reduces the risk of unexpected service degradation and aligns with the core requirements of the CM functions defined in TS 32.600.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following is NOT listed as a system modification function in TS 32.600 §6.1?

- A. Creation of Network Elements and Network Resources
- B. Deletion of Network Elements and Network Resources
- C. Conditioning of Network Elements and Network Resources
- D. Migration of Network Elements

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：D。The specification enumerates creation, deletion, and conditioning; migration is not mentioned.

</details>

**2.** Before a resource can be put back into service after a modification, what must be ensured?

- A. Only physical installation is required.
- B. Only logical installation is required.
- C. All required actions are completed correctly.
- D. No actions are needed if the modification is minor.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。The standard mandates that all actions be correctly finished before service restoration.

</details>

**3.** In the conditioning process, which step is performed first?

- A. Unlocking the MO/NR.
- B. Required modification.
- C. Locking or logical removal of the MO/NR.
- D. Verification of the result.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。The standard lists locking/logical removal as the initial action.

</details>

**4.** A modification to which type of data can directly affect resource throughput during the change?

- A. Physical equipment specifications.
- B. Executable software version numbers.
- C. Logical data with controlling influence.
- D. None of the above.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。Data that controls resources can impact throughput while being modified.

</details>
