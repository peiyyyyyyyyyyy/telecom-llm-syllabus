# 5.4.1 正常基線

所屬：[5.4 指標趨勢](../5-4.md)

## 正文

In performance‑management (PM) and key‑performance‑indicator (KPI) analytics, a *normal baseline* represents the statistically derived “typical” value of a metric against which current observations are compared. Establishing a baseline is the first step in detecting anomalies, spikes, or capacity trends because it defines what the system *normally* looks like under expected load conditions. The 3GPP specification explicitly states that analytics may compute *statistics of the management data* over a past period, which is precisely how a baseline is formed【TS 28.104 §7.2.8】.

### Statistical Foundations
A baseline is most often expressed by the mean (average) $\mu$ and, optionally, the standard deviation $\sigma$ of a series of measurements $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N\}$ collected over a defined observation window.  

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i
$$

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \mu)^2}
$$

* $x_i$ – individual measurement of a PM or KPI (unit depends on the metric, e.g., Mbps, %).  
* $N$ – number of samples within the observation window (the *period in the past* referenced by the standard).  

The specification notes that statistics may be generated “over a period in the past” or by a “sliding window procedure”【TS 28.104 §7.2.8】. A sliding window continuously discards the oldest sample and adds the newest, keeping the baseline up‑to‑date without re‑computing from scratch.

### Worked Example
Consider the downlink throughput KPI measured every 5 minutes for a small cell. Over the last 12 samples (i.e., one hour), the values (in Mbps) are:

`45, 48, 47, 46, 49, 50, 48, 47, 46, 48, 49, 47`

1. Compute the mean:  

$$
\mu = \frac{45+48+47+46+49+50+48+47+46+48+49+47}{12}
     = \frac{572}{12}
     \approx 47.7\ \text{Mbps}
$$

2. Compute the standard deviation:  

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{11}\sum_{i=1}^{12}(x_i-47.7)^2}
       \approx 1.6\ \text{Mbps}
$$

Thus the **normal baseline** for this KPI is $47.7\pm1.6$ Mbps. Any real‑time measurement that exceeds $\mu + 3\sigma \approx 52.5$ Mbps could be flagged for further analysis as a potential spike or anomaly.

### Using the Baseline for Threshold Assessment
Operators often configure static thresholds for alarm generation. The reference warns that thresholds set “too high or too low” may generate spurious alarms because they do not adapt to traffic fluctuations【TS 28.104 §7.2.8】. By comparing live measurements against the statistically derived baseline, the Management Data Analytics (MDA) function can *assess the validity of threshold configuration* and even suggest auto‑configuration actions【TS 28.104 §7.2.8】. This assessment is part of requirement **REQ‑SPM\_MDA‑0A**, which mandates analytics that evaluate threshold configuration and recommend optimisations.

### Common Mistakes and Practical Tips

| Mistake | Why It Happens | Correct Practice |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| **Using an overly short observation window** (e.g., a single hour for a metric that varies daily) | Insufficient data leads to a baseline that reflects transient conditions rather than true normal behaviour. | Choose a window that captures the typical diurnal/weekly pattern; a sliding window can help maintain relevance while retaining enough history. |
| **Ignoring variance** and relying only on the mean | The mean alone cannot indicate the expected range of normal fluctuations, causing false positives/negatives. | Compute and store $\sigma$ (or other percentiles) to define confidence bands around the baseline. |
| **Static thresholds unrelated to the baseline** | Thresholds set without reference to the baseline may be misaligned with actual traffic levels. | Use the baseline statistics to derive dynamic thresholds (e.g., $\mu \pm 3\sigma$) and periodically re‑evaluate them with updated statistics. |
| **Not updating the baseline after network upgrades** | New equipment or configuration changes shift the normal operating point, rendering the old baseline obsolete. | Re‑calculate the baseline after any major change, or employ a sliding‑window approach that naturally adapts over time. |

By grounding alarm logic in statistically sound baselines, engineers can reduce false alarms, improve root‑cause analysis speed, and provide a solid foundation for higher‑level analytics such as load balancing or mobility optimisation【TS 28.104 §7.2.8】.

- A. Only the mean value of a metric over a past period.
