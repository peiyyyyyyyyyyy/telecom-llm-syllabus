### 1.3.2.1 What is SMS and Why It Matters  
Short Message Service (SMS) is a packet‑switched, store‑and‑forward messaging protocol defined originally in the GSM specifications and retained throughout UMTS, LTE, and 5G releases. Although the payload is limited to a few hundred characters, SMS remains a cornerstone service for:

* **Human‑to‑Human (H2H) communication** – reliable, low‑cost texting that works even on low‑signal or idle devices.  
* **Machine‑to‑Human (M2H) notifications** – alarms, OTPs (one‑time passwords), and service alerts that must reach the user quickly and with high reliability.  
* **Machine‑to‑Machine (M2M) signalling** – lightweight telemetry for IoT devices that cannot afford the overhead of IP‑based protocols.

Because SMS is carried over the signaling channels (the *SCCP* in GSM/UMTS and the *NAS* in LTE/5G) rather than the user‑plane, it can be delivered while the device is in idle mode, making it uniquely suitable for emergency alerts and network‑wide broadcast (e.g., Cell Broadcast Service, CBS).

### 1.3.2.2 Core Definitions  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit / Typical Value |
|--------|---------|----------------------|
| $L$ | Length of the user‑visible text (characters) | characters |
| $C_{7}$ | Payload capacity per segment for 7‑bit default alphabet | 160 characters |
| $C_{U}$ | Payload capacity per segment for UCS‑2 (Unicode) | 70 characters |
| $U_{udh}$ | User Data Header length (bytes) used for concatenation, EMS, etc. | 6 – 7 bytes |
| $C_{seg}$ | Effective payload per concatenated segment | $C - \frac{U_{udh}\times8}{b}$ characters, where $b$ = bits per character (7 or 16) |
| $N_{seg}$ | Number of SMS segments required for a given message | integer |
| $T_{proc}$ | Processing time at SMSC (SMS Center) | ms |
| $T_{queue}$ | Queueing delay within the operator network | ms |
| $T_{trans}$ | Transmission time over the air interface (including retransmissions) | ms |
| $T_{avg}$ | Average end‑to‑end delivery latency | $T_{proc}+T_{queue}+T_{trans}$ |
| $N_{sent}$ | Number of SMS messages transmitted by the originating entity | count |
| $N_{delivered}$ | Number of SMS messages successfully delivered to the recipient’s handset | count |
| $DSR$ | Delivery Success Rate | $\displaystyle DSR = \frac{N_{delivered}}{N_{sent}}\times100\%$ |

**SMS payload limits**  
* The TP‑User‑Data (TP‑UD) field is limited to 140 bytes (1120 bits).  
* With the 7‑bit default alphabet, 140 bytes → $140\times8/7 = 160$ characters.  
* With UCS‑2 (16‑bit) encoding, 140 bytes → $140\times8/16 = 70$ characters.  

When a message exceeds these limits, the sender’s device creates a *concatenated SMS* (also called *multipart SMS*). Each segment carries a User Data Header (UDH) that identifies the total number of parts and the sequence number. The UDH consumes 6 bytes (48 bits) for the concatenation IE, reducing the per‑segment payload:

$$
C_{seg}^{7} = 160 - \left\lceil\frac{U_{udh}\times8}{7}\right\rceil
\qquad
C_{seg}^{U} = 70 - \left\lceil\frac{U_{udh}\times8}{16}\right\rceil
$$

The required number of segments is:

$$
N_{seg}= \left\lceil \frac{L}{C_{seg}} \right\rceil
$$

### 1.3.2.3 Worked Example  

A user wants to send a bilingual message containing 250 characters, where 180 characters are Latin (7‑bit) and the remaining 70 characters are Chinese (UCS‑2). The device must decide how many SMS segments are needed.

1. **Determine encoding per part**  
   *Latin part*: 180 characters → fits in one 7‑bit segment (capacity 160). Since 180 > 160, it needs two segments.  
   *Chinese part*: 70 characters → exactly one UCS‑2 segment (capacity 70).

2. **Calculate payload after UDH** (concatenation required for the Latin part)  
   *Udh length* = 6 bytes → 48 bits.  
   For 7‑bit:  

   $$
   C_{seg}^{7}=160-\left\lceil\frac{48}{7}\right\rceil=160-7=153\text{ characters}
   $$

   Hence the Latin part needs  

   $$
   N_{seg}^{7}= \left\lceil\frac{180}{153}\right\rceil = 2\text{ segments}
   $$

   For the Chinese part, no concatenation is needed, so full 70‑character capacity is available.

3. **Total segments**  

$$
N_{total}= N_{seg}^{7}+ N_{seg}^{U}=2+1=3\text{ SMS PDUs}
$$

4. **Cost estimate** (assuming $0.05 per SMS)  

$$
\text{Cost}=3\times0.05\ \text{USD}=0.15\ \text{USD}
$$

Thus the bilingual message will be transmitted as three concatenated SMS PDUs, costing $0.15.

### 1.3.2.4 Performance Metrics  

* **Latency** – In a well‑engineered core, $T_{proc}$ ≈ 10 ms, $T_{queue}$ ≈ 20‑50 ms, and $T_{trans}$ depends on radio conditions (typically 30‑100 ms). Hence $T_{avg}$ is often below 200 ms for a single‑segment SMS, but can increase to several seconds for concatenated messages due to additional store‑and‑forward steps.

* **Reliability** – Operators target a Delivery Success Rate (DSR) > 99 % for domestic traffic. International roaming and inter‑operator hand‑over may lower DSR because of differing SMSC implementations and possible filtering.

* **Traffic Load** – For a network handling $N_{SMS}=2\times10^{6}$ messages per hour, the average arrival rate is  

$$
\lambda = \frac{2\times10^{6}}{3600\ \text{s}} \approx 556\ \text{SMS/s}
$$

The SMSC must be dimensioned to process this load with adequate queuing capacity.

### 1.3.2.5 Common Mistakes and Practical Notes  

| Pitfall | Why It Happens | Mitigation |
|---------|----------------|------------|
| **Assuming 160‑character limit for all languages** | Unicode characters require 2 bytes each, halving the capacity. | Detect the required DCS (Data Coding Scheme) and apply the correct $C$ value. |
| **Neglecting UDH overhead in concatenated SMS** | Forgetting that each segment loses payload to the header leads to under‑estimating $N_{seg}$. | Always subtract $\lceil U_{udh}\times8/b\rceil$ from the per‑segment capacity. |
| **Overlooking network‑induced duplication** | SMSC may retransmit after a timeout, resulting in duplicate messages at the handset. | Implement duplicate detection using the TP‑Message‑Reference (TP‑MR) field. |
| **Relying on SMS for time‑critical alerts** | Although SMS is fast, worst‑case latency can exceed a few seconds, especially under congestion. | For mission‑critical alerts, use Cell Broadcast Service (CBS) or dedicated push‑notification channels. |
| **Assuming uniform DSR across roaming partners** | Different operators apply varying filtering and charging policies. | Monitor inter‑operator DSR and consider fallback mechanisms (e.g., OTT messaging) for high‑value traffic. |

**Key Takeaway:** SMS remains a highly reliable, low‑overhead messaging service, but engineers must respect encoding limits, header overhead, and network latency to guarantee cost‑effective delivery and to meet service‑level expectations.
