# 1.5.2 RAN 智慧控制器：Near-RT RIC、Non-RT RIC、xApp、rApp

所屬：[1.5 開放與虛擬化](../1-5.md)

> 本頁正文與練習題由本機模型 gpt-oss-120b 撰寫，經程式驗算與另一個模型（Gemma-4-31B）交叉驗題，尚待專家審閱。

## 正文

The Open RAN Alliance (O‑RAN) introduces a programmable control plane for the Radio Access Network (RAN) called the **RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC)**. By separating control logic from the underlying radio hardware, the RIC enables rapid deployment of new algorithms (e.g., AI/ML‑based load balancing, interference mitigation, or QoS enforcement) without changing the O‑RU/O‑DU/O‑CU software stacks. In practice, operators can roll out a new feature across a multi‑vendor RAN in days rather than months, improving spectrum efficiency, reducing operational expenditure (OPEX), and supporting new use cases such as private‑network slicing.

The RIC is split into two timing domains:

| Domain | Typical decision latency | Typical workload | Primary interface |
|--------|--------------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Near‑RT RIC** | 10 ms – 1 s (real‑time to near‑real‑time) | Per‑slot or per‑sub‑frame optimizations (e.g., handover, scheduling) | **E2** (RAN‑Node ↔ Near‑RT RIC) |
| **Non‑RT RIC** | > 1 s (policy, training, analytics) | Long‑term optimization, model training, policy generation | **A1** (Non‑RT RIC ↔ Near‑RT RIC) |

Both domains host **applications** that implement specific control functions:

* **xApp** – runs on the Near‑RT RIC, processes fast‑changing RAN measurements, and issues control actions via the E2 interface.
* **rApp** – runs on the Non‑RT RIC, performs analytics, policy generation, or machine‑learning model training, and pushes policies to xApps via the A1 interface.

Below we detail each component, the data flow, and a simple quantitative example.

---

### 1.5.2.1 Near‑RT RIC

The Near‑RT RIC receives **E2 measurement reports** (e.g., PRB usage, RLC buffer status, SINR) from O‑DU/O‑CU nodes. It stores the data in a time‑series database and forwards a subset to registered xApps. An xApp may request additional metrics using **E2 subscription** messages, specifying a reporting period $T_{rep}$ (seconds) and a latency budget $L_{max}$ (seconds). The Near‑RT RIC guarantees that the end‑to‑end latency from radio measurement to xApp decision does not exceed $L_{max}$.

A typical latency budget:

$$
L_{total}=L_{radio}+L_{proc}+L_{ctrl}\le L_{max}
$$

where  

* $L_{radio}$ – propagation + processing time in O‑RU/O‑DU (≈ 0.5 ms for fronthaul).  
* $L_{proc}$ – time to ingest, store, and forward the report (≈ 1 ms).  
* $L_{ctrl}$ – time for the xApp to compute the control action (depends on algorithm, often ≤ 5 ms).

If $L_{max}=10$ ms, the xApp must finish within $L_{ctrl}\le 3.5$ ms.

---

### 1.5.2.2 Non‑RT RIC

The Non‑RT RIC operates on a longer time scale. It collects **KPM (Key Performance Measurement)** data from the Near‑RT RIC, runs analytics or trains ML models, and publishes **policy objects** (e.g., thresholds, weight vectors) to the Near‑RT RIC. The interface is **A1**, which defines a **policy subscription** and **policy update** procedure.

A common formulation for a reinforcement‑learning (RL) policy is a weighted sum of KPIs:

$$
\Phi = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{KPI}_i,
$$

where  

* $w_i$ – policy weight (dimensionless), $\sum w_i = 1$.  
* $\text{KPI}_i$ – normalized key performance indicator (0 – 1).  

The Non‑RT RIC periodically recomputes $w_i$ based on long‑term objectives (e.g., maximizing cell‑edge throughput while minimizing power).

---

### 1.5.2.3 xApp and rApp Lifecycle

| Step | Actor | Action |
|------|-------|--------|
| 1 | **rApp** (Non‑RT) | Pull KPM from Near‑RT RIC, train model, compute new policy $\Phi$. |
| 2 | **rApp** | Push policy to Near‑RT RIC via **A1** `PolicyUpdate`. |
| 3 | **Near‑RT RIC** | Distribute policy to relevant xApps. |
| 4 | **xApp** | Subscribe to E2 measurements, apply policy $\Phi$ to decide control actions (e.g., adjust scheduling weight). |
| 5 | **xApp** | Issue **E2 Control** messages (e.g., `RANParameterModification`) to O‑DU/O‑CU. |
| 6 | **Radio node** | Enforce the new configuration. |

### 1.5.2.4 Worked Example: Load‑Balancing xApp

Consider a small urban cell cluster with two neighboring cells, **Cell A** and **Cell B**. Each cell reports its **PRB utilization** $U$ (percentage) every $T_{rep}=100$ ms. The Near‑RT RIC forwards the latest values to a load‑balancing xApp that decides whether to offload users from the overloaded cell.

**Given data**

| Cell | PRB utilization $U$ (%) |
|------|--------------------------|
| A    | 85 |
| B    | 45 |

The xApp uses the simple rule:

$$
\text{If } U_A > U_{th} \text{ and } U_B < U_{th}^{low} \text{ then trigger handover of } N_{UE} \text{ users.}
$$

where  

* $U_{th}=80\%$ (overload threshold).  
* $U_{th}^{low}=60\%$ (under‑load threshold).  
* $N_{UE}= \left\lfloor \frac{U_A-U_{th}}{U_A} \times N_{total} \right\rfloor$ with $N_{total}=20$ active users in Cell A.

**Computation**

1. $U_A > U_{th}$ (85 > 80) → condition satisfied.  
2. $U_B < U_{th}^{low}$ (45 < 60) → condition satisfied.  
3. Compute $N_{UE}$:  

   $$
   N_{UE}= \left\lfloor \frac{85-80}{85} \times 20 \right\rfloor
          = \left\lfloor \frac{5}{85} \times 20 \right\rfloor
          = \left\lfloor 1.176 \right\rfloor = 1.
   $$

The xApp issues an **E2 Control** message to the O‑DU of Cell A requesting a handover of one UE to Cell B. Assuming the handover execution time $t_{ho}=30$ ms, the total latency from measurement to handover completion is:

$$
L_{total}=L_{radio}+L_{proc}+L_{ctrl}+t_{ho}=0.5\text{ ms}+1\text{ ms}+2\text{ ms}+30\text{ ms}=33.5\text{ ms},
$$

well below the allowed $L_{max}=100$ ms for this use case.

### 1.5.2.5 Common Mistakes & Practical Notes

| Mistake | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Confusing xApp/rApp scope** – treating an xApp as a long‑term optimizer. | xApps must respect the near‑real‑time latency budget; heavy ML training will miss the deadline. | Keep computationally intensive tasks in rApps; use pre‑trained models or lightweight inference in xApps. |
| **Ignoring the A1/E2 version mismatch** – deploying an xApp built for A1‑v2 on a Near‑RT RIC that only supports A1‑v1. | The xApp may fail to parse policy updates, leading to silent loss of control. | Verify the RIC’s supported interface versions during onboarding; use version negotiation in the manifest. |
| **Over‑subscribing to measurements** – requesting sub‑millisecond reporting intervals for many KPIs. | Saturates the E2 link, increases $L_{proc}$, and can cause packet loss. | Apply **measurement pruning**: subscribe only to KPIs needed by the xApp, and aggregate where possible. |
| **Assuming deterministic latency** – treating $L_{total}$ as a fixed number. | Radio and processing delays are stochastic; occasional spikes can breach $L_{max}$, causing missed control actions. | Design xApps with a safety margin (e.g., allocate only 70 % of $L_{max}$ for computation). |
| **Hard‑coding policy parameters** – embedding static thresholds in the xApp code. | Reduces flexibility; operators cannot adapt to traffic changes without redeploying the xApp. | Externalize thresholds via A1 policy objects; allow rApps to update them dynamically. |

**Practical tip:** When developing an xApp, start with a **simulation environment** (e.g., O‑RAN Software Community’s near‑RT RIC emulator) to measure $L_{proc}$ and $L_{ctrl}$ under realistic load. Only after meeting the latency budget should the code be containerized for deployment on the production Near‑RT RIC.
