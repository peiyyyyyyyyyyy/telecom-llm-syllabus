### 1. Why millimeter‑wave (mmWave) and terahertz (THz) matter

The explosive growth of mobile data traffic, driven by ultra‑high‑definition video, augmented reality, and massive machine‑type communications, forces network designers to seek spectrum far beyond the traditional sub‑6 GHz bands. The **Frequency Range 2 (FR2)** defined by 3GPP TS 38.300 spans **24 GHz – 52.6 GHz** (the mmWave band). Above 100 GHz lies the **terahertz (THz) window** (0.1 – 10 THz). These bands offer:

* **Gigahertz‑level contiguous bandwidths** (e.g., 400 MHz per carrier in FR2, several GHz in THz), enabling multi‑Gb/s physical‑layer rates.
* **Small wavelengths** (λ ≈ 1–10 mm at 30 GHz, λ ≈ 0.1 mm at 3 THz) that allow dense antenna arrays and ultra‑narrow beams, which increase spatial reuse and mitigate interference.
* **New propagation phenomena** (severe free‑space loss, molecular absorption, surface roughness scattering) that must be modeled accurately for reliable link budgeting.

Consequently, mmWave and THz are core enablers for **6G** use cases such as holographic telepresence, tactile internet, and integrated sensing‑communication (ISAC).

### 2. Fundamental definitions and formulas

#### 2.1 Free‑space path loss (FSPL)

The Friis transmission equation gives the received power $P_{\mathrm{rx}}$ (in watts) for a line‑of‑sight (LoS) link:

$$
P_{\mathrm{rx}} = P_{\mathrm{tx}}\, G_{\mathrm{tx}}\, G_{\mathrm{rx}} \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^{\!2},
$$

where  

* $P_{\mathrm{tx}}$ – transmit power (W)  
* $G_{\mathrm{tx}}, G_{\mathrm{rx}}$ – linear (dimensionless) antenna gains of the transmitter and receiver  
* $\lambda$ – wavelength (m), $\lambda = c/f$ with $c = 3\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ and $f$ the carrier frequency (Hz)  
* $d$ – link distance (m)

In decibel form:

$$
\boxed{ \mathrm{FSPL(dB)} = 20\log_{10}(4\pi d) - 20\log_{10}(\lambda) }
      = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f) + 32.45,
$$

with $d$ in km and $f$ in MHz. The last term $32.45$ dB is a constant derived from $c$.

#### 2.2 Atmospheric and molecular absorption loss

At mmWave/THz frequencies, the atmosphere introduces an additional **specific attenuation** $a_{\mathrm{atm}}$ (dB/km) that depends on frequency, temperature, pressure, and humidity. A widely used model (ITU‑R P.676‑12) provides $a_{\mathrm{atm}}(f)$. The total attenuation over distance $d$ (km) is

$$
L_{\mathrm{atm}}(d) = a_{\mathrm{atm}}(f)\, d \quad \text{[dB]}.
$$

For a 28 GHz link under standard sea‑level conditions, $a_{\mathrm{atm}}\approx0.02$ dB/km; at 300 GHz it can exceed 1 dB/km, and near 0.56 THz absorption peaks can reach > 10 dB/km.

#### 2.3 Link budget and Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio (SNR)

The **received noise power** $N$ (W) over bandwidth $B$ (Hz) is

$$
N = k\, T_{\mathrm{sys}}\, B,
$$

where  

* $k = 1.38\times10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}$ – Boltzmann constant,  
* $T_{\mathrm{sys}} = T_{0} (10^{\mathrm{NF}/10})$ – system noise temperature (K),  
* $T_{0}=290\,\text{K}$ – reference temperature,  
* $\mathrm{NF}$ – noise figure (dB).

The **SNR** (linear) is

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{rx}}}{N},
\qquad
\mathrm{SNR_{dB}} = P_{\mathrm{rx,dB}} - N_{\mathrm{dB}}.
$$

#### 2.4 Beamforming gain

A uniform planar array (UPA) with $N_{\mathrm{ant}}$ isotropic elements and ideal phase control yields a **maximum array gain**

$$
G_{\mathrm{array}} = N_{\mathrm{ant}} \quad \text{(linear)} \quad\text{or}\quad 10\log_{10}(N_{\mathrm{ant}})\ \text{dB}.
$$

In practice, element pattern, mutual coupling, and quantized phase shifters reduce the gain; a typical efficiency factor $\eta_{\mathrm{bf}}$ (0.7–0.9) is applied:

$$
G_{\mathrm{bf}} = \eta_{\mathrm{bf}}\, N_{\mathrm{ant}}.
$$

### 3. Worked example – 28 GHz urban microcell

* **Scenario:** A 28 GHz NR FR2 link from a base station (BS) to a user equipment (UE) at $d = 100$ m.  
* **Parameters:**  

| Symbol | Value | Unit |
|--------|-------|------|
| $P_{\mathrm{tx}}$ | 30 dBm | (10 mW) |
| $G_{\mathrm{tx}}$ | 20 dBi | (100) |
| $G_{\mathrm{rx}}$ | 10 dBi | (10) |
| $f$ | 28 GHz | (Hz) |
| $B$ | 1 GHz | (Hz) |
| $\mathrm{NF}$ | 7 dB | — |
| $a_{\mathrm{atm}}$ | 0.02 dB/km | — |

**Step 1 – FSPL**

$$
\mathrm{FSPL_{dB}} = 20\log_{10}(0.1) + 20\log_{10}(28\,000) + 32.45
                 \approx -20 + 89.0 + 32.45 = 101.45\ \text{dB}.
$$

**Step 2 – Atmospheric loss**

$$
L_{\mathrm{atm}} = 0.02\,\frac{\text{dB}}{\text{km}} \times 0.1\ \text{km}=0.002\ \text{dB}\ (\text{negligible}).
$$

**Step 3 – Received power**

Transmit power in dBm: $30$ dBm. Antenna gains: $20$ dBi + $10$ dBi = $30$ dB.

$$
P_{\mathrm{rx,dB}} = 30\ \text{dBm} + 30\ \text{dB} - 101.45\ \text{dB}
                  \approx -41.45\ \text{dBm}.
$$

Convert to watts: $P_{\mathrm{rx}} = 10^{(-41.45-30)/10}=7.1\times10^{-12}\ \text{W}$.

**Step 4 – Noise power**

System noise temperature:

$$
T_{\mathrm{sys}} = 290\ \text{K}\times 10^{7/10} \approx 290 \times 5.01 = 1{,}453\ \text{K}.
$$

Noise power:

$$
N = k T_{\mathrm{sys}} B = 1.38\times10^{-23}\times1{,}453\times10^{9}
  \approx 2.0\times10^{-12}\ \text{W} = -57\ \text{dBm}.
$$

**Step 5 – SNR**

$$
\mathrm{SNR_{dB}} = -41.45\ \text{dBm} - (-57\ \text{dBm}) = 15.55\ \text{dB}.
$$

With a 1 GHz bandwidth, an SNR of 15.5 dB comfortably supports a 64‑QAM modulation (≈ 5 bits/s/Hz) and yields a spectral efficiency of ≈ 4.5 bits/s/Hz after coding overhead, i.e., ≈ 4.5 Gb/s raw throughput.

### 4. Terahertz (THz) channel highlights

| Aspect | Typical values / models |
|--------|--------------------------|
| Frequency range | 0.1 – 10 THz |
| Wavelength | 30 µm – 3 mm |
| Available bandwidth | 10 – 100 GHz per carrier (depends on atmospheric windows) |
| Path loss (FSPL) | Same formula; at 1 THz, FSPL over 10 m ≈ 100 dB |
| Molecular absorption | Dominated by water vapor, oxygen; line‑by‑line absorption peaks described by HITRAN database |
| Antenna technology | Plasmonic graphene sheets, metasurfaces; typical array gains > 30 dBi for compact sizes |
| Noise floor | Thermal noise dominates; however, receiver noise figure can be > 10 dB due to limited low‑noise amplifiers |

Because absorption lines are **frequency‑selective**, system designers often choose **transparent windows** (e.g., 0.3‑0.35 THz, 0.55‑0.65 THz) for long‑range links, while broader windows (0.9‑1.1 THz) are reserved for short‑range indoor or chip‑to‑chip communication.

### 5. Common pitfalls and practical notes

| Pitfall | Why it hurts | Mitigation |
|---------|--------------|------------|
| **Neglecting atmospheric loss** at > 100 GHz | Underestimates path loss, leads to optimistic link budgets | Use ITU‑R P.676‑12 tables or the **ITU‑R M.2135‑1** model for mmWave/THz |
| **Treating antenna gain as isotropic** | Misses the huge beamforming advantage (or over‑estimates if mis‑aligned) | Model array gain explicitly; include pointing loss (typically 1‑3 dB) |
| **Mixing linear and dB units** | Errors propagate through logarithmic calculations | Keep a consistent unit system; convert once and stay linear or dB throughout |
| **Assuming Rayleigh fading** in LoS‑dominant mmWave | Rayleigh predicts deep fades that rarely occur; may over‑design diversity | Use Rician or Nakagami‑m models with high K‑factor for LoS links |
| **Ignoring hardware impairments** (phase‑shifter quantization, PA non‑linearity) | Real‑world SNR can be 3‑6 dB lower than theoretical | Include implementation loss margins (2‑5 dB) in the link budget |

Finally, **beam alignment latency** is a critical system‑level issue: narrow beams (≤ 5°) require fast tracking algorithms; otherwise, link outage dominates performance, especially for mobile users or UAV platforms.
