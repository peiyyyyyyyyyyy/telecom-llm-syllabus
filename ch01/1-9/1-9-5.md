# 1.9.5 無線資訊與能量同傳（SWIPT）

## 正文

In many emerging wireless systems—Internet‑of‑Things (IoT) sensor networks, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms, and future 6G deployments—devices are expected to operate for years without manual battery replacement.  Conventional approaches separate the radio‑frequency (RF) link for data transmission from a dedicated power‑transfer link, which wastes spectrum and increases hardware complexity.  **Simultaneous Wireless Information and Power Transfer (SWIPT)** addresses this by allowing a single RF waveform to carry both information bits and usable electrical energy to the receiver.  The concept is attractive because it exploits the same propagation medium for two purposes, potentially reducing deployment cost, enabling ultra‑dense sensor fields, and supporting new services such as “energy‑as‑a‑service”.

---

### 1. System Model and Key Definitions  

Consider a narrow‑band point‑to‑point link operating over a flat fading channel.  The transmitted baseband signal is $x(t)$ with average power constraint  

$$
\mathbb{E}\{|x(t)|^{2}\}=P_{\text{tx}}\quad \text{[W]}. 
$$

The complex channel gain is $h\in\mathbb{C}$, and the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) at the information decoder has power spectral density $N_{0}$ [W/Hz].  The received RF signal after the antenna is  

$$
y(t)=h\,x(t)+n(t),
$$

where $n(t)$ is zero‑mean complex Gaussian with variance $N_{0}B$ (bandwidth $B$ [Hz]).

A SWIPT receiver splits the incoming RF power into two branches:

* **Information decoding (ID) branch** – extracts the data.
* **Energy harvesting (EH) branch** – rectifies the RF signal to DC.

Two canonical architectures are:

| Architecture | Principle |
|--------------|-----------|
| **Power‑splitting (PS)** | A fraction $\rho\in[0,1]$ of the received power is sent to the EH circuit; the remaining $1-\rho$ goes to the ID circuit. |
| **Time‑switching (TS)** | The receiver alternates between pure ID mode (duration $\alpha T$) and pure EH mode (duration $(1-\alpha)T$) within each transmission block of length $T$. |

The **energy conversion efficiency** of the rectifier is denoted $\eta\in(0,1]$ (dimensionless).  The harvested DC power is therefore  

$$
P_{\text{harv}}=
\begin{cases}
\eta\,\rho\,|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}, & \text{(PS)}\\[4pt]
\eta\,(1-\alpha)\,|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}, & \text{(TS)} .
\end{cases}
\tag{1}
$$

The **signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR)** at the ID branch for PS is  

$$
\gamma_{\text{ID}}=\frac{(1-\rho)\,|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}}{N_{0}B},
\tag{2}
$$

and for TS (during the ID interval)  

$$
\gamma_{\text{ID}}=\frac{|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}}{N_{0}B}.
\tag{3}
$$

Assuming Gaussian signalling, the achievable information rate (bits/s) is  

$$
R = B\log_{2}\!\bigl(1+\gamma_{\text{ID}}\bigr).
\tag{4}
$$

The **rate–energy (R–E) region** is the set of all $(R, P_{\text{harv}})$ pairs achievable by varying $\rho$ (or $\alpha$) while satisfying the transmit power constraint.  This region characterises the fundamental trade‑off: increasing harvested power reduces the SNR and thus the data rate, and vice‑versa.

---

### 2. Fundamental Limits  

For a given channel realization $|h|^{2}$ and noise level, the Pareto‑optimal boundary of the R‑E region can be derived analytically for the PS architecture.  Substituting (2) into (4) and eliminating $\rho$ yields  

$$
P_{\text{harv}} = \eta\,|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}} - \eta\frac{N_{0}B}{\ln 2}\,2^{R/B},
\tag{5}
$$

valid for $0\le R \le B\log_{2}\!\bigl(1+|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}/N_{0}B\bigr)$.  Equation (5) shows that the harvested power decreases exponentially with the target rate, highlighting the importance of efficient waveform design and channel state information (CSI) at the transmitter.

When the rectifier exhibits **non‑linear behavior**, a more accurate model uses a polynomial or sigmoidal function $f(\cdot)$ of the input RF power $P_{\text{in}}$:

$$
P_{\text{harv}} = f\!\bigl(\rho |h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}\bigr),\qquad 
f(x)=\frac{a x^{m}}{1+b x^{n}},
\tag{6}
$$

where $a,b,m,n$ are hardware‑specific parameters obtained from calibration.  Non‑linear models predict that high‑peak‑to‑average‑power ratio (PAPR) waveforms (e.g., multisine) can improve harvested power without sacrificing rate.

### 3. Worked Example (Power‑Splitting)  

*Given*:  

* Transmit power $P_{\text{tx}} = 1~\text{W}$ (30 dBm).  
* Carrier frequency $f_{c}=2.4~\text{GHz}$, bandwidth $B = 2~\text{MHz}$.  
* Path‑loss exponent $\alpha_{\text{pl}} = 3$, distance $d = 5~\text{m}$.  
* Reference path loss $L_{0}= -30~\text{dB}$ at $d_{0}=1~\text{m}$.  
* Noise PSD $N_{0}= -174~\text{dBm/Hz}$ (thermal noise).  
* Energy conversion efficiency $\eta = 0.5$.  
* Desired harvested power $P_{\text{harv}} = 100~\text{mW}$.

**Step 1 – Compute channel power gain**  

$$
L(d)=L_{0}+10\alpha_{\text{pl}}\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{d_{0}}\right)
      = -30 + 10\cdot3\log_{10}(5)
      \approx -30 + 30\cdot0.699 = -9.3~\text{dB}.
$$

Thus $|h|^{2}=10^{L(d)/10}=10^{-0.93}\approx 0.117$ (dimensionless power gain).

**Step 2 – Determine required power‑splitting ratio**  

From (1) for PS:  

$$
\rho = \frac{P_{\text{harv}}}{\eta |h|^{2} P_{\text{tx}}}
      = \frac{0.1}{0.5 \times 0.117 \times 1}
      \approx 1.71.
$$

Since $\rho$ cannot exceed 1, the target $100~\text{mW}$ is unattainable with the given link budget.  Reduce the target to $P_{\text{harv}} = 50~\text{mW}$:

$$
\rho = \frac{0.05}{0.5 \times 0.117} \approx 0.85.
$$

**Step 3 – Compute resulting SNR and rate**  

Noise power $N = N_{0}B = 10^{-17.4}\,\text{W/Hz}\times2\times10^{6}\,\text{Hz}
          \approx 7.94\times10^{-12}\,\text{W}$.

SNR for ID (2):  

$$
\gamma_{\text{ID}} = \frac{(1-\rho)\,|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}}{N}
 = \frac{0.15 \times 0.117 \times 1}{7.94\times10^{-12}}
 \approx 2.2\times10^{9}\;(\text{≈ 93 dB}).
$$

The resulting rate (4):  

$$
R = 2\times10^{6}\,\log_{2}(1+2.2\times10^{9})
  \approx 2\times10^{6}\times 31.1 \approx 62.2~\text{Mbps}.
$$

Thus, with $\rho=0.85$, the receiver can harvest $50~\text{mW}$ while still supporting a multi‑Mbps link—demonstrating the feasibility of SWIPT in short‑range IoT scenarios.

### 4. Common Mistakes and Practical Notes  

| Pitfall | Why it Happens | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Assuming linear EH** | Many textbooks use $P_{\text{harv}}=\eta\rho|h|^{2}P_{\text{tx}}$, ignoring diode saturation. | Use calibrated non‑linear models (e.g., (6)) for high input power or high‑PAPR waveforms. |
| **Neglecting CSI at the transmitter** | Without CSI, the transmitter cannot adapt $\rho$ or beamforming, leading to sub‑optimal R‑E trade‑off. | Implement limited feedback or channel reciprocity; for massive MIMO, exploit statistical CSI. |
| **Violating regulatory EIRP limits** | Harvesting often encourages high transmit power, but spectrum regulations cap equivalent isotropically radiated power (EIRP). | Design SWIPT waveforms within the allowed EIRP; consider directional antennas or distributed power beacons. |
| **Overlooking hardware impairments** | Power splitters introduce insertion loss; rectifiers have impedance mismatch. | Account for splitter loss $L_{\text{sp}}$ (dB) by reducing effective $\rho$; match rectifier input impedance to antenna. |
| **Using the same waveform for both functions without optimisation** | A waveform optimal for data (e.g., low PAPR) may be sub‑optimal for energy. | Joint waveform optimisation (e.g., multisine + OFDM) can improve harvested power while preserving rate. |

Finally, SWIPT is not a universal solution; its benefit peaks in scenarios where the energy demand is modest relative to the RF link budget (short distances, line‑of‑sight, or dedicated power beacons).  System designers must weigh the added complexity of power splitters, rectifier design, and CSI acquisition against the savings in battery replacement and wiring.
