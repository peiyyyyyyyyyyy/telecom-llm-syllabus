### 1.9.6 AI in Wireless Networks – Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Reinforcement Learning, and Federated Learning  

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become a cornerstone of next‑generation (5G‑Advanced and 6G) wireless systems. By allowing the network to **learn from data**, AI techniques can adapt radio resource management, topology control, and security functions in ways that traditional rule‑based algorithms cannot. In practice, AI enables:

* **Real‑time traffic prediction** for proactive scheduling.  
* **Dynamic spectrum allocation** that respects interference constraints.  
* **Beamforming and user‑association decisions** that scale with massive MIMO and reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS).  
* **Energy‑aware operation** of UAV‑mounted base stations and satellite gateways.  

Below we introduce the four AI paradigms most frequently applied to telecom networks, present their mathematical foundations, and illustrate how they are used in a simple network‑control problem.

---

#### 1. Machine Learning (ML) – Supervised & Unsupervised  

Machine learning builds a statistical model $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ that maps an input feature vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ to a target $y$ (classification) or $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ (regression). The model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are obtained by minimizing a loss function over a training set $\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$.

* **Supervised loss (cross‑entropy for classification)**  
  $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \mathbf{1}\{y^{(i)}=c\}\,\log \hat{p}_c^{(i)}$$  
  where $\hat{p}_c^{(i)} = \frac{\exp(z_c^{(i)})}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}\exp(z_j^{(i)})}$ is the soft‑max output, $z_c^{(i)}$ the logit for class $c$, $C$ the number of classes, and $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ the indicator function.

* **Unsupervised loss (k‑means clustering)**  
  $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KM}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\min_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\|\mathbf{x}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|_2^2,$$  
  where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ is the centroid of cluster $k$ and $K$ the number of clusters.

In telecom, supervised learning is often used for **channel quality prediction** (features: UE speed, past SINR values; target: future CQI), while unsupervised learning can discover **traffic patterns** for load balancing.

---

#### 2. Deep Learning (DL)  

Deep learning is a subset of ML where $f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is a **deep neural network (DNN)** composed of $L$ layers. For a fully‑connected layer $l$,
$$\mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \sigma\!\big(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}\big),$$  
where $\mathbf{h}^{(0)}=\mathbf{x}$, $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_l\times n_{l-1}}$ the weight matrix, $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_l}$ the bias vector, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ a non‑linear activation (e.g., ReLU $ \sigma(z)=\max(0,z)$).  

Key advantages for wireless networks:

* **Feature extraction** from raw I/Q samples, removing the need for handcrafted channel models.  
* **End‑to‑end mapping** from measured pilot signals to optimal precoding vectors.  
* **Scalability**: convolutional or graph neural networks can handle large antenna arrays or network graphs.

Training follows the same loss minimization as generic ML, typically via stochastic gradient descent (SGD):
$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}),$$  
where $\eta$ is the learning rate.

---

#### 3. Reinforcement Learning (RL)  

RL treats the network as an **agent** interacting with an environment (the radio medium). At discrete time $t$, the agent observes a state $s_t$, selects an action $a_t$, receives a scalar reward $r_t$, and transitions to $s_{t+1}$. The goal is to maximize the expected discounted return
$$G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k} r_{t+k},$$  
with discount factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$.  

The optimal action‑value function satisfies the **Bellman optimality equation**:
$$Q^{*}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^{*}(s',a') \mid s,a \right].$$  

A popular model‑free algorithm is **Q‑learning**, which updates a table or a DNN approximator $Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})$:
$$\boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \alpha \,\big( r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a';\boldsymbol{\theta}) - Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta}),$$  
where $\alpha$ is the learning rate.

In wireless systems RL is used for **dynamic power control**, **hand‑over decision**, and **spectrum sharing**, where the reward may combine throughput, latency, and energy consumption.

---

#### 4. Federated Learning (FL)  

Federated learning enables many distributed devices (e.g., user equipments, edge nodes) to collaboratively train a global model **without sharing raw data**, thus preserving privacy and reducing backhaul load. The canonical procedure (Federated Averaging, FedAvg) proceeds in rounds $t=0,1,\dots$:

1. **Server broadcasts** the current global parameters $\mathbf{w}^{t}$ to a subset $\mathcal{S}^{t}$ of $K$ clients.  
2. **Client $k$** performs $E$ local SGD steps on its private dataset of size $n_k$, obtaining $\mathbf{w}^{t+1}_k$.  
3. **Server aggregates**  
   $$\mathbf{w}^{t+1}= \sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_k}{n}\,\mathbf{w}^{t+1}_k,\qquad n=\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_k,$$  
   producing the new global model.

The aggregation weight $\frac{n_k}{n}$ reflects the relative data volume of each client (unitless). FL is attractive for **distributed CSI learning**, **UE‑side traffic prediction**, and **privacy‑preserving anomaly detection**.

---

### Worked Example: Q‑Learning for Distributed Power Control  

Consider a simple two‑cell downlink scenario. Each base station (BS) can transmit at either **low** power $P_{\text{L}} = 10$ dBm or **high** power $P_{\text{H}} = 20$ dBm. The state $s$ is the pair of received SINR levels $(\text{SINR}_1,\text{SINR}_2)$ quantized as **good (G)** if $\ge 10$ dB, otherwise **bad (B)**. The action $a$ is the power choice of the acting BS. Reward is defined as
$$r = \begin{cases}
+1 & \text{if both users are G after the action}\\
-0.5 & \text{if any user is B}\\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}.$$

Assume discount factor $\gamma=0.9$ and learning rate $\alpha=0.5$. The Q‑table is initialized to zero. In one iteration, BS 1 observes state $s = (B,B)$, selects $a = P_{\text{H}}$, observes next state $s' = (G,B)$ and receives reward $r=-0.5$.

Update for the entry $Q(s,a)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Target} &= r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') = -0.5 + 0.9 \times 0 = -0.5,\\
Q_{\text{new}}(s,a) &= Q_{\text{old}}(s,a) + \alpha\big(\text{Target} - Q_{\text{old}}(s,a)\big)\\
&= 0 + 0.5 \times (-0.5 - 0) = -0.25.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the Q‑value for taking high power in the bad‑bad state becomes $-0.25$, discouraging that action in future decisions.

### Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls  

| Issue | Why it Happens | Remedy |
|------|----------------|--------|
| **Over‑fitting to a specific traffic trace** | Training data may not capture the full variability of user mobility, weather, or interference. | Use regularization, cross‑validation, and augment data with synthetic channel realizations. |
| **Non‑i.i.d. data across devices in FL** | UE traffic patterns are highly heterogeneous, breaking the assumption of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples. | Apply **FedProx** or adaptive weighting schemes; increase the number of participating clients per round. |
| **Excessive inference latency** | Deep models (e.g., >10 M parameters) can exceed the sub‑millisecond processing budget of URLLC. | Deploy model compression (pruning, quantization) or edge‑accelerators; consider shallow networks for latency‑critical loops. |
| **Reward design in RL** | A simplistic reward (e.g., throughput only) may lead to unfair resource allocation or instability. | Combine multiple KPIs (throughput, latency, power) with appropriate scaling; test reward shaping in a simulator before over‑the‑air trials. |
| **Stale global model in FL** | Communication delays cause clients to train on outdated parameters, degrading convergence. | Use asynchronous FL or limit the number of local epochs $E$ to keep drift small. |

By respecting these considerations, AI‑driven techniques can be safely integrated into real‑world telecom infrastructures, paving the way for the adaptive, high‑capacity networks envisioned for 6G.
