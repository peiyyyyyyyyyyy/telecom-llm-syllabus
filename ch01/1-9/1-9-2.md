## 正文

Modern wireless systems are no longer confined to terrestrial base stations. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and satellite constellations constitute the two main pillars of *non‑terrestrial networks* (NTNs). They extend coverage to remote or disaster‑struck areas, provide backhaul for dense urban deployments, and enable global‑scale services such as positioning, broadband Internet, and Internet of Things (IoT) connectivity. In 5G Release 17 and the forthcoming 6G vision, NTNs are explicitly integrated into the radio access network (RAN) architecture, and their performance is evaluated with the same rigor as terrestrial links.

---

### 1. Key Definitions

| Symbol | Definition | Unit |
|--------|------------|------|
| $h$ | UAV or satellite altitude above sea level | m |
| $d$ | 3‑D slant range between transmitter and receiver | m |
| $r$ | Horizontal distance (ground projection) | m |
| $f_c$ | Carrier frequency | Hz |
| $c$ | Speed of light ($3\times10^8$) | m/s |
| $G_t$, $G_r$ | Antenna gains of transmitter and receiver | dimensionless (linear) |
| $P_t$ | Transmit power (effective isotropic radiated power, EIRP) | W |
| $L_{\text{FS}}$ | Free‑space path loss factor (linear) | dimensionless |
| $L_{\text{atm}}$ | Atmospheric absorption loss (linear) | dimensionless |
| $N_0$ | Thermal noise power spectral density | $-174$ dBm/Hz |
| $B$ | System bandwidth | Hz |
| $\gamma$ | Received signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) | linear (or dB) |
| $v$ | Relative velocity (e.g., satellite speed) | m/s |
| $f_D$ | Doppler shift | Hz |
| $K$ | Rician $K$‑factor (ratio of LOS to scattered power) | dimensionless |

#### 1.1 Geometry of an NTN Link  

For a UAV or satellite at altitude $h$ and a ground terminal located at horizontal distance $r$, the slant range is  

$$
d = \sqrt{h^{2}+r^{2}} .
$$

The elevation angle $\theta$ is  

$$
\theta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{h}{r}\right) .
$$

Higher $\theta$ reduces atmospheric attenuation and improves the probability of line‑of‑sight (LOS).

#### 1.2 Free‑Space Path Loss (FSPL)

In the absence of obstruction, the FSPL (linear) is  

$$
L_{\text{FS}} = \left(\frac{4\pi d f_c}{c}\right)^{2}.
$$

In dB form:

$$
L_{\text{FS}}[\text{dB}] = 20\log_{10}(d) + 20\log_{10}(f_c) + 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{c}\right).
$$

#### 1.3 Received Power and SNR  

The received power $P_r$ (W) is  

$$
P_r = \frac{P_t G_t G_r}{L_{\text{FS}} L_{\text{atm}}}.
$$

Thermal noise power $N$ over bandwidth $B$ is  

$$
N = N_0 B.
$$

Hence the SNR is  

$$
\gamma = \frac{P_r}{N}.
$$

#### 1.4 Doppler Shift  

For a satellite moving at speed $v$ relative to the ground terminal, the Doppler shift is  

$$
f_D = \frac{v}{c} f_c \cos\phi,
$$

where $\phi$ is the angle between the velocity vector and the line‑of‑sight. High $f_D$ challenges carrier synchronization and channel estimation.

#### 1.5 Rician Fading for UAV Links  

UAV‑ground links often experience a dominant LOS component plus diffuse multipath. The envelope $R$ follows a Rician distribution with PDF  

$$
p_R(r) = \frac{r}{\sigma^{2}} \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{r^{2}+s^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Bigr) I_0\!\Bigl(\frac{r s}{\sigma^{2}}\Bigr),
$$

where $s^{2}=K\sigma^{2}$, $K$ is the Rician factor, $\sigma^{2}$ the scattered power, and $I_0$ the modified Bessel function of the first kind.

### 2. Worked Example: LEO Satellite Downlink at Ku‑band  

Assume a Low‑Earth‑Orbit (LEO) satellite at altitude $h=600\,$km serving a ground terminal directly underneath ($r=0$). Parameters:

* Carrier frequency $f_c = 12\,$GHz (Ku‑band).
* Transmit EIRP $P_t = 30\,$dBm (1 W) with $G_t = 30\,$dBi (linear $G_t=10^{3}$).
* Ground antenna gain $G_r = 20\,$dBi (linear $G_r=10^{2}$).
* Atmospheric loss $L_{\text{atm}} = 2\,$dB (linear $=1.58$).
* Bandwidth $B = 20\,$MHz.
* Thermal noise density $N_0 = -174\,$dBm/Hz.

**Step 1 – Slant range**  
Directly overhead, $d = h = 600\,000\,$m.

**Step 2 – FSPL (dB)**  

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{FS}}[\text{dB}] &= 20\log_{10}(600\,000) + 20\log_{10}(12\times10^{9}) \\
&\quad + 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{3\times10^{8}}\right) \\
&\approx 115.56 + 182.44 - 147.55 \\
&= 150.45\ \text{dB}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3 – Received power (dBm)**  

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_r[\text{dBm}] &= P_t[\text{dBm}] + G_t[\text{dBi}] + G_r[\text{dBi}] \\
&\quad - L_{\text{FS}}[\text{dB}] - L_{\text{atm}}[\text{dB}] \\
&= 30 + 30 + 20 - 150.45 - 2 \\
&= -72.45\ \text{dBm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 4 – Noise power (dBm)**  

$$
N[\text{dBm}] = N_0[\text{dBm/Hz}] + 10\log_{10}(B) = -174 + 10\log_{10}(20\times10^{6}) \approx -101\ \text{dBm}.
$$

**Step 5 – SNR (dB)**  

$$
\gamma[\text{dB}] = P_r - N = -72.45 - (-101) = 28.55\ \text{dB}.
$$

The link comfortably exceeds typical modulation‑coding scheme (MCS) thresholds (e.g., 16‑QAM requires ≈12 dB), confirming that a 20 MHz Ku‑band LEO downlink can deliver multi‑Gbps rates with modest antenna gains.

### 3. Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it Happens | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Neglecting atmospheric loss at high frequencies** | At Ka‑/Ku‑band, rain attenuation can add 5–20 dB. | Use ITU‑R P.618 rain loss models; include a margin (e.g., 3 dB) in link budget. |
| **Assuming constant elevation angle** | UAVs move rapidly; elevation changes affect $L_{\text{atm}}$ and $K$‑factor. | Dynamically adapt transmit power or beam steering based on real‑time $\theta$. |
| **Over‑looking Doppler in LEO** | $v\approx7.5$ km/s yields $f_D\approx 300$ kHz at 12 GHz, causing inter‑carrier interference. | Implement robust carrier recovery (e.g., pilot‑aided PLL) and adaptive sub‑carrier spacing in OFDM. |
| **Using terrestrial path‑loss models for aerial links** | Ground models include clutter loss that is absent for high‑elevation LOS. | Apply the FSPL plus elevation‑dependent excess loss (e.g., 0–2 dB for $θ>30°$). |
| **Ignoring regulatory EIRP limits** | Satellite bands have strict EIRP caps to avoid interference. | Verify compliance with ITU‑R and regional regulations before system design. |

Finally, the integration of UAVs and satellites into 5G/6G hinges on *flexible waveform design*, *beamforming* (massive MIMO on the space segment), and *network slicing* to allocate resources across heterogeneous links. Understanding the fundamental link‑budget equations and the unique propagation phenomena (e.g., Doppler, Rician fading) is essential for any telecom engineer working on NTNs.
