## 正文

In any carrier‑grade telecommunications network, the ability to detect, isolate, and resolve faults quickly determines service quality, revenue protection, and regulatory compliance. The *fault management process* (sometimes called *障礙管理* in Mandarin) is the structured workflow that transforms a raw alarm or customer complaint into a documented, closed incident. A well‑engineered process reduces mean time to repair (MTTR), improves network availability, and provides traceable evidence for performance reporting and root‑cause analysis.

---

### 1. Core Stages of the Fault Management Process

| Stage | Primary Objective | Typical Activities | Key Output |
|-------|-------------------|--------------------|------------|
| **1. Intake (受理)** | Capture the fault report and create a work ticket. | • Receive alarms from NMS/OSS, or customer calls.<br>• Validate the report (duplicate check, severity classification).<br>• Register a *Fault Ticket* (FT) with a unique identifier FT‑XXXX. | Fault Ticket with initial data (time, source, severity). |
| **2. Assignment (派工)** | Route the ticket to the appropriate technical group. | • Determine responsible domain (e.g., access, transmission, core).<br>• Apply *assignment rules* (skill matrix, load balancing).<br>• Notify field engineers or NOC staff. | Updated FT showing assignee, expected response time (SLA). |
| **3. Restoration (修復)** | Diagnose and correct the fault to restore service. | • Perform remote diagnostics (CLI, SNMP, NetConf).<br>• If necessary, dispatch field crew for on‑site repair.<br>• Document actions, parts used, and timestamps. | Restoration time $t_{\text{repair}}$, status “Restored”. |
| **4. Closure (結案)** | Verify that service is fully functional and archive the record. | • Conduct post‑repair verification (QoS test, customer confirmation).<br>• Update ticket with final remarks and root‑cause classification.<br>• Archive for KPI calculation. | Closed FT, ready for reporting. |

---

### 2. Quantitative Metrics

Fault management is not only procedural; it is measured by well‑defined performance indicators.

1. **Mean Time to Repair (MTTR)**  
   $$\text{MTTR} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} t_{\text{repair},i}}{N}$$  
   where $t_{\text{repair},i}$ is the elapsed time from *acceptance* to *restoration* for incident $i$, and $N$ is the number of incidents in the observation window. Unit: **hours** (or minutes).

2. **Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF)** – network‑wide reliability metric:  
   $$\text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total operational time}}{\text{Number of failures}}$$  
   Unit: **hours**.

3. **Availability (A)** – the proportion of time the service is operational:  
   $$A = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}}$$  
   Often expressed as a percentage; carrier targets are typically $>99.9\%$ (three‑nines).

4. **Service Level Agreement (SLA) Compliance** – ratio of incidents resolved within the agreed response/resolution time.

---

### 3. Worked Example

A regional mobile operator monitors a 4G LTE eNodeB cluster. Over a 30‑day period:

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Number of faults (N) | 8 |
| Total repair time (Σ $t_{\text{repair}}$) | 24 hours |
| Total operational time of the cluster | 720 hours (30 days × 24 h) |
| Number of failures (same as N) | 8 |

1. **Calculate MTTR**  
   $$\text{MTTR} = \frac{24\ \text{h}}{8} = 3\ \text{h}$$

2. **Calculate MTBF**  
   $$\text{MTBF} = \frac{720\ \text{h}}{8} = 90\ \text{h}$$

3. **Determine Availability**  
   $$A = \frac{90}{90 + 3} = \frac{90}{93} \approx 0.9677 \; (\text{or } 96.77\%)$$  

   The operator’s availability is below the typical 99.9 % target, indicating that either the MTBF must be increased (e.g., by hardware upgrades) or the MTTR reduced (e.g., by better remote diagnostics).

4. **SLA Check**  
   Suppose the SLA requires restoration within 4 h for “high‑severity” faults. All 8 incidents were resolved in ≤ 3 h, so SLA compliance = 100 %.

### 4. Interaction with Supporting Systems

| System | Role in Fault Management |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Network Management System (NMS)** | Generates alarms, supplies topology for root‑cause analysis. |
| **Operations Support System (OSS) / Ticketing** | Stores Fault Tickets, enforces workflow rules, records SLA timestamps. |
| **Geographic Information System (GIS)** | Provides location data for field crew dispatch. |
| **Configuration Management Database (CMDB)** | Links assets to responsible teams and maintenance windows. |

Integration via standard interfaces (e.g., ITU‑T X.736 alarm format, RESTful OSS APIs) ensures that the intake stage is automated, reducing manual entry errors.

---

### 5. Common Mistakes & Practical Notes

| Mistake | Impact | Mitigation |
|---------|--------|------------|
| **Skipping severity validation** | Low‑priority alarms may consume resources, high‑priority faults may be delayed. | Implement automated severity scoring (e.g., based on affected services, customer impact). |
| **Manual ticket creation** | Inconsistent data, longer intake time, higher MTTR. | Use alarm‑to‑ticket auto‑correlation; enforce mandatory fields. |
| **Poor assignment rules** | Overloading a single crew, increased travel time. | Maintain a *skill‑matrix* and geographic load‑balancing algorithm. |
| **Insufficient documentation during restoration** | Hinders root‑cause analysis, repeats errors. | Require engineers to fill a structured “Action Log” before moving to closure. |
| **Closing tickets without verification** | “Ghost” faults remain, customers experience recurring outages. | Enforce a post‑repair verification step (automated test or customer acknowledgment). |

**Practical tip:** Maintain a *fault‑trend dashboard* that visualizes MTTR, MTBF, and SLA compliance per domain (access, transport, core). Rapidly spotting an upward MTTR trend prompts a review of diagnostic tools or spare‑part inventory, preventing SLA breaches.

---

In summary, the fault management process is the backbone of network reliability operations. By rigorously applying the four stages—Intake, Assignment, Restoration, Closure—and continuously measuring MTTR, MTBF, and availability, telecom operators can meet stringent service commitments and sustain a competitive edge.
