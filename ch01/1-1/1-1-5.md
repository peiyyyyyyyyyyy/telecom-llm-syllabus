# 1.1.5 4G 核心網（EPC）

所屬：[1.1 網路架構](../1-1.md)

## 正文

The **Evolved Packet Core (EPC)** is the packet‑switched backbone of a 4G LTE system. It interconnects the radio access network (E‑UTRAN) with external packet data networks (PDNs) such as the Internet or corporate networks, and it provides the signalling functions that control mobility, session management and security. Because all user traffic in LTE is carried as IP packets, the EPC’s design – a small set of logically distinct nodes with well‑defined interfaces – determines the scalability, latency and reliability of the whole mobile broadband service.

---

### Logical Nodes and Their Core Functions  

| Logical node | Primary role (as defined in 3GPP TS 23.401) | Key functions (selected) |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **MME** (Mobility Management Entity) | NAS signalling control plane | NAS signalling, NAS security, inter‑CN signalling for mobility (S3), UE reachability in ECM‑IDLE, Tracking Area list management, PDN‑GW and Serving‑GW selection, roaming (S6a), authentication, authorization, bearer management, lawful interception, warning message transfer, support for CIoT EPS optimisation (user‑data transport, header compression, etc.)【TS 23.401 §4.4.2】 |
| **SGW** (Serving Gateway) | Terminates the user‑plane toward E‑UTRAN | Terminates user‑plane interface towards E‑UTRAN, acts as local mobility anchor for inter‑eNodeB handover, forwards “end marker” during handover, buffers downlink packets in ECM‑IDLE, lawful interception, packet routing/forwarding, transport‑level packet marking (DiffServ/ARP), accounting for inter‑operator charging【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.2】 |
| **PGW** (PDN Gateway) | Terminates the user‑plane toward the PDN | Terminates the SGi interface, allocates UE IP address, per‑user packet filtering (e.g. DPI), lawful interception, transport‑level packet marking, accounting for inter‑operator charging, UL/DL service‑level charging and gating (via PCRF/OCS), rate enforcement (APN‑AMBR, GBR), DHCPv4/v6, optional Non‑IP data transfer, “end marker” signalling on SGW change【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.3】 |
| **HSS** (Home Subscriber Server) – not described in detail in the excerpt but referenced for roaming (S6a) | Central subscriber data repository | Provides authentication, subscription data, and roaming support (accessed via S6a)【TS 23.401 §4.4.2】 |

> **Note:** The MME and SGW may be implemented in the same physical node or in separate nodes; the same statement applies to the PGW and SGW【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.1】.  

---

### Non‑roaming Architecture and Interface Highlights  

In a typical non‑roaming deployment (Figure 4.2.1‑1 in the specification), a UE connects to an eNodeB, which forwards user‑plane packets to the **Serving GW (S‑GW)** over the S1‑U interface and control messages to the **MME** over S1‑AP. The S‑GW forwards user data to the **PDN GW (P‑GW)** via the **S5/S8** interface. When the SGW and PGW are not collocated, the **S5** interface is used between them, as explicitly noted in the specification【TS 23.401 §4.2.1】.

---

### Quantitative Relationships  

Because the EPC enforces a strict one‑to‑one mapping between a UE and its serving gateway, the number of SGWs per UE is a constant:

$$
N_{\text{SGW}} = 1 \qquad \text{(for every UE at a given time)} \tag{1}
$$

This follows directly from the statement “For each UE associated with the EPS, at a given point of time, there is a single Serving GW”【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.2】.

A UE may simultaneously attach to several PDNs (e.g., Internet and corporate APN). In that case, the number of PGWs can increase, but each PDN is served by at most one PGW per UE:

$$
1 \le N_{\text{PGW}} \le N_{\text{PDN}} \tag{2}
$$

where $N_{\text{PDN}}$ is the number of distinct PDN connections the UE has. The upper bound is implied by “If a UE is accessing multiple PDNs, there may be more than one PDN GW for that UE”【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.3】.

---

### Worked Example  

**Scenario:** UE A attaches to the network and requests two PDN connections: an “Internet” APN and a “Corporate” APN.  

1. **MME selection** – The MME authenticates the UE (authentication function) and selects a Serving GW (SGW) and, for each APN, a PDN GW (PGW)【TS 23.401 §4.4.2】.  
2. **SGW assignment** – Because of (1), UE A has exactly one SGW: $N_{\text{SGW}} = 1$.  
3. **PGW assignment** – Two APNs ⇒ two PGWs may be allocated: $N_{\text{PGW}} = 2$.  
4. **IP address allocation** – Each PGW allocates an IP address for its PDN (PGW function)【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.3】.  
5. **User‑plane flow** – User data from the eNodeB traverses S1‑U → SGW → S5 → PGW → Internet/Corporate network.  

If UE A later releases the corporate APN, the corresponding PGW is released, leaving $N_{\text{PGW}} = 1$. The SGW remains unchanged throughout the session, illustrating the constant‑one‑SGW rule.

---

### Common Mistakes and Practical Notes  

* **Assuming multiple SGWs per UE.** The EPC specification enforces a single SGW per UE at any moment; deploying more than one leads to signalling conflicts and violates the standard【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.2】.  
* **Confusing S5 with S6a.** S5 is the user‑plane interface between SGW and PGW (or between non‑collocated SGW/PGW)【TS 23.401 §4.2.1】, whereas S6a is used by the MME to communicate with the HSS for roaming and authentication【TS 23.401 §4.4.2】.  
* **Neglecting the “end marker” procedure.** During inter‑eNodeB handover, the SGW must send an “end marker” to the source eNodeB to assist re‑ordering; omitting this can cause packet loss or duplication【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.2】.  
* **Collocation confusion.** Both the MME‑SGW pair and the SGW‑PGW pair may be implemented as a single physical node, but this is optional; network designers must verify the chosen deployment matches the intended architecture【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.1】.  
* **Assuming the PGW performs NAS signalling.** NAS signalling is the exclusive domain of the MME; the PGW handles user‑plane functions such as IP address allocation and packet filtering【TS 23.401 §4.4.2】 vs. 【TS 23.401 §4.4.3.3】.  

Understanding these roles and constraints is essential for designing robust LTE networks, troubleshooting mobility failures, and planning upgrades to 5G where the EPC concepts evolve into the Service‑Based Architecture (5GC).
