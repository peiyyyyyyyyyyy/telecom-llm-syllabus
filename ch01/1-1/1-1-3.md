# 1.1.3 RAN 架構演進：CU／DU／RU 分離

所屬：[1.1 網路架構](../1-1.md)

## 正文

In legacy LTE the radio access network (RAN) was built around a monolithic eNodeB that combined all base‑station functions—radio processing, lower‑layer protocol stacks, and higher‑layer control—into a single piece of hardware. 5G NR introduced a *functional split* that separates the RAN into three logical blocks:

* **O‑RU (Radio Unit)** – the hardware that contains the antenna array, power amplifiers, low‑noise amplifiers, and the RF front‑end.  It performs *digitisation* (A/D, D/A) and *RF* up‑/down‑conversion.  
* **O‑DU (Distributed Unit)** – implements the lower PHY, MAC, and part of the RLC layer.  It is responsible for real‑time radio‑resource scheduling, HARQ processing, and the transport of user‑plane data to/from the RU.  
* **O‑CU (Central Unit)** – hosts the higher layers (PDCP, SDAP, RRC) and the control‑plane functions that are latency‑insensitive, such as mobility management and security.

This three‑level decomposition is often called **CU‑DU‑RU split**.  It matters because it lets operators **scale** each function independently, **centralise** control for network slicing, and **optimise transport** (midhaul) resources.  By moving the DU closer to the RU (e.g., at a macro site) the strict latency budget of the radio link is respected, while the CU can be pooled in a data centre to serve many cells.

---

### 1.1.3.1 Functional Split Options

3GPP TS 38.401 defines several split options (0–7).  The most widely deployed in 5G is **Option 2** (also called *midhaul*), where the split occurs **between the high‑PHY and low‑PHY**.  In this case:

* The RU handles RF and **high‑PHY** (FFT/IFFT, CP insertion/removal).
* The DU handles **low‑PHY**, MAC, and above.

A more extreme split, **Option 7.x**, pushes the split further down to the *resource element* level, requiring a *C‑PRI* (Common Public Radio Interface)‑style fronthaul with far higher bandwidth but tighter latency.

---

### 1.1.3.2 Bandwidth and Latency Formulas

For a given split, the required **fronthaul data rate** $B$ (in bit s$^{-1}$) can be approximated by  

$$
B = \frac{2 \, N_{\text{ant}} \, N_{\text{SC}} \, n_{\text{bits}} \, f_{\text{s}}}{\eta}
\tag{1}
$$

where  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|----------|------|
| $N_{\text{ant}}$ | Number of transmit/receive antenna elements (e.g., 64) | – |
| $N_{\text{SC}}$ | Number of active sub‑carriers per OFDM symbol (e.g., 1200 for 20 MHz) | – |
| $n_{\text{bits}}$ | Quantisation bits per I/Q sample (e.g., 15) | bits |
| $f_{\text{s}}$ | Sample rate of the baseband signal (e.g., 30.72 Msps for 20 MHz) | samples s$^{-1}$ |
| $\eta$ | Compression factor (≥ 1).  $\eta = 1$ for uncompressed C‑PRI; typical values 3–5 for functional‑split compression. | – |

The factor “2” accounts for separate I and Q streams.

The **one‑way latency budget** $L_{\text{max}}$ for a split is defined by the protocol timing.  For Option 2 the 3GPP specification requires  

$$
L_{\text{max}} \le 250~\mu\text{s}
\tag{2}
$$

Any additional transport delay (propagation, queuing, processing) must stay within this bound.

---

### 1.1.3.3 Worked Example

*Scenario*: An operator deploys a massive‑MIMO gNB with 64 antennas, 20 MHz carrier, 15‑bit I/Q quantisation, and chooses Option 2 with a compression factor $\eta = 4$.

1. **Calculate the sample rate**  
   For 20 MHz NR, $f_{\text{s}} = 30.72$ Msps (standard NR numerology 0).

2. **Active sub‑carriers**  
   $N_{\text{SC}} = 1200$ (standard for 20 MHz).

3. **Insert values into (1)**  

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \frac{2 \times 64 \times 1200 \times 15 \times 30.72\times10^{6}}{4} \\
  &= \frac{2 \times 64 \times 1200 \times 15 \times 30.72}{4}\,\text{Mbps} \\
  &= \frac{2 \times 64 \times 1200 \times 15 \times 30.72}{4}\times10^{6} \\
  &= \frac{2 \times 64 \times 1200 \times 15 \times 30.72}{4}\times10^{6}\\
  &= 1.77 \times 10^{9}\ \text{bit s}^{-1} \\
  &= 1.77\ \text{Gbps}
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the midhaul link must sustain **≈ 1.8 Gbps** per sector.

4. **Check latency**  
   Assuming a 40 km fibre link (propagation ≈ 200 µs) and a 30 µs switch processing delay, the total one‑way latency is 230 µs, which satisfies (2).

### 1.1.3.4 Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | Remedy |
|---------|---------------|--------|
| **Under‑estimating fronthaul bandwidth** – using the compressed rate of the RU‑DU split but forgetting the factor 2 for I/Q. | Mis‑reading Eq. (1). | Always double the baseband stream before applying compression. |
| **Violating latency budget** – placing the DU too far from the RU or adding too many intermediate switches. | Latency adds linearly with each hop. | Keep the DU within ~10 km of the RU for Option 2; use deterministic Ethernet (e.g., TSN) to guarantee < 250 µs. |
| **Mismatched clock distribution** – the RU and DU must share a common reference (e.g., 10 MHz) for coherent beamforming. | Independent oscillators introduce phase drift. | Deploy a precision timing protocol (PTP IEEE 1588v2) or Synchronous Ethernet to distribute the clock. |
| **Incorrect split selection for small cells** – using Option 2 for low‑capacity small cells leads to over‑provisioned transport. | Small cells often have few antennas; a higher‑layer split (Option 7.2) may be sufficient. | Evaluate antenna count and required throughput; choose the split that minimises cost while meeting latency. |

Finally, remember that **virtualisation** of the CU and DU (e.g., O‑CU in a cloud‑native core) introduces additional processing latency.  Operators should benchmark the end‑to‑end stack, not just the raw transport, before finalising the split.
