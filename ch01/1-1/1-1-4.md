## 正文

In a mobile system the *transport network* carries user‑plane and control‑plane traffic between the radio access components (RU, DU, CU) and the core network (CN). It therefore determines the end‑to‑end latency, the achievable data rates, and the overall reliability of the service. Modern 4G/5G deployments separate the transport into three logical segments:

| Segment | Typical endpoints | Typical distance | Typical technology |
|---------|-------------------|------------------|--------------------|
| **Front‑haul** | Radio Unit (RU) ↔ Distributed Unit (DU) | < 2 km (often < 500 m) | Fiber (e.g., 10 GBASE‑LR) or short‑range microwave (mmWave) |
| **Mid‑haul** | DU ↔ Central Unit (CU) or aggregation point | 2 km – 50 km | Fiber (single‑mode, 40 Gbps/100 Gbps) or microwave (e.g., 18 GHz, 28 GHz) |
| **Back‑haul** | CU ↔ Core Network (EPC/5GC) | 10 km – hundreds of km | High‑capacity fiber (DWDM) or long‑haul microwave (e.g., 6 GHz, 12 GHz) |

Understanding the physical layer of each segment is essential for network planning, capacity budgeting, and meeting latency targets (e.g., < 1 ms for URLLC over front‑haul).

---

### 1. Optical Fiber

#### 1.1 Basic parameters
* **Wavelength (λ)** – typically 1310 nm (O‑band) or 1550 nm (C‑band).  
* **Attenuation (α)** – fiber loss expressed in dB/km. Typical values: 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm, 0.20 dB/km at 1550 nm.  
* **Dispersion (D)** – ps/(nm·km), influences maximum symbol rate.  

#### 1.2 Link budget
The received optical power $P_{r}$ (dBm) is given by:

$$
P_{r}=P_{t}-\alpha \cdot L - \text{ConnectorLoss} - \text{SpliceLoss}
$$

where  
* $P_{t}$ – transmitted power (dBm),  
* $L$ – fiber length (km),  
* $\alpha$ – attenuation (dB/km),  
* ConnectorLoss, SpliceLoss – typically 0.5 dB each.

A safety margin (often 3–6 dB) is added to guarantee performance under aging.

#### 1.3 Capacity
Shannon’s theorem provides an upper bound on the achievable data rate $C$ (bits/s) on a noise‑limited channel:

$$
C = B \log_{2}\!\left(1+\frac{S}{N}\right)
$$

* $B$ – electrical bandwidth (Hz),  
* $S/N$ – signal‑to‑noise ratio (linear).  

In practice, Ethernet or OTN line codes introduce overhead. For a 10 GbE link using 64b/66b encoding, the *net* payload is:

$$
C_{\text{net}} = C_{\text{line}} \times \frac{64}{66}
$$

---

### 2. Microwave (Radio‑frequency) Links

#### 2.1 Basic parameters
* **Frequency (f)** – 6 GHz, 12 GHz, 18 GHz, 28 GHz, 60 GHz (mmWave) are common.  
* **Free‑space path loss (FSPL)** – expressed in dB:

$$
\text{FSPL}=20\log_{10}(d)+20\log_{10}(f)+92.45
$$

where $d$ is distance (km) and $f$ is frequency (GHz).  

* **Antenna gain (G)** – usually 30–45 dBi for parabolic dishes; higher at mmWave.  

#### 2.2 Rain attenuation
Rain is the dominant impairment for frequencies > 10 GHz. ITU‑R P.838 gives specific attenuation $γ_R$ (dB/km):

$$
γ_R = k R^{α}
$$

* $R$ – rain rate (mm/h),  
* $k, α$ – frequency‑dependent coefficients (look‑up table).  

Total rain loss $L_R = γ_R \cdot d$.

#### 2.3 Link budget
The received microwave power $P_{r}$ (dBm) is:

$$
P_{r}=P_{t}+G_{t}+G_{r}-\text{FSPL}-L_{R}-\text{OtherLosses}
$$

A fade margin of 10–20 dB is typical for reliability.

### 3. Worked Example – Designing a Front‑haul Fiber Link

**Scenario**: A 5G gNB with a 200 Mbps peak user traffic must connect an RU to a DU over 800 m of single‑mode fiber (λ = 1550 nm). The transceiver can emit $P_{t}=0$ dBm. Connector loss = 0.5 dB per pair, splice loss = 0.2 dB per splice (assume two splices). The required minimum received power for the chosen 10 GbE transceiver is $-12$ dBm.

**Step 1 – Compute total loss**

Attenuation: $\alpha = 0.20$ dB/km → over 0.8 km, loss = $0.20 \times 0.8 = 0.16$ dB.  

Connector loss = $0.5$ dB (Tx) + $0.5$ dB (Rx) = $1.0$ dB.  

Splice loss = $2 \times 0.2 = 0.4$ dB.

Total loss $L_{\text{total}} = 0.16 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 1.56$ dB.

**Step 2 – Received power**

$$
P_{r}=P_{t}-L_{\text{total}} = 0\ \text{dBm} - 1.56\ \text{dB} = -1.56\ \text{dBm}
$$

**Step 3 – Margin check**

Required $P_{\text{min}} = -12$ dBm, so margin = $-1.56 - (-12) = 10.44$ dB, well above the typical 3 dB safety margin. The link is feasible without amplification.

**Step 4 – Capacity verification**

A 10 GbE link with 64b/66b encoding yields:

$$
C_{\text{net}} = 10\ \text{Gbps} \times \frac{64}{66} \approx 9.7\ \text{Gbps}
$$

Since 200 Mbps ≪ 9.7 Gbps, the transport capacity is ample.

### 4. Practical Notes & Common Mistakes

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | How to avoid |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| **Neglecting dispersion penalty** | At 100 Gbps+ the chromatic dispersion can limit reach, especially on multimode fiber. | Use single‑mode fiber for > 10 Gbps; verify dispersion budget with the transceiver’s tolerance. |
| **Using only FSPL for microwave** | Atmospheric gases, foliage, and rain introduce extra loss, especially > 20 GHz. | Apply ITU‑R models for rain, gaseous attenuation, and include a fade margin (≥ 10 dB). |
| **Mismatching impedance on fiber** | Connector type (LC, SC) or polishing loss can add > 1 dB per pair. | Perform a visual inspection, use APC connectors for low return loss, and test with an OTDR. |
| **Assuming symmetric traffic** | Back‑haul often carries aggregated traffic from many cells; peak load can be several times the average. | Dimension back‑haul with a traffic‑burst factor (e.g., 2–3× average) and consider statistical multiplexing. |
| **Overlooking regulatory limits** | Microwave bands may have maximum ERP or EIRP restrictions. | Consult national spectrum authority tables; include required attenuation or lower power amplifiers. |

A well‑engineered transport network blends fiber’s ultra‑low loss and high capacity with microwave’s rapid deployability for hard‑to‑reach sites, while respecting the distinct budgets of front‑haul, mid‑haul, and back‑haul.
