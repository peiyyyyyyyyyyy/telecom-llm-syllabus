## 正文

In modern cellular and broadband wireless networks, interference is the dominant factor limiting spectral efficiency, coverage, and quality of service (QoS). While the traditional notion of interference focuses on co‑channel users sharing the same frequency, two increasingly relevant phenomena are **Remote Interference Management (RIM)** and **Cross‑Link Interference (CLI)**.  

*Why they matter*  
- **Remote Interference (RIM)** arises when a transmission from a cell or sector that is not the serving cell of a user nonetheless creates a measurable interference level at the user equipment (UE). This can happen in dense heterogeneous deployments (macro‑pico, macro‑small‑cell, or multi‑operator sharing) where the physical distance to the interfering transmitter is modest but the path loss is reduced by line‑of‑sight (LOS) conditions or by favorable antenna patterns. RIM degrades the received signal‑to‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio (SINR) and can trigger unnecessary handovers or radio link failures.  

- **Cross‑Link Interference (CLI)** appears in systems that reuse the same spectrum across *different* logical links, such as in Time Division Duplex (TDD) LTE/NR where uplink (UL) and downlink (DL) transmissions occur on the same carrier but in opposite directions, or in coordinated multipoint (CoMP) where multiple transmission points (TPs) serve a UE simultaneously. When the UL transmission of one UE interferes with the DL reception of another UE (or the same UE) on a neighboring cell, the interference is termed CLI. CLI is especially critical in dense TDD deployments because UL and DL power levels differ by orders of magnitude (e.g., UE TX power ≈ 23 dBm vs. eNodeB TX power ≈ 46 dBm).

Both RIM and CLI are *non‑co‑channel* in the sense that they do not belong to the same scheduled resource block (RB) of the serving cell, yet they occupy the same frequency band and time slot, thereby contaminating the intended signal.

---

### Key Definitions  

| Symbol | Definition | Unit |
|--------|------------|------|
| $P_{\text{tx}}^{(i)}$ | Transmit power of interfering node $i$ (eNB, small cell, or UE) | dBm |
| $G_{\text{tx}}^{(i)}$, $G_{\text{rx}}^{(j)}$ | Antenna gains of transmitter $i$ and receiver $j$ | dBi |
| $L_{i\to j}$ | Large‑scale path loss from $i$ to $j$, including shadowing | dB |
| $F_{i\to j}$ | Small‑scale fading factor (linear, $0< F\le 1$) | – |
| $I_{\text{RIM}}$ | Aggregate remote interference power at the UE | mW |
| $I_{\text{CLI}}$ | Aggregate cross‑link interference power at the receiver (UE or eNB) | mW |
| $N_0$ | Thermal noise power density (≈ −174 dBm/Hz) | dBm/Hz |
| $B$ | System bandwidth (e.g., 10 MHz) | Hz |
| $\text{SINR}$ | Signal‑to‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio | linear (or dB) |

The received interference power from a single interferer $i$ is  

$$
P_{i\to j}^{\text{rx}} \;=\; P_{\text{tx}}^{(i)} + G_{\text{tx}}^{(i)} + G_{\text{rx}}^{(j)} - L_{i\to j} + 10\log_{10}(F_{i\to j})\;\;[\text{dBm}]
$$

The total remote interference is the sum over all remote interferers $\mathcal{R}$:  

$$
I_{\text{RIM}} \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}} 10^{\frac{P_{i\to j}^{\text{rx}}}{10}} \;\;[\text{mW}]
$$

Similarly, for CLI we consider UL‑to‑DL (or DL‑to‑UL) cross‑link pairs $\mathcal{C}$:  

$$
I_{\text{CLI}} \;=\; \sum_{(u,b)\in\mathcal{C}} 10^{\frac{P_{u\to b}^{\text{rx}}}{10}} \;\;[\text{mW}]
$$

The SINR at the intended receiver (UE for DL, eNB for UL) is then  

$$
\text{SINR} \;=\; \frac{P_{\text{serv}}^{\text{rx}}}{I_{\text{RIM}} + I_{\text{CLI}} + N_0 B}
$$

where $P_{\text{serv}}^{\text{rx}}$ is the received power from the serving transmitter.

---

### Worked Example  

Consider a 5 G NR TDD cell with $B = 20$ MHz.  

- Serving eNB DL TX power: $P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{serv}} = 46$ dBm, $G_{\text{tx}}^{\text{serv}} = 15$ dBi, UE antenna gain $G_{\text{rx}}^{\text{UE}} = 0$ dBi, path loss $L_{\text{serv}\to\text{UE}} = 130$ dB.  

- Remote small cell (SC) operating on the same carrier, DL TX power $P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{SC}} = 30$ dBm, $G_{\text{tx}}^{\text{SC}} = 10$ dBi, distance to UE such that $L_{\text{SC}\to\text{UE}} = 110$ dB.  

- CLI scenario: a neighboring UE $U_1$ transmits UL at $P_{\text{tx}}^{U_1}=23$ dBm, $G_{\text{tx}}^{U_1}=0$ dBi, UE‑to‑eNB path loss $L_{U_1\to\text{eNB}} = 115$ dB.  

Assume $F=1$ (no fading) and $N_0 = -174$ dBm/Hz.

1. **Serving DL received power**  

$$
P_{\text{serv}}^{\text{rx}} = 46 + 15 + 0 - 130 = -69\ \text{dBm}
$$

2. **Remote interference power**  

$$
P_{\text{SC}}^{\text{rx}} = 30 + 10 + 0 - 110 = -70\ \text{dBm}
$$
$$
I_{\text{RIM}} = 10^{\frac{-70}{10}} = 10^{-7}\ \text{W}=0.1\ \text{mW}
$$

3. **CLI power (UL‑to‑DL)**  

$$
P_{U_1\to\text{eNB}}^{\text{rx}} = 23 + 0 + 0 - 115 = -92\ \text{dBm}
$$
$$
I_{\text{CLI}} = 10^{\frac{-92}{10}} = 6.3\times10^{-10}\ \text{W}=0.00063\ \text{mW}
$$

4. **Noise power**  

$$
N = N_0 + 10\log_{10}(B) = -174 + 10\log_{10}(20\times10^{6}) \approx -174 + 73 = -101\ \text{dBm}
$$
$$
N = 10^{\frac{-101}{10}} = 7.9\times10^{-11}\ \text{W}=0.000079\ \text{mW}
$$

5. **SINR**  

$$
\text{SINR} = \frac{10^{-6.9}}{0.1 + 0.00063 + 0.000079}\approx \frac{1.26\times10^{-7}}{0.100709}\approx 1.25\times10^{-6}
$$
$$
\text{SINR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(1.25\times10^{-6}) \approx -58\ \text{dB}
$$

The SINR is catastrophically low because the remote small cell contributes interference comparable to the desired signal. If the SC were muted or its DL power reduced by 10 dB, the SINR would improve by roughly 10 dB, illustrating the importance of RIM mitigation (e.g., coordinated scheduling, power control, or dynamic TDD configuration).

### Practical Interference‑Management Techniques  

| Technique | RIM Target | CLI Target | Remarks |
|-----------|------------|------------|---------|
| **Dynamic TDD (DTDD) alignment** | – | Reduces overlapping UL/DL sub‑frames between neighboring cells, lowering CLI probability. | Requires fast backhaul signaling (sub‑ms). |
| **Inter‑cell Interference Coordination (ICIC) / eICIC** | Adjusts DL power, PRB muting for remote cells. | Can also mute UL resources to protect DL. | Legacy LTE term; still used for RIM in heterogeneous networks. |
| **Beamforming & Null Steering** | Directs main lobe away from remote UEs, creates spatial nulls toward them. | Nulls can be placed toward UL‑receiving nodes of neighboring cells. | Dependent on antenna array size and CSI accuracy. |
| **Power Control (Closed‑loop)** | UE and eNB adjust TX power based on measured RIM. | UL power reduction directly cuts CLI. | Trade‑off with coverage; must respect minimum QoS. |
| **Frequency Partitioning** | Separate frequency bands for macro and small cells (partial reuse). | Assign orthogonal sub‑bands for UL/DL in adjacent cells. | Simple but reduces spectral efficiency. |
| **Coordinated Scheduling (CS) / CoMP** | Scheduler jointly decides which resources remote cells may use. | Align UL/DL schedules to avoid simultaneous opposite‑direction transmissions. | Requires tight X2 or Xn interface latency (<1 ms). |

### Common Mistakes & Practical Notes  

1. **Treating RIM as negligible because of distance** – In dense urban or indoor deployments, path loss exponents can be as low as 2 (free‑space) due to waveguide effects; remote interferers may dominate the interference budget.  

2. **Assuming CLI only occurs in TDD** – Even in FDD, cross‑link can arise from carrier aggregation where different component carriers share the same hardware chain, leading to leakage.  

3. **Neglecting antenna pattern side‑lobes** – Small side‑lobe gains (e.g., 3 dBi) can raise remote interference unexpectedly.  

4. **Using only average SINR for planning** – CLI is bursty; instantaneous SINR can dip far below the average, causing HARQ failures. Simulations should include the full distribution.  

5. **Over‑relying on static ICIC patterns** – Static muting does not adapt to traffic load changes; dynamic schemes (DTDD, CS) are essential for modern dense networks.  

**Practical tip:** When measuring RIM in the field, use a calibrated spectrum analyzer or UE drive‑test tool to capture the *interference‐plus‑noise* floor while the serving cell is silent (e.g., during a scheduled blank sub‑frame). This isolates remote contributions and helps calibrate the interference models used in network planning tools.
