## 正文

Wireless LANs (WLANs) and wired Ethernet are the backbone of campus, enterprise, and home connectivity.  The IEEE 802 family defines the media‑access, frame, and physical‑layer (PHY) specifications that enable devices from different vendors to interoperate.  In practice, “Wi‑Fi” refers to the IEEE 802.11 family, while “Ethernet” corresponds to IEEE 802.3.  A related low‑rate, low‑power family, IEEE 802.15.4, underpins Zigbee, Thread and many IoT solutions.  Understanding the protocol stack, data‑rate calculations, and the constraints imposed by the shared medium is essential for designing reliable, high‑capacity networks.

---

### 1. Key Definitions and Formulas  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $B$ | Channel bandwidth (the width of the allocated spectrum) | Hz |
| $M$ | Modulation order (e.g., $M=64$ for 64‑QAM) | – |
| $k=\log_2 M$ | Bits per modulation symbol | bits/symbol |
| $R_c$ | Coding rate (ratio of information bits to total bits after FEC) | – |
| $N_{\text{ss}}$ | Number of spatial streams (MIMO) | – |
| $\eta$ | Spectral efficiency = $k R_c N_{\text{ss}}$ | bits/s/Hz |
| $R_{\text{raw}}$ | Raw PHY data rate = $\eta B$ | bits/s (bps) |
| $O$ | MAC‑layer overhead factor (fraction of time spent on preamble, ACK, inter‑frame spaces, etc.) | – |
| $R_{\text{net}}$ | Net user throughput = $(1-O) R_{\text{raw}}$ | bps |
| $P_{\text{tx}}$ | Transmit power (dBm) | dBm |
| $G_{\text{tx}}, G_{\text{rx}}$ | Antenna gains (dBi) | dBi |
| $L_{\text{fs}}$ | Free‑space path loss (dB) | dB |
| $L_{\text{other}}$ | Additional losses (e.g., wall attenuation) | dB |
| $P_{\text{rx}}$ | Received power | dBm |

**Raw PHY data rate**  
$$
R_{\text{raw}} = \eta \, B = k\,R_c\,N_{\text{ss}}\, B
$$
The IEEE 802.11 PHY defines $k$ and $R_c$ for each Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS).  For example, MCS 9 in 802.11ac uses 256‑QAM ($k=8$) with $R_c=5/6$.

**Link budget** (simplified)  
$$
P_{\text{rx}} = P_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{rx}} - L_{\text{fs}} - L_{\text{other}}
$$
A device can reliably decode a frame if $P_{\text{rx}}$ exceeds the receiver sensitivity for the selected MCS.

**Shannon capacity (theoretical upper bound)**  
$$
C = B \log_2\!\bigl(1 + \mathrm{SNR}\bigr) \quad\text{[bits/s]}
$$
where $\mathrm{SNR}=P_{\text{rx}}/N_0 B$ and $N_0$ is the noise spectral density (≈ –174 dBm/Hz at 300 K).  Real 802.11 implementations achieve 30‑50 % of $C$ due to practical coding, overhead, and contention.

**Medium Access**  
Wi‑Fi uses Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA).  Prior to transmitting, a station senses the channel for a Distributed Coordination Function Inter‑Frame Space (DIFS) and then selects a random backoff counter.  Optional RTS/CTS handshaking reduces hidden‑node collisions but adds extra overhead.

**Key MAC frame fields**  
- **Frame Control** (type/subtype, flags)  
- **Duration/ID** (NAV reservation)  
- **Address fields** (up to 4 MAC addresses)  
- **Sequence Control** (fragment number, sequence number)  
- **Payload** (LLC data)  
- **FCS** (CRC‑32)  

**802.15.4 PHY** operates in the 2.4 GHz ISM band with 250 kbps data rate using O‑QPSK and a 2 MHz channel bandwidth.  It employs a slotted CSMA/CA algorithm and optional ACK frames, making it suitable for low‑power sensor networks.

**802.3 Ethernet** defines a deterministic, wired MAC based on Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD) for half‑duplex links, and full‑duplex point‑to‑point links that eliminate collisions.  Modern Ethernet uses fixed PHY rates (e.g., 1 GbE, 10 GbE) and auto‑negotiation of duplex and speed.

### 2. Worked Example  

**Problem:** A Wi‑Fi 6 (802.11ax) access point (AP) serves a single STA on a 20 MHz channel using MCS 11 (1024‑QAM, $R_c=5/6$, $N_{\text{ss}}=2$).  The MAC overhead for preamble, SIFS, ACK, and inter‑frame spacing is estimated at $O = 0.25$ (i.e., 25 % of channel time).  Compute:

1. The raw PHY data rate $R_{\text{raw}}$.  
2. The net user throughput $R_{\text{net}}$.

**Solution:**  

1. Modulation order $M = 1024 \Rightarrow k = \log_2 1024 = 10$ bits/symbol.  
   Spectral efficiency $\eta = k R_c N_{\text{ss}} = 10 \times \frac{5}{6} \times 2 = \frac{100}{6} \approx 16.67\;\text{bits/s/Hz}$.  

   With $B = 20\;\text{MHz} = 20\times10^6\;\text{Hz}$:  

   $$
   R_{\text{raw}} = \eta B = 16.67 \times 20\times10^6 \approx 333.4 \times10^6\;\text{bps} = 333\;\text{Mbps}.
   $$

2. Accounting for overhead:  

   $$
   R_{\text{net}} = (1-O) R_{\text{raw}} = 0.75 \times 333\;\text{Mbps} \approx 250\;\text{Mbps}.
   $$

Thus the STA can expect a sustained throughput of roughly **250 Mbps** under ideal conditions.  In practice, contention with other stations, channel fading, and regulatory power limits will reduce this value.

### 3. Common Mistakes and Practical Notes  

| Mistake | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Equating raw data rate with user throughput** | Overlooks MAC overhead (preamble, ACK, back‑off) and contention, leading to over‑optimistic capacity planning. | Always apply an overhead factor (typically 20–30 % for 802.11ac/ax) and consider realistic traffic mixes. |
| **Ignoring channel bonding limits** | Wider channels increase $B$ but also raise interference probability and may be prohibited by local regulations. | Verify regional channel plan; use dynamic frequency selection (DFS) to avoid radar bands. |
| **Forgetting antenna gain and path‑loss in link budget** | Leads to deployments where stations cannot achieve the required MCS, causing frequent retransmissions. | Compute $P_{\text{rx}}$ for the worst‑case distance; select appropriate AP transmit power and antenna types. |
| **Misconfiguring RTS/CTS** | Disabling RTS/CTS in dense environments can cause hidden‑node collisions, increasing packet loss. | Enable RTS/CTS for frames larger than the RTS threshold in high‑density or obstructed deployments. |
| **Assuming Ethernet is always error‑free** | In long copper runs (>100 m) or with poor cabling, bit errors and collisions (in half‑duplex) can appear. | Use proper cable categories, respect maximum segment lengths, and prefer full‑duplex links wherever possible. |

**Practical tip:** When dimensioning a Wi‑Fi network, start from the *net* throughput requirement per user, back‑calculate the needed raw PHY rate, then select the smallest MCS and channel width that satisfy the link budget at the cell edge.  For IoT deployments, IEEE 802.15.4’s low duty‑cycle and channel hopping provide resilience against interference, but its 250 kbps ceiling limits video or high‑speed data use cases.
