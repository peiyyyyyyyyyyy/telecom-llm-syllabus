## 正文

In a telecommunications network the ultimate purpose of the radio access and the core transport layers is to move user data from a source to a destination. Two elementary yet intertwined performance figures describe how well the network accomplishes this task:

* **Throughput** – the amount of user‑payload bits successfully delivered per unit time (usually expressed in bits s$^{-1}$ or Mbit s$^{-1}$).  
* **Latency** – the time elapsed between the moment a user‑layer packet (or a higher‑layer segment) is generated and the moment it is received at the destination (expressed in seconds or milliseconds).

Both metrics directly impact the perceived quality of service (QoS). Video streaming, cloud gaming, and real‑time industrial control are highly sensitive to latency, whereas bulk file transfer and software updates are primarily limited by throughput. Network designers therefore must understand how throughput and latency are produced, how they interact, and how to measure them accurately.

---

### 1.6.3.1 Formal Definitions

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit | Description |
|--------|----------|------|-------------|
| $T$    | **Throughput** | bit s$^{-1}$ (or Mbit s$^{-1}$) | Average number of payload bits delivered per second over a measurement interval. |
| $B$    | **Channel bandwidth** | Hz | Width of the allocated radio or wired spectrum. |
| $\eta$ | **Spectral efficiency** | bit s$^{-1}$ Hz$^{-1}$ | Net useful bits transmitted per second per Hertz, after accounting for coding, modulation, and protocol overhead. |
| $R_{\text{peak}}$ | **Peak data rate** | bit s$^{-1}$ | Theoretical maximum $R_{\text{peak}} = B\;\eta$ assuming continuous transmission and no higher‑layer constraints. |
| $L$    | **Latency** | s (or ms) | End‑to‑end one‑way delay from packet generation to successful reception. |
| $D_{\text{prop}}$ | **Propagation delay** | s | Time for the signal to travel the physical path (distance / propagation speed). |
| $D_{\text{tx}}$  | **Transmission delay** | s | Time to push all bits of a packet onto the link: $D_{\text{tx}} = \frac{P}{R}$, where $P$ is packet size (bits) and $R$ is the link data rate (bit s$^{-1}$). |
| $D_{\text{proc}}$| **Processing delay** | s | Time spent in encoding/decoding, header compression, scheduling, etc., at each node. |
| $D_{\text{queue}}$| **Queueing delay** | s | Time a packet waits in buffers because the link is busy. |
| $BDP$  | **Bandwidth‑Delay Product** | bit | Product $B\cdot L$, representing the amount of data “in flight” on a link. |

The **end‑to‑end latency** is the sum of the four components:

$$
L = D_{\text{prop}} + D_{\text{tx}} + D_{\text{proc}} + D_{\text{queue}} .
$$

Throughput measured over a finite interval $T_{\text{obs}}$ can be expressed as

$$
T = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i}{T_{\text{obs}}}
$$

where $P_i$ is the payload size of the $i^{\text{th}}$ successfully received packet and $N$ is the number of such packets.

---

### 1.6.3.2 Relationship Between Throughput and Latency

A useful engineering insight is the **bandwidth‑delay product (BDP)**:

$$
\text{BDP} = B \times L .
$$

BDP tells how many bits must be “in the pipe” to fully utilize the available bandwidth. If a sender’s congestion window (or, in LTE/NR, the HARQ process buffer) is smaller than the BDP, the link cannot be kept busy, and the observed throughput will fall short of the peak $R_{\text{peak}}$ even though the radio conditions are excellent.

---

### 1.6.3.3 Worked Example

Consider an LTE‑Advanced cell operating with a 20 MHz carrier (the standard 5‑MHz component carrier aggregation factor 4). Assume the following:

* Spectral efficiency $\eta = 2.0$ bit s$^{-1}$ Hz$^{-1}$ (typical for 64‑QAM with a moderate coding rate).  
* Average packet size $P = 1500$ bytes = $12\,000$ bits.  
* Propagation distance $d = 1000$ km over fiber (propagation speed $v \approx 2\times10^{8}$ m s$^{-1}$).  
* Processing delay per hop $D_{\text{proc}} = 1$ ms (typical for LTE MAC/PHY).  
* Queueing delay $D_{\text{queue}} = 2$ ms (moderately loaded core network).  

1. **Peak data rate**  

$$
R_{\text{peak}} = B \,\eta = 20\times10^{6}\,\text{Hz}\times 2.0\;\frac{\text{bit}}{\text{s·Hz}} = 40\times10^{6}\;\text{bit s}^{-1}=40\;\text{Mbit s}^{-1}.
$$

2. **Transmission delay** (sending one 1500‑byte packet at the peak rate)  

$$
D_{\text{tx}} = \frac{P}{R_{\text{peak}}}= \frac{12\,000\;\text{bit}}{40\times10^{6}\;\text{bit s}^{-1}} = 3.0\times10^{-4}\;\text{s}=0.30\;\text{ms}.
$$

3. **Propagation delay**  

$$
D_{\text{prop}} = \frac{d}{v}= \frac{1\,000\,000\;\text{m}}{2\times10^{8}\;\text{m s}^{-1}} = 5.0\times10^{-3}\;\text{s}=5.0\;\text{ms}.
$$

4. **Total one‑way latency**  

$$
L = D_{\text{prop}} + D_{\text{tx}} + D_{\text{proc}} + D_{\text{queue}}
   = 5.0\;\text{ms} + 0.30\;\text{ms} + 1.0\;\text{ms} + 2.0\;\text{ms}
   = 8.30\;\text{ms}.
$$

5. **Bandwidth‑Delay Product**  

$$
\text{BDP} = B \times L = 20\times10^{6}\;\text{Hz}\times 8.30\times10^{-3}\;\text{s}
          = 166\,000\;\text{bit} \approx 21\;\text{kB}.
$$

Interpretation: To keep the 20 MHz LTE link fully utilized, the sender must have at least ~21 kB of outstanding data (e.g., multiple HARQ processes or a TCP congestion window). If the application only sends a single 1500‑byte packet and then waits for an acknowledgement, the effective throughput collapses to roughly $P/L \approx 1.44$ Mbit s$^{-1}$, far below the 40 Mbit s$^{-1}$ peak.

### 1.6.3.4 Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Confusing bandwidth with throughput** | Bandwidth ($B$) is a physical resource; throughput ($T$) is what the user actually experiences after protocol overhead, retransmissions, and congestion. | Always report both: *available* bandwidth (e.g., 20 MHz) and *measured* throughput (e.g., 28 Mbit s$^{-1}$). |
| **Neglecting protocol overhead** | MAC headers, RLC segmentation, and IP/UDP/TCP headers can consume 10‑30 % of the radio resources, reducing effective $\eta$. | Include the overhead factor $ \eta_{\text{net}} = \eta \times (1 - \text{overhead\%})$ when calculating $R_{\text{peak}}$. |
| **Assuming latency is constant** | Queueing delay $D_{\text{queue}}$ varies with load; under heavy traffic it can dominate $L$, causing jitter and throughput collapse. | Measure latency under representative load conditions, and use queuing theory (e.g., M/M/1) to predict $D_{\text{queue}}$. |
| **Ignoring BDP when sizing buffers** | Too small a transmit buffer yields under‑utilization; too large a buffer leads to bufferbloat and excessive RTT. | Dimension buffers roughly equal to the BDP, and apply Active Queue Management (AQM) to keep queueing delay low. |
| **Using one‑way latency without symmetry** | In many wireless links the uplink and downlink have different capacities and processing times, so the round‑trip time (RTT) is not simply $2L$. | When evaluating protocols that rely on RTT (e.g., TCP), measure both directions independently. |

By keeping these considerations in mind, engineers can correctly interpret throughput and latency measurements, design radio resource management (RRM) algorithms that respect the BDP, and ultimately deliver services that meet the stringent requirements of modern applications.
