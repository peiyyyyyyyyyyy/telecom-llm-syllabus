## 正文

In any cellular system the radio link between a user equipment (UE) and a base‑station (eNodeB/gNodeB) is characterised first by how much power arrives at the UE antenna. This *signal strength* directly influences coverage planning, hand‑over decisions, and power‑control algorithms. Two quantities are universally used in 3GPP LTE/NR specifications: **Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP)** and **Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI)**. Although both are expressed in decibel‑milliwatts (dBm), they measure different aspects of the received waveform and therefore have distinct roles in network optimisation.

---

### 1. Definitions and formulas  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $P_{\text{RS}}$ | Power of a single reference signal (e.g., LTE CRS, NR SS/PBCH) measured over its bandwidth | dBm |
| $N_{\text{RB}}$ | Number of resource blocks (RBs) over which RSSI is integrated | – |
| $B_{\text{RB}}$ | Bandwidth of one RB (15 kHz in LTE, 12 sub‑carriers × 15 kHz = 180 kHz in NR) | Hz |
| $B_{\text{RSSI}}$ | Total measurement bandwidth = $N_{\text{RB}}\times B_{\text{RB}}$ | Hz |
| $P_{\text{RSSI}}$ | Total received power (signal + inter‑cell interference + thermal noise) summed over $B_{\text{RSSI}}$ | dBm |
| $P_{\text{RSRP}}$ | Average received power of the *cell‑specific* reference signal, *excluding* interference and noise | dBm |
| $G_{\text{UE}}$ | UE antenna gain (often omitted because both RSRP and RSSI are measured at the UE input) | dBi |

#### 1.1 RSRP  

For LTE, RSRP is defined as the linear average of the power of the **cell‑specific reference signal (CRS)** over the *resource elements* (REs) that carry the CRS within a measurement bandwidth of 180 kHz (i.e., one RB). In NR the analogous quantity is the **SS‑RSRP** measured on the synchronization signal block (SSB). The formal definition is:

$$
\boxed{ P_{\text{RSRP}} \;[\text{dBm}] \;=\; 10\log_{10}\!\left( \frac{1}{N_{\text{RE}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{RE}}} P_{i} \right) }
$$

where $P_{i}$ is the linear power (in milliwatts) of the $i$‑th RE containing the reference signal, and $N_{\text{RE}}$ is the total number of such REs in the measurement bandwidth (typically 12 REs per RB for LTE CRS). Because only the *desired* cell’s reference signal is considered, RSRP is a clean indicator of the *large‑scale path loss* experienced by the UE.

#### 1.2 RSSI  

RSSI aggregates **all** power observed in the measurement bandwidth, regardless of its origin:

$$
\boxed{ P_{\text{RSSI}} \;[\text{dBm}] \;=\; 10\log_{10}\!\left( \sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{RB}}} \bigl( P_{\text{signal},k}+P_{\text{interf},k}+P_{\text{noise},k} \bigr) \right) }
$$

In practice the UE hardware integrates the received waveform over $N_{\text{RB}}$ contiguous RBs (often the full system bandwidth) and reports a single value. RSSI therefore reflects *total* received power, which is useful for estimating the **carrier‑to‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio (CINR)** when combined with the corresponding RSRP measurement.

#### 1.3 Relationship between RSRP, RSSI, and SINR  

When the UE knows the number of RBs used for the RSSI measurement, a simple approximation of the **Signal‑to‑Interference‑plus‑Noise Ratio (SINR)** can be derived:

$$
\text{SINR}_{\text{lin}} \approx \frac{P_{\text{RSRP,lin}}}{P_{\text{RSSI,lin}} - P_{\text{RSRP,lin}}}
$$

where the subscript “lin” denotes linear power (mW) obtained by converting dBm values: $P_{\text{lin}} = 10^{(P_{\text{dBm}}-30)/10}$.

---

### 2. Worked example  

A UE connected to an LTE cell measures the following:

* Measured RSRP = **–95 dBm** (average over the CRS REs).
* Measured RSSI = **–78 dBm** over the full 10 MHz carrier (i.e., $N_{\text{RB}}=50$ RBs, each 180 kHz).

**Step 1 – Convert to linear power.**  

$$
P_{\text{RSRP,lin}} = 10^{\frac{-95-30}{10}} = 10^{-12.5}\,\text{W}= 3.16\times10^{-13}\,\text{W}
$$

$$
P_{\text{RSSI,lin}} = 10^{\frac{-78-30}{10}} = 10^{-10.8}\,\text{W}= 1.58\times10^{-11}\,\text{W}
$$

**Step 2 – Estimate interference + noise power.**  

$$
P_{\text{I+N,lin}} = P_{\text{RSSI,lin}} - P_{\text{RSRP,lin}} \approx 1.58\times10^{-11} - 3.16\times10^{-13} \approx 1.55\times10^{-11}\,\text{W}
$$

**Step 3 – Compute SINR (linear and dB).**  

$$
\text{SINR}_{\text{lin}} = \frac{3.16\times10^{-13}}{1.55\times10^{-11}} \approx 0.0204
$$

$$
\text{SINR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(0.0204) \approx -16.9\;\text{dB}
$$

A SINR of –17 dB indicates the UE is operating at the edge of coverage; higher‑order modulation would be impossible, and the scheduler will likely allocate robust coding (e.g., QPSK with low code rate).

---

### 3. Practical notes and common pitfalls  

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | Correct practice |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| **Confusing RSRP with RSSI** | Both are reported in dBm and obtained from the same RF front‑end, leading to the assumption they are interchangeable. | Remember that RSRP isolates the *desired* cell’s reference signal, while RSSI includes *all* power in the measurement bandwidth. Use RSRP for path‑loss estimation, RSSI for CINR calculations. |
| **Using the wrong measurement bandwidth for RSSI** | Some UE manufacturers expose RSSI over a *single* RB (180 kHz) while the spec defines it over the *entire* system bandwidth. | Verify the UE’s reporting mode (e.g., “RSSI‑full‑band” vs “RSSI‑RB”). Adjust the $N_{\text{RB}}$ value accordingly when converting to SINR. |
| **Neglecting antenna gain** | RSRP and RSSI are defined at the UE antenna port; forgetting the UE’s gain can lead to inconsistent link‑budget calculations. | When comparing to a theoretical path‑loss model, add the UE antenna gain (typically 0 dBi for handset) to the measured value. |
| **Assuming linearity of dBm subtraction** | Some engineers subtract dBm values directly (e.g., “RSSI – RSRP”) to obtain interference power, which is mathematically invalid. | Convert both measurements to linear watts, subtract, then convert back to dBm if needed. |
| **Over‑reliance on a single sample** | RSRP and RSSI fluctuate due to fast fading; a single reading may misrepresent the link quality. | Average multiple consecutive measurements (e.g., over 200 ms) or use the UE’s built‑in filtering before feeding the values into network‑planning tools. |

---

### 4. Relevance to network design  

* **Coverage maps** are generated from RSRP heat‑maps because RSRP correlates strongly with distance‑dependent path loss.  
* **Capacity planning** uses RSSI together with measured interference to predict the achievable throughput per cell.  
* **Mobility algorithms** (e.g., hand‑over trigger thresholds) often compare the serving cell’s RSRP against neighbouring cells’ RSRP values, while RSSI helps to avoid hand‑overs into overly noisy cells.  

Understanding the distinction and proper handling of RSRP and RSSI is therefore essential for any telecom engineer involved in radio‑network optimisation, performance monitoring, or algorithm development.
