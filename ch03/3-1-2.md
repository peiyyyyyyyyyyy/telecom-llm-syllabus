# 3.1.2 自由空間路徑損耗

所屬：[3.1 訊號與通道](3-1.md)

## 內文

#### 為什麼要學

自由空間路徑損耗（free-space path loss, FSPL）是所有無線鏈路計算的起點：衛星鏈路、微波回傳、視距（line-of-sight, LOS）的毫米波鏈路幾乎可以直接用它估算；地面行動網路雖然還要加上繞射、遮蔽等額外損耗，但規劃時也常用 FSPL 當作「最好情況」的下限，或用它算參考距離上的損耗。知道 FSPL 怎麼隨距離和頻率變化，就能快速判斷「頻段從 700 MHz 換到 3.5 GHz 會多掉幾 dB」「距離加倍要多多少功率」這類問題。

#### 定義與符號

| 符號 | 意思 | 單位 |
|---|---|---|
| $P_t$、$P_r$ | 發射功率、接收功率 | W（或 dBm） |
| $G_t$、$G_r$ | 發射、接收天線增益 | 無因次（或 dBi） |
| $d$ | 收發天線之間的距離 | m（或 km） |
| $f$ | 載波頻率 | Hz（或 MHz、GHz） |
| $\lambda$ | 波長，$\lambda = c/f$ | m |
| $c$ | 光速，$299{,}792{,}458$ m/s | m/s |
| $L_{\text{FS}}$ | 自由空間路徑損耗（兩個等向性天線之間） | 無因次，或 dB |
| $D$ | 天線最大尺寸（判斷遠場用） | m |

#### 公式與推導

發射功率 $P_t$ 經等向性天線均勻散到半徑 $d$ 的球面上，功率密度是 $P_t/(4\pi d^2)$。接收端的等向性天線有效面積是 $\lambda^2/(4\pi)$，兩者相乘再把天線增益放進來，就得到 Friis 傳輸公式：

$$P_r = P_t\,G_t\,G_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2$$

把天線增益拿掉後剩下的那一項的倒數，就是自由空間路徑損耗：

$$L_{\text{FS}} = \left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right)^2$$

取 dB：

$$L_{\text{FS}}(\text{dB}) = 20\log_{10}d + 20\log_{10}f + 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi}{c}$$

當 $d$ 用公尺、$f$ 用赫茲時，最後一項是 $20\log_{10}(4\pi/299{,}792{,}458) = -147.55$ dB。換成工程上常用的單位，只要把單位的倍數併進常數：

| $d$ 的單位 | $f$ 的單位 | 併入的量 | 常數 |
|---|---|---|---|
| km | MHz | $20\log_{10}10^3 + 20\log_{10}10^6 = 180$ | $-147.55 + 180 = 32.45$（常寫成 32.44 或 32.4） |
| km | GHz | $60 + 180 = 240$ | $-147.55 + 240 = 92.45$ |
| m | GHz | $0 + 180 = 180$ | $32.45$（與 km／MHz 相同） |

$$L_{\text{FS}}(\text{dB}) = 32.44 + 20\log_{10}d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10}d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10}f_{\text{GHz}}$$

用精確光速算出的常數是 32.448 與 92.448；把光速取成 $3\times10^8$ m/s 時是 32.442 與 92.442。所以文獻上 32.44、32.45、32.4 都看得到，彼此只差 0.01 dB，對工程計算沒有影響；真正要緊的是**常數必須和單位配對**：32.44 配 km 與 MHz（或 m 與 GHz），92.45 配 km 與 GHz。

從公式可以直接讀出兩個規律：距離加倍或頻率加倍，損耗各增加 $20\log_{10}2 = 6.02$ dB；距離或頻率變 10 倍，損耗增加 20 dB。

適用條件：收發之間是沒有遮蔽、沒有反射的視距路徑；接收點在發射天線的遠場（一般取 $d \ge 2D^2/\lambda$）；公式裡的損耗是對等向性天線定義的，實際天線的增益另外加。頻率越高損耗越大，並不是空氣把高頻吃掉了，而是因為同樣增益的接收天線在高頻時有效面積比較小；大氣吸收、降雨衰減是另外要加的項目，不在 FSPL 裡面。

#### 算例

**算例 1：3.5 GHz、2 km**

已知：$f = 3500$ MHz，$d = 2$ km，視距無遮蔽。求 FSPL（dB，取到小數第 2 位）。

- 選公式：km 與 MHz，用常數 32.44。
- $20\log_{10}2 = 6.02$ dB；$20\log_{10}3500 = 70.88$ dB。
- $L_{\text{FS}} = 32.44 + 6.02 + 70.88 = 109.34$ dB。
- 合理性檢查：直接用 $(4\pi d f/c)^2$ 與精確光速計算是 109.35 dB，只差 0.01 dB。若距離拉到 4 km，應該再多 6.02 dB，變成 115.36 dB。若同樣 2 km 但頻率改成 700 MHz，頻率是原來的 1/5，損耗少 $20\log_{10}5 = 13.98$ dB，變成 95.36 dB——這就是低頻段涵蓋比較遠的原因之一。

**算例 2：同步衛星下行鏈路**

已知：地球同步軌道衛星到地面站的距離取 35,786 km（衛星在正上方時的高度），下行頻率 12 GHz。求 FSPL。

- 選公式：km 與 GHz，用常數 92.45。
- $20\log_{10}35786 = 91.07$ dB；$20\log_{10}12 = 21.58$ dB。
- $L_{\text{FS}} = 92.45 + 91.07 + 21.58 = 205.10$ dB。
- 合理性檢查：衛星鏈路損耗在 200 dB 上下是正常量級，所以衛星通訊需要高增益的碟形天線。如果把 12 GHz 誤代成 12（MHz）卻仍用 92.45，或用 32.44 卻代 GHz，答案會差 60 dB，一看量級就能發現。

#### 常見錯誤

- **常數和單位不配對**：32.44 配 km／MHz，92.45 配 km／GHz；距離給公尺、頻率給 GHz 時先換單位，或改用 m／GHz 的 32.45。
- **寫成 $10\log_{10}$**：FSPL 和距離、頻率的平方成正比，dB 式裡是 $20\log_{10}$。
- **把天線增益算進兩次或漏算**：FSPL 不含天線增益；接收功率要寫成 $P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L_{\text{FS}}$（全部用 dB 系列單位）。
- **在非視距環境直接用 FSPL**：市區、室內的實際損耗通常大得多，要用 3.1.3 的模型。

#### 和其他小節的關係

本節的 dB 運算來自 3.1.1；3.1.3 的對數距離模型用本節的 FSPL 當作參考距離 $d_0$ 上的損耗，並把指數 2 推廣成路徑損耗指數 $n$；3.3.5 由最大容許路徑損耗反推涵蓋距離時，會把本節公式倒過來解 $d$；3.3.1 的 EIRP 與 3.3.2 的天線增益則是 Friis 公式裡的另外幾項。

**出處**　ITU-R 建議書 P.525-5〈Calculation of free-space attenuation〉（2024）；Friis 傳輸公式見 Rappaport, *Wireless Communications: Principles and Practice*，大尺度路徑損耗一章的自由空間傳播模型；Goldsmith, *Wireless Communications*，路徑損耗與陰影衰落一章。

#### 情境題：FSPL formula and unit-pair constants

The test case is a 4.9 GHz link with an 850 m unobstructed line-of-sight path in free space.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**詳解**　$20\log_{10}(850) = 58.5884$ dB. $20\log_{10}(4.9) = 13.8039$ dB. $L_{fs} = 32.45 + 58.5884 + 13.8039 = 104.8423$ dB, which rounds to 104.84 dB.

**3.1.2S002**　Forms A and B use different units for both distance and frequency, yet they have the same constant 32.45. Explain why.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The loss depends only on the product $d \cdot f$. Going from (km, MHz) to (m, GHz) makes the distance number 1000 times larger (+60 dB in the $20\log_{10} d$ term) and the frequency number 1000 times smaller (−60 dB in the $20\log_{10} f$ term). The two shifts cancel, so the constant does not change.

**詳解**　$d_{\text{km}} \cdot f_{\text{MHz}} = (d_{\text{m}}/10^{3}) \cdot (10^{3} f_{\text{GHz}}) = d_{\text{m}} \cdot f_{\text{GHz}}$. Since $L_{fs} = K + 20\log_{10}(d \cdot f)$ and the numerical product is identical for the two unit pairs, $K$ must be identical too.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525; T. S. Rappaport, *Wireless Communications: Principles and Practice* (free-space propagation model).

</details>

**3.1.2S003**　Form C has a constant that is exactly 60 dB larger than that of form A. Where does the 60 dB come from, and does it depend on the value used for the speed of light?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　It comes only from the frequency unit: a frequency expressed in GHz is a number 1000 times smaller than the same frequency in MHz, so $20\log_{10} f$ is 60 dB smaller and the constant must be 60 dB larger to compensate. The 60 dB is $20\log_{10}(1000)$ and is independent of $c$; the value of $c$ only decides whether the pair is 32.45/92.45 (exact $c$) or 32.44/92.44 ($c = 3\times10^{8}$ m/s).

**詳解**　$20\log_{10} f_{\text{MHz}} = 20\log_{10}(1000 f_{\text{GHz}}) = 60 + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{GHz}}$. Substituting into form A gives $32.45 + 60 + 20\log_{10} d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{GHz}}$, i.e. the constant 92.45 of form C.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946.

</details>

**3.1.2S009**　A script returns 68.47 dB as the free-space path loss of this 12.5 km, 5.06 GHz link. Without redoing the full calculation, how can an engineer tell that the result contains a unit mistake?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The loss at 1 km and 1 GHz is already 92.45 dB (the constant of the km/GHz form), and it grows with both distance and frequency. Any link longer than 1 km at a frequency above 1 GHz must therefore exceed 92.45 dB. A result of 68.47 dB is impossible and is exactly 60 dB below the true 128.47 dB, the signature of a factor-of-1000 unit slip (m versus km, or MHz versus GHz).

**詳解**　$128.47 - 68.47 = 60.00$ dB $= 20\log_{10}(1000)$. A one-step unit slip in either the distance or the frequency changes the result by exactly 60 dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S010**　Why is the free-space formula a reasonable model for this air-to-ground path, while it would be a poor model for two vehicles 12.5 km apart on the ground with 2 m antenna heights at the same frequency?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The free-space formula assumes an unobstructed path with no significant contribution from reflections or diffraction, i.e. a clear line of sight with the first Fresnel zone free of obstacles. The aircraft is high above the ground, so the direct ray is clear. For two low antennas 12.5 km apart the path grazes the terrain: Earth curvature, buildings, vegetation and the ground reflection all intervene, and the actual loss is far larger than the free-space value.

**詳解**　Free-space loss is the lower bound that applies when only spherical spreading acts on the wave. It gives the correct answer only when the propagation geometry keeps the ground and obstacles out of the first Fresnel zone.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525; ITU-R Recommendation P.530 (path clearance); T. S. Rappaport, *Wireless Communications: Principles and Practice*.

#### 情境題：Effect of doubling frequency or distance

**Scenario | Refarming a rooftop sector from 1800 MHz to 3600 MHz**

An operator moves a rooftop sector from an 1800 MHz carrier to a 3600 MHz carrier. The sector serves a fixed rooftop terminal 1.6 km away with a clear line of sight (free space assumed). In both bands the transmitter power is 46 dBm, the sector antenna gain is 18 dBi and the terminal antenna gain is 8 dBi. The planning team uses $L_{fs} = 32.45 + 20\log_{10} d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{MHz}}$.

**3.1.2S011**　By how many dB does the free-space path loss to the terminal increase when the carrier frequency doubles from 1800 MHz to 3600 MHz? Round to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　6.02 dB

**詳解**　$\Delta L = 20\log_{10}(3600/1800) = 20\log_{10}(2) = 6.0206$ dB, which rounds to 6.02 dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S012**　Compute the received power at the terminal on the 3600 MHz carrier in dBm, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　−35.66 dBm

**詳解**　$20\log_{10}(1.6) = 4.0824$ dB; $20\log_{10}(3600) = 71.1261$ dB. $L_{fs} = 32.45 + 4.0824 + 71.1261 = 107.6584$ dB. $P_r = 46 + 18 + 8 - 107.6584 = -35.6584$ dBm, which rounds to −35.66 dBm.

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946; ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S013**　A new engineer says "the air absorbs twice as much energy at 3600 MHz, which is why the free-space loss is higher." Is this explanation correct?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　No. Free-space loss contains no absorption at all. The wave spreads over the same sphere at both frequencies, so the power flux density at 1.6 km is the same. The frequency term appears because the formula is defined between isotropic antennas, and the effective aperture of an isotropic antenna, $\lambda^2/(4\pi)$, shrinks by a factor of four when the frequency doubles, so it collects one quarter of the power (−6.02 dB).

**詳解**　$P_r = S \cdot A_e$ with $S = \text{EIRP}/(4\pi d^2)$ independent of frequency and $A_e = G\lambda^2/(4\pi)$. Doubling $f$ halves $\lambda$ and divides $A_e$ by 4 for the same gain $G$.

**出處**　C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design* (effective aperture); H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946.

</details>

**3.1.2S014**　After the move to 3600 MHz, the operator wants the terminal to receive the same power as it did at 1800 MHz. State two equivalent ways of recovering the difference within the free-space model.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The 6.02 dB must be recovered. Either add 6.02 dB to the sum of transmit power and antenna gains (for example a transmitter with four times the power, or antennas whose combined gain is 6.02 dB higher), or halve the path length to 0.8 km, because halving the distance lowers the free-space loss by the same 6.02 dB that doubling the frequency added.

**詳解**　$L_{fs}$ depends on the product $d \cdot f$: doubling $f$ and halving $d$ leaves the product, and therefore the loss, unchanged. Otherwise $P_t + G_t + G_r$ must rise by $20\log_{10}(2) = 6.02$ dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S015**　A planner estimates the loss on the new carrier as follows. Step 1: the loss at 1800 MHz and 1.6 km is $32.45 + 4.0824 + 65.1055 = 101.64$ dB. Step 2: the frequency doubles, and doubling corresponds to $10\log_{10}(2) = 3.01$ dB. Step 3: the loss at 3600 MHz is $101.64 + 3.01 = 104.65$ dB. #### 情境題：Wavelength, effective aperture and far field

**Scenario | The same dishes on two frequency bands**

An operator compares a 7 GHz band and a 38 GHz band for a 5 km line-of-sight hop. In both cases the same transmitter power is used and both ends carry parabolic dishes of 0.6 m diameter with an aperture efficiency of 0.6, so that the gain is $G = \eta(\pi D/\lambda)^2$. Free-space propagation is assumed and $c = 3\times10^{8}$ m/s.

**3.1.2S031**　Compute the gain of one 0.6 m dish at 38 GHz in dBi, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　45.34 dBi

**詳解**　$\lambda = 3\times10^{8}/(38\times10^{9}) = 0.0078947$ m. $\pi D/\lambda = \pi \times 0.6/0.0078947 = 238.761$; squared: $57\,006.8$. $G = 0.6 \times 57\,006.8 = 34\,204.1$. $10\log_{10}(34\,204.1) = 45.3408$ dBi, which rounds to 45.34 dBi.

**出處**　C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design* (aperture antennas, gain of a reflector).

</details>

**3.1.2S032**　With the same dishes and the same transmitter power, by how many dB does the received power change when the hop is moved from 7 GHz to 38 GHz? Give the signed change (positive for an increase), rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　+14.69 dB

**詳解**　Free-space loss rises by $20\log_{10}(38/7) = 20\log_{10}(5.4286) = 14.6937$ dB. The gain of a fixed-diameter dish is proportional to $f^2$, so each dish gains 14.6937 dB (from 30.6471 dBi to 45.3408 dBi). Net change: $+14.6937 + 14.6937 - 14.6937 = +14.6937$ dB, which rounds to +14.69 dB.

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946; C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design*.

</details>

**3.1.2S033**　The free-space path loss is 14.69 dB higher at 38 GHz, yet the received power on this hop is higher at 38 GHz. Explain why there is no contradiction.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Free-space path loss is defined between isotropic antennas, whose effective aperture $\lambda^2/(4\pi)$ shrinks as the frequency rises; that is the whole origin of the frequency term. Here the physical aperture is held constant instead. A fixed dish intercepts the same power at any frequency (its gain rises as $f^2$ and cancels the loss increase on the receive side), while the transmit dish forms a narrower beam and concentrates more power on the receiver. The result is a net improvement proportional to $f^2$.

**詳解**　With fixed apertures $A_t$ and $A_r$, the Friis equation can be written $P_r = P_t A_t A_r/(\lambda^2 d^2)$, which increases as $f^2$.

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946 (aperture form of the transmission formula).

</details>

**3.1.2S034**　The free-space comparison favours 38 GHz. Name the effects outside the free-space formula that, in practice, make 38 GHz unsuitable for long hops even though it wins here.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Rain attenuation, which grows steeply with frequency and is of the order of several dB per km at 38 GHz in heavy rain; atmospheric gaseous absorption, which is also higher; and the much narrower beam of the same dish, which makes alignment and mast sway critical. None of these appears in the free-space formula, which only describes spreading loss in a clear, lossless medium, so higher bands are used for short hops and lower bands for long ones.

**詳解**　The free-space result is a clear-air baseline. A complete design adds rain attenuation (computed from the rain rate exceeded for the required availability), gaseous absorption and a fade margin to the free-space loss.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.530 (design of terrestrial line-of-sight systems); ITU-R Recommendation P.838 (specific attenuation model for rain); ITU-R Recommendation P.676 (attenuation by atmospheric gases).

</details>

**3.1.2S038**　What goes wrong if the laboratory nevertheless applies the Friis free-space equation, with the panel's far-field gain, to the 8 m measurement?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　At 8 m the probe is in the radiating near field (Fresnel region) of the panel. The contributions from different parts of the aperture arrive with phase differences larger than the far-field criterion allows, so the beam is not fully formed: the main lobe is broadened, nulls are filled in and the on-axis power density is lower than the far-field gain implies. The Friis equation, which assumes a distance-independent gain and $1/d^2$ decay, then predicts more received power than is measured, and the measured pattern is not the far-field pattern.

**詳解**　The criterion $2D^2/\lambda$ corresponds to a maximum phase error of $\pi/8$ (22.5°) across the aperture. At 8 m $< 10.83$ m the phase error exceeds this limit.

**出處**　C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design* (field regions).

</details>

**詳解**　(a) $2 \times 10.8333 = 21.6667$ m. (b) $4 \times 10.8333 = 43.3333$ m.

**出處**　C. A. Balanis, *Antenna Theory: Analysis and Design* (field regions).

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The free-space formula applies only while a clear line of sight exists. Beyond the horizon the path is diffracted, and the loss rises far above the free-space value. The practical range is therefore limited by geometry, not by the link budget.

**詳解**　The free-space result is an upper bound on range from power alone. A second, independent condition is line of sight; the smaller of the two distances governs.

**詳解**　$\text{EIRP} = 27\ \text{dBW} = 27 + 30 = 57$ dBm. $P_r = \text{EIRP} + G_r - L_{fs} = 57 + 3 - 183.0467 = -123.0467$ dBm, which rounds to −123.05 dBm.

**3.1.2S053**　When the same satellite is lower in the sky, the slant range grows to 25 000 km, 3500 km more than before. Is the additional loss large? Explain what determines it.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　No, it is only about 1.31 dB. In free space the change in loss depends on the ratio of the distances, not on their difference: $20\log_{10}(25\,000/21\,500) = 1.31$ dB. An extra 3500 km is small relative to a path that is already 21 500 km long, whereas the same 3500 km added to a short terrestrial path would be enormous.

**詳解**　$25\,000/21\,500 = 1.1628$; $20\log_{10}(1.1628) = 1.3100$ dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

**3.1.2S055**　The calculation uses the free-space formula for a path that crosses the Earth's atmosphere. Is that legitimate, and which further losses does a complete Earth–space budget add?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　It is legitimate as the baseline: almost all of the path is in vacuum and the spreading loss is the same with or without the atmosphere. The atmosphere is handled by separate additive terms, which the free-space formula does not contain: gaseous absorption, rain and cloud attenuation (small at 1.5 GHz, significant above about 10 GHz), ionospheric and tropospheric scintillation, plus polarization and pointing losses. The total loss is the free-space loss plus these terms.

**詳解**　$L_{\text{total}} = L_{fs} + L_{\text{gas}} + L_{\text{rain}} + L_{\text{other}}$, where only $L_{fs}$ follows from distance and frequency alone.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.618 (propagation data and prediction methods for Earth–space systems); ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

</details>

**3.1.2S057**　Compute the received power at the edge-of-footprint handset in dBm, rounded to two decimal places.

**3.1.2S058**　A colleague says: "If the satellite flew at 1200 km instead of 600 km, twice the EIRP would give the handset beneath it the same received power." Is this right?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　No. Doubling the distance spreads the power over four times the area, so the free-space loss rises by 6.02 dB and the EIRP must be four times larger (+6.02 dB), not twice (+3.01 dB). Twice the EIRP would leave the handset 3.01 dB short.

**詳解**　$20\log_{10}(1200/600) = 6.0206$ dB, a linear power factor of $10^{0.60206} = 4$.

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946.

**3.1.2S060**　For the edge-of-footprint handset, which distance belongs in the formula: the satellite altitude (600 km), the distance along the ground from the sub-satellite point, or the slant range (1500 km)? Why?

**Scenario | A 42 km long-haul hop at 6.2 GHz**

A long-haul microwave hop spans 42 km at 6.2 GHz. The transmitter output is 29 dBm, each end has a 1.8 m dish with 38.5 dBi gain, and waveguide and branching losses are 2 dB at each end. The receiver threshold is −72 dBm. Only free-space loss is considered on the path, with $L_{fs} = 92.45 + 20\log_{10} d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{GHz}}$.

**3.1.2S061**　Compute the received signal level in dBm, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　−38.76 dBm

**詳解**　$20\log_{10}(42) = 32.4650$ dB; $20\log_{10}(6.2) = 15.8478$ dB. $L_{fs} = 92.45 + 32.4650 + 15.8478 = 140.7628$ dB. $P_r = 29 + 38.5 + 38.5 - 2 - 2 - 140.7628 = -38.7628$ dBm, which rounds to −38.76 dBm.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.530 (design of terrestrial line-of-sight systems); ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S062**　Given a received signal level of −38.76 dBm, compute the fade margin relative to the receiver threshold in dB, to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　33.24 dB

**詳解**　Fade margin $= P_r - P_{\text{threshold}} = -38.76 - (-72) = 33.24$ dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.530.

</details>

**3.1.2S063**　The free-space budget leaves more than 33 dB in hand. Why is such a large margin normal for a hop of this length rather than a sign of over-design?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Free-space loss is only the loss in a calm, well-mixed atmosphere with full path clearance. On a 42 km hop at 6 GHz the received level fluctuates because of multipath fading caused by atmospheric layering and surface reflections, and deep fades of 20–40 dB occur for small fractions of time; changes in refractivity (the k-factor) can also reduce clearance and add diffraction loss. The fade margin is what keeps the link above threshold during those events, so availability targets require it.

**詳解**　Availability is set by the probability that fading exceeds the margin; that probability rises steeply with path length, which is why long hops carry margins of 30–40 dB.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.530 (multipath fading and path clearance).

</details>

**3.1.2S064**　A planner enters the hop into a tool by hand. Step 1: $20\log_{10}(42) = 32.46$ dB and $20\log_{10}(6200) = 75.85$ dB. Step 2: $L_{fs} = 92.45 + 32.46 + 75.85 = 200.76$ dB. Step 3: $P_r = 29 + 77 - 4 - 200.76 = -98.76$ dBm, below the threshold, so the hop is declared infeasible. Identify the wrong step, explain the error, and give the correct received level in dBm to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Step 1 is wrong: the frequency was entered in MHz (6200), but the constant 92.45 requires GHz (6.2). The loss is overstated by exactly 60 dB. The correct received level is −38.76 dBm, and the hop is feasible.

**詳解**　$20\log_{10}(6.2) = 15.8478$ dB. $L_{fs} = 92.45 + 32.4650 + 15.8478 = 140.7628$ dB. $P_r = 29 + 77 - 4 - 140.7628 = -38.7628$ dBm, which rounds to −38.76 dBm.

**出處**　ITU-R Recommendation P.525.

</details>

**3.1.2S065**　The free-space loss of this hop is 140.76 dB. Expressed as a linear fraction, how much of the radiated power would reach the far end between isotropic antennas, and what does that imply about the role of the dishes?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　About $8.4\times10^{-15}$ of the power, i.e. less than one part in $10^{14}$. Between isotropic antennas the 29 dBm transmitter (less 4 dB of waveguide loss) would deliver about −116 dBm, far below the −72 dBm threshold. The two dishes together contribute 77 dB, more than half of the free-space loss in dB, and are what make the hop workable: on long hops the link is closed by antenna gain, not by transmitter power.

**詳解**　$10^{-140.7628/10} = 8.389\times10^{-15}$. Without dish gain: $29 - 4 - 140.76 = -115.76$ dBm. With the dishes: $-115.76 + 77 = -38.76$ dBm.

**出處**　H. T. Friis, "A Note on a Simple Transmission Formula," Proc. IRE, 1946; ITU-R Recommendation P.530.
