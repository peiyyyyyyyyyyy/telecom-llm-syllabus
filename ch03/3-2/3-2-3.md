## 正文

In a practical radio access network the choice of modulation determines how many bits are packed into each transmitted symbol and how robust the link is against noise and interference. Engineers therefore need a quantitative link between a modulation format, the signal‑to‑noise ratio expressed as $E_b/N_0$ (energy per information bit over noise spectral density), and the resulting bit error rate (BER). This relationship is the cornerstone of link‑budget calculations, adaptive modulation and coding (MCS) selection, and ultimately the achievable spectral efficiency of a system.

---

### 1. Key definitions  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $M$ | Modulation order (number of distinct symbols) | – |
| $k=\log_2 M$ | Bits per symbol | bits/symbol |
| $E_s$ | Energy per transmitted symbol | joule (J) |
| $E_b$ | Energy per information bit, $E_b = E_s/k$ | joule (J) |
| $N_0$ | One‑sided noise power spectral density (AWGN) | watt/Hz (W/Hz) |
| $E_b/N_0$ | Energy‑per‑bit to noise‑spectral‑density ratio, a measure of link quality | dimensionless (often expressed in dB) |
| $\gamma$ | Instantaneous signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) per symbol, $\gamma = E_s/N_0$ | dimensionless |
| $Q(x)$ | Gaussian Q‑function, $Q(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_x^{\infty}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt$ | – |
| $\operatorname{erfc}(x)$ | Complementary error function, $\operatorname{erfc}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_x^{\infty}e^{-t^2}\,dt$ | – |
| $P_b$ | Bit error probability (BER) | – |
| $P_s$ | Symbol error probability (SER) | – |

The **Q‑function** describes the tail probability of a zero‑mean unit‑variance Gaussian random variable and is the standard way to express error probabilities for coherent detection in additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN).

---

### 2. BER formulas for common constellations  

#### 2.1 Phase‑Shift Keying (PSK)

*Binary PSK (BPSK, $M=2$)*  

$$
P_b^{\text{BPSK}} = Q\!\left(\sqrt{2\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\right) .
$$

*Coherent $M$‑ary PSK ($M\ge 4$)* – using the nearest‑neighbour approximation:

$$
P_s^{\text{PSK}} \approx 2\,Q\!\left(\sqrt{2\frac{E_s}{N_0}}\sin\!\frac{\pi}{M}\right) .
$$

The corresponding BER is obtained by dividing the SER by $k=\log_2 M$ (exact for Gray‑coded constellations):

$$
P_b^{\text{PSK}} \approx \frac{1}{k}\,P_s^{\text{PSK}} .
$$

#### 2.2 Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM)

For square $M$‑QAM ($M = L^2$, $L$ points per dimension) the SER in AWGN is approximated by

$$
P_s^{\text{QAM}} \approx 4\!\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\right)
Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{M-1}\,\frac{E_s}{N_0}}\right) .
$$

Again, with Gray coding the BER is

$$
P_b^{\text{QAM}} \approx \frac{P_s^{\text{QAM}}}{k} .
$$

Because $E_s = kE_b$, the expressions can be rewritten directly in terms of $E_b/N_0$.

---

### 3. Worked example  

**Problem:** A link uses coherent QPSK ($M=4$, $k=2$). The transmitter delivers $E_b/N_0 = 10\;\text{dB}$. Compute the theoretical BER.

**Solution steps**

1. Convert $E_b/N_0$ from dB to linear scale:  

$$
\frac{E_b}{N_0}\bigg|_{\text{lin}} = 10^{10/10}=10 .
$$

2. For QPSK the BER expression is identical to BPSK because the constellation points are orthogonal in the I/Q plane:  

$$
P_b = Q\!\left(\sqrt{2\frac{E_b}{N_0}}\right) .
$$

3. Compute the argument of the Q‑function:  

$$
\sqrt{2\frac{E_b}{N_0}} = \sqrt{2\times 10}= \sqrt{20}=4.4721 .
$$

4. Evaluate the Q‑function (using a table or calculator):  

$$
Q(4.4721) \approx 3.9\times 10^{-6}.
$$

**Result:** The predicted BER for QPSK at $E_b/N_0=10\,$dB is roughly $3.9\times10^{-6}$.

*Interpretation:* At this $E_b/N_0$ the link is extremely reliable; practical systems would typically target a BER of $10^{-5}$ or $10^{-6}$ before applying forward error correction (FEC).

---

### 4. Practical notes and common pitfalls  

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Correct practice |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| **Confusing $E_s/N_0$ with $E_b/N_0$** | $E_s = kE_b$; forgetting the factor $k$ leads to a 3‑dB error for 16‑QAM ($k=4$). | Always convert: $E_s/N_0 = k\,E_b/N_0$. |
| **Using the SER formula for BER without Gray coding** | Non‑Gray mappings double the BER for many symbols. | Verify that the modulation uses Gray coding; otherwise apply the exact mapping error probabilities. |
| **Applying the nearest‑neighbour approximation at low $M$** | For BPSK and QPSK the exact BER is $Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$, not the approximated SER‑to‑BER conversion. | Use the exact BPSK/QPSK formula; the approximation is only needed for $M\ge 8$. |
| **Neglecting the effect of channel coding** | The raw BER is only part of the link budget; strong FEC can reduce the post‑decoding BER by orders of magnitude. | Compute the uncoded BER first, then apply the coding gain specified by the chosen MCS. |
| **Assuming the Q‑function is linear in dB** | $Q(\cdot)$ is exponential; a 1‑dB change can produce a large BER swing. | Use the exact $Q$ expression or a reliable lookup table when performing link‑budget sweeps. |

Remember that the formulas above assume an **AWGN channel** and **coherent detection**. In frequency‑selective fading, multipath, or non‑coherent receivers the effective $E_b/N_0$ must be replaced by an appropriate *effective* SNR (e.g., the post‑combining SNR of an OFDM subcarrier). Nevertheless, the Q‑function‑based expressions remain the reference point for system design and for calibrating simulation models.
