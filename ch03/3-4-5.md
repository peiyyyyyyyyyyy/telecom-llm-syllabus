# 3.4.5 延遲與吞吐量分析

所屬：[3.4 訊務與存取](3-4.md)

## 內文

#### 為什麼要學

延遲和吞吐量是用戶最直接感受到的兩個指標，也是服務等級協議與 KPI 的主角。一個封包從送出到收到，時間花在四個地方：節點處理、排隊等待、把位元送上鏈路、在媒介中傳播。分清楚這四項，才能判斷問題出在哪裡：光纖太長（傳播）、鏈路太慢（傳輸）、流量太滿（排隊），還是設備運算（處理）。前傳／中傳／後傳的距離限制、邊緣運算的部署位置、衛星鏈路的延遲、傳輸層視窗該開多大，都靠這套分解來估算。

#### 定義與符號

| 符號 | 意思 | 單位 |
|---|---|---|
| $d_{\text{proc}}$ | 處理延遲：檢查標頭、查表、編解碼等 | 秒 |
| $d_{\text{queue}}$ | 排隊延遲：在緩衝區等待輪到傳送 | 秒 |
| $d_{\text{trans}}$ | 傳輸延遲：把整個封包的位元推上鏈路 | 秒 |
| $d_{\text{prop}}$ | 傳播延遲：訊號在媒介中走完距離 | 秒 |
| $L$ | 封包長度 | bit |
| $R$ | 鏈路速率 | bit/s |
| $d$ | 鏈路實體長度 | m |
| $v$ | 訊號在媒介中的傳播速度 | m/s |
| RTT | 來回時間（round-trip time） | 秒 |
| BDP | 頻寬延遲積（bandwidth-delay product） | bit |
| $a$ | 傳播延遲與傳輸延遲的比值 | 無因次 |

#### 公式與推導

單一節點（一跳）的延遲是四項相加：

$$d_{\text{nodal}}=d_{\text{proc}}+d_{\text{queue}}+d_{\text{trans}}+d_{\text{prop}}$$

**傳輸延遲**只和封包大小與鏈路速率有關，和距離無關：

$$d_{\text{trans}}=\frac{L}{R}$$

**傳播延遲**只和距離與媒介有關，和封包大小、鏈路速率無關：

$$d_{\text{prop}}=\frac{d}{v}$$

真空中 $v=c=299{,}792{,}458$ m/s，無線電波在空氣中可視為相同（算例常取 $3\times10^8$ m/s）。光纖中的速度是 $c$ 除以纖芯的折射率；折射率約 1.5，所以**常用近似值是 $v\approx2\times10^8$ m/s**（約為 $c$ 的三分之二），相當於每公里約 5 μs。這是估算用的近似，實際值隨光纖種類與波長略有不同。

**排隊延遲**隨流量變動，是四項中唯一有明顯隨機性的。若可用 M/M/1 近似，$\mu=R/L$（每秒可送出的封包數），$\rho=\lambda/\mu<1$，則平均排隊延遲 $W_q=\rho/(\mu-\lambda)$（見 3.4.3）。**處理延遲**由設備決定，通常由量測或規格給定。

封包經過多跳時，端到端延遲是各跳的總和。在儲存轉送（store-and-forward）網路中，每一跳都要收完整個封包才轉送，所以每一跳都要再付一次傳輸延遲。

**吞吐量**　穩態下，端到端的吞吐量受最慢的那一段限制：

$$\text{吞吐量}\le\min(R_1,R_2,\dots,R_n)$$

這一段稱為瓶頸鏈路（bottleneck link）。

**頻寬延遲積**　

$$\text{BDP}=R\times\text{RTT}$$

單位是 bit/s 乘以秒，結果是 **bit**（除以 8 才是 byte）。它的意義是「鏈路上同時在飛、尚未被確認的資料量上限」。使用視窗式流量控制的傳輸協定，視窗至少要有 BDP 這麼大才能把鏈路填滿；視窗小於 BDP 時，吞吐量約為「視窗大小÷RTT」。有些文獻用單程延遲定義，使用時要先確認是哪一種。

**延遲比 $a$**　$a=d_{\text{prop}}/d_{\text{trans}}$。$a\ll1$ 表示封包的頭已經到對端、尾巴還沒送完；$a\gg1$ 表示鏈路上可以同時裝下很多個封包。對停等式（stop-and-wait）協定，在無錯誤、確認封包長度與處理時間可忽略的假設下，鏈路利用率為

$$U=\frac{1}{1+2a}$$

#### 算例

**算例 1：一跳的四種延遲**

已知：封包 1,500 byte，鏈路速率 100 Mbit/s，光纖長 20 km（取 $v=2\times10^8$ m/s），處理延遲 50 μs（假設值）；封包平均到達率 5,000 個/秒，用 M/M/1 近似排隊。求總延遲。

- 換單位：$L=1500\times8=12{,}000$ bit。
- 傳輸：$d_{\text{trans}}=12000/(100\times10^6)=1.2\times10^{-4}$ 秒＝120 μs。
- 傳播：$d_{\text{prop}}=20\times10^3/(2\times10^8)=1.0\times10^{-4}$ 秒＝100 μs。
- 排隊：$\mu=100\times10^6/12000=8333.3$ 個/秒；$\rho=5000/8333.3=0.6<1$；$W_q=0.6/(8333.3-5000)=0.6/3333.3=1.8\times10^{-4}$ 秒＝180 μs。
- 總和：$50+180+120+100=450$ μs。
- 合理性檢查：M/M/1 的停留時間 $W=1/(\mu-\lambda)=300$ μs，等於排隊 180 μs 加傳輸 120 μs，一致。把鏈路升級到 1 Gbit/s，傳輸延遲降為 12 μs、排隊延遲也大幅下降，但傳播延遲 100 μs 不變；要縮短傳播延遲只能縮短距離。
- 延伸：$a=100/120=0.8333$，停等式協定的利用率 $U=1/(1+2\times0.8333)=1/2.6667=0.375$。

**算例 2：頻寬延遲積**

已知：瓶頸鏈路 1 Gbit/s，RTT＝40 ms（假設值）。求 BDP，並換成 byte 與 1,500 byte 封包數。

- $\text{BDP}=1\times10^9\times0.04=4\times10^7$ bit。
- 換成 byte：$4\times10^7/8=5\times10^6$ byte＝5 MB（以 $10^6$ 計）。
- 封包數：$4\times10^7/12000=3333.3$，約 3,333 個。
- 合理性檢查：若傳輸層視窗只有 65,535 byte，吞吐量上限約 $65535\times8/0.04=1.31\times10^7$ bit/s，只有 13.1 Mbit/s，遠低於 1 Gbit/s；可見長延遲、高速率的路徑需要大視窗。

另外，2,000 km 光纖的單程傳播延遲為 $2\times10^6/(2\times10^8)=0.01$ 秒＝10 ms，來回 20 ms；這是任何設備升級都消不掉的下限。

#### 常見錯誤

1. **傳輸延遲與傳播延遲混淆**：傳輸延遲是 $L/R$，和距離無關；傳播延遲是 $d/v$，和速率無關。提高鏈路速率不會讓訊號跑得更快。
2. **bit 與 byte 沒換算**：封包長度給 byte、速率給 bit/s，要先乘 8。BDP 的結果是 bit。
3. **光纖裡用 $3\times10^8$ m/s**：光纖中的速度約為 $2\times10^8$ m/s，用真空光速會低估延遲約三分之一。
4. **RTT 與單程延遲混用**：BDP、視窗計算用的是來回時間；只給單程延遲時，對稱路徑要乘 2。

#### 和其他小節的關係

排隊延遲直接使用 3.4.3 的 $W_q$；傳輸延遲 $L/R$ 就是該節的平均服務時間 $1/\mu$。鏈路速率 $R$ 的上限來自 3.2 的容量與調變。3.4.4 的碰撞重傳會在本節的延遲之外再加上退避等待時間。

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, *Data Networks*，延遲模型與資料鏈路控制（停等式協定）的章節；Kleinrock, *Queueing Systems, Volume 2: Computer Applications*，電腦通訊網路的延遲分析；IETF RFC 7323〈TCP Extensions for High Performance〉（說明高頻寬延遲積路徑需要視窗縮放）。

**3.4.5G001**　A data-centre switch sends a 9000-byte jumbo Ethernet frame onto a 10 Gbit/s port. Taking 1 byte = 8 bits and ignoring preamble and inter-frame gap, determine the transmission delay of the frame, in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

**答案**　7.20

**詳解**　$L = 9000 \times 8 = 72{,}000$ bit. $d_{trans} = L/R = 72000 / (10 \times 10^9) = 7.2 \times 10^{-6}$ s $= 7.20$ µs.
\boxed{7.20}

**3.4.5G002**　A 1500-byte packet is sent over an 85 km single-hop fibre link running at 1 Gbit/s. Take the propagation speed in the fibre as $2 \times 10^8$ m/s and 1 byte = 8 bits. Ignoring processing and queueing, determine the time from the moment the first bit is put on the link until the last bit is received at the far end, in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　437.00

**詳解**　Transmission: $d_{trans} = 1500 \times 8 / 10^9 = 12{,}000/10^9 = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ s $= 12$ µs. Propagation: $d_{prop} = 85{,}000 / (2 \times 10^8) = 4.25 \times 10^{-4}$ s $= 425$ µs. The last bit leaves after 12 µs and arrives 425 µs later: total $= 12 + 425 = 437.00$ µs.
\boxed{437.00}

#### 單題：Store-and-forward end-to-end delay

**3.4.5G006**　A 1500-byte packet crosses three links in series between two hosts, through two store-and-forward routers. Every link runs at 1 Gbit/s and is a 10 km fibre with propagation speed $2 \times 10^8$ m/s. Each router adds 20 µs of processing delay, there is no queueing, and 1 byte = 8 bits. Determine the end-to-end one-way delay, in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　226.00

**詳解**　Per link: $d_{trans} = 12{,}000/10^9 = 12$ µs and $d_{prop} = 10{,}000/(2 \times 10^8) = 50$ µs. Store-and-forward means the packet is transmitted once on every link: $3 \times (12 + 50) = 186$ µs. Processing at two routers: $2 \times 20 = 40$ µs. Total $= 186 + 40 = 226.00$ µs.
\boxed{226.00}

All links are fibre with propagation speed $2 \times 10^8$ m/s. Ignore processing and queueing and take 1 byte = 8 bits. Determine the end-to-end delay, in milliseconds, rounded to three decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　2.271

\boxed{2.271}

#### 單題：Queueing delay inside the nodal delay

**3.4.5G011**　A router output port runs at 50 Mbit/s. Packets arrive as a Poisson process at 4500 packets/s, and packet lengths are exponentially distributed with mean 1000 bytes (1 byte = 8 bits), so the port can be modelled as M/M/1. Determine the mean queueing delay $W_q$ (waiting time before transmission starts), in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　411.43

**詳解**　$\mu = R/L = 50 \times 10^6 / 8000 = 6250$ packets/s. $\rho = 4500/6250 = 0.72$. $W_q = \rho/(\mu - \lambda) = 0.72/(6250 - 4500) = 0.72/1750 = 4.1143 \times 10^{-4}$ s $= 411.43$ µs.
\boxed{411.43}

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, Data Networks (M/M/1 queue); Kleinrock, Queueing Systems, Vol. 1

</details>

**3.4.5G012**　A 20 Mbit/s link leaves a router; packets arrive as a Poisson process at 3000 packets/s with exponentially distributed lengths of mean 500 bytes (1 byte = 8 bits). The router's processing delay is 30 µs per packet, and the link is a 12 km fibre with propagation speed $2 \times 10^8$ m/s. Model the output port as M/M/1, whose mean time in system $1/(\mu-\lambda)$ includes both waiting and transmission. Determine the mean nodal delay (processing + queueing + transmission + propagation), in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　590.00

**詳解**　$\mu = 20 \times 10^6/4000 = 5000$ packets/s; $\rho = 3000/5000 = 0.6$. Queueing + transmission: $W = 1/(5000 - 3000) = 5 \times 10^{-4}$ s $= 500$ µs (of which $1/\mu = 200$ µs is transmission). Propagation: $12{,}000/(2 \times 10^8) = 60$ µs. Total $= 30 + 500 + 60 = 590.00$ µs.
\boxed{590.00}

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, Data Networks (delay components; M/M/1 system time)

**3.4.5G013**　A 100 Mbit/s link carries packets with exponentially distributed lengths of mean 1250 bytes (1 byte = 8 bits) arriving as a Poisson process (M/M/1). The operator requires the mean queueing delay $W_q$ (waiting time, excluding transmission) to stay at or below 400 µs. Determine the largest offered load that meets this target, in Mbit/s, rounded to two decimal places.

\boxed{80.00}

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, Data Networks (M/M/1 queue)

#### 單題：Bandwidth–delay product

**3.4.5G016**　A 10 Gbit/s path has a round-trip time of 25 ms. Using BDP = rate × RTT and 1 MB = $10^6$ bytes (1 byte = 8 bits), determine the bandwidth–delay product in MB, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　31.25

**詳解**　$\text{BDP} = 10 \times 10^9 \times 0.025 = 2.5 \times 10^8$ bit. In bytes: $2.5 \times 10^8 / 8 = 3.125 \times 10^7$ bytes $= 31.25$ MB.

**3.4.5G017**　A 100 Gbit/s wavelength runs over a 6600 km submarine fibre route; the propagation speed in the fibre is $2 \times 10^8$ m/s and the return path has the same length. **出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (transmission delay $L/R$)

#### 情境題：Multi-hop end-to-end delay

**Scenario | Metro path across an aggregation ring**

A 1500-byte packet (1 byte = 8 bits) travels from a cell-site router to a core router over four store-and-forward links: 1 Gbit/s over 5 km, 10 Gbit/s over 40 km, 10 Gbit/s over 60 km and 1 Gbit/s over 3 km. <details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　641.40 µs

</details>

**3.4.5S012**　What percentage of the end-to-end delay is propagation delay? Give the answer in percent, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　84.19 %

**詳解**　$540/641.4 = 0.84191 = 84.19$ %.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (end-to-end delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S013**　If the packet were a 64-byte voice or control packet instead of 1500 bytes, which delay components would change? Would the end-to-end delay change much?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Only the transmission delays shrink (26.4 µs to about 1.1 µs); propagation and processing stay at 615 µs, so the total drops only by about 4 %.

**詳解**　Transmission delay scales with packet length: $64 \times 8 = 512$ bit gives 0.512 µs per 1 Gbit/s link and 0.0512 µs per 10 Gbit/s link, about 1.13 µs in total. Propagation (540 µs) depends on distance only, and the processing delay here is given per packet (75 µs). The total becomes about 616 µs instead of 641 µs. On this path, distance dominates.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (transmission vs propagation delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S014**　Why does the packet pay a transmission delay on every link in this path, and what would change if the routers used cut-through forwarding?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　A store-and-forward router must receive the whole packet before it can check and forward it, so the full $L/R$ is paid on every link; cut-through starts forwarding after the header, so the per-hop transmission is replaced by a short header delay (only when output and input rates allow it).

**詳解**　In store-and-forward, the packet is fully buffered and checked (for example the frame check sequence) before it is sent on, so each link adds its own $L/R_i$. A cut-through switch begins transmitting on the output as soon as the destination is read from the header, so the packet's bits stream through and only one full transmission delay (on the slowest link) plus small header delays remain. Cut-through is possible only when the output is free and not faster than the input, and errored frames are forwarded before they can be detected.

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, Data Networks (store-and-forward and cut-through switching)

</details>

**Scenario | Delivering a video segment over three hops**

**答案**　240.24 ms

**詳解**　Packet time $= 12{,}000/10^8 = 120$ µs. The first packet arrives after $3 \times 120 = 360$ µs; the other 1999 follow one every 120 µs. Total $= (3 + 2000 - 1) \times 120 = 2002 \times 120 = 240{,}240$ µs $= 240.24$ ms.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　720.00 ms

**詳解**　Per link $= 3 \times 10^6 \times 8/10^8 = 0.24$ s $= 240$ ms. Three links $= 3 \times 240 = 720.00$ ms.

**3.4.5S018**　Why does cutting the segment into packets reduce the delivery time from 720 ms to about 240 ms, although the total number of bits sent per link is the same?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Because of pipelining: with packets, all three links work at the same time on different packets, so only the first packet pays the per-hop delay on every link; with one large unit, each link sits idle until the previous link has finished.

**詳解**　With segmentation, while link 2 forwards packet $k$, link 1 already sends packet $k+1$. The total time becomes roughly (time to send the whole segment once) + (number of extra hops × one packet time) $= 240 + 2 \times 0.12$ ms. With a single unit, the three 240 ms transmissions happen one after the other.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (message segmentation)

</details>

**3.4.5S019**　Following this logic, an engineer suggests using 40-byte packets to cut delay further. Why is this a bad idea in practice?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Each packet carries a fixed header and costs per-packet processing; with very small packets the header overhead and per-packet processing grow while the pipelining gain (already only 0.24 ms here) becomes negligible.

**詳解**　With 1500-byte packets the pipelining penalty is already only $2 \times 0.12 = 0.24$ ms over the 240 ms minimum. A typical IP/TCP header of 40 bytes would make a 40-byte packet carry no payload at all; even 100-byte payloads would add 40 % overhead and multiply the number of packets each router must look up and forward. Packet size is a trade-off between pipelining gain, header overhead and per-packet processing.

**出處**　Bertsekas & Gallager, Data Networks (packet length and overhead trade-off)

#### 情境題：Bandwidth–delay product and windows

**Scenario | GEO satellite backhaul for a remote cell site**

A remote cell site is backhauled through a bent-pipe geostationary satellite. The slant range from the site to the satellite is 37,500 km and from the satellite to the gateway is 36,500 km; radio waves travel at $3 \times 10^8$ m/s. The link runs at 20 Mbit/s in each direction. Ground and on-board processing are neglected; 1 byte = 8 bits, 1 MB = $10^6$ bytes.

**3.4.5S031**　Using BDP = rate × RTT, with RTT from propagation only (site → satellite → gateway and back), determine the bandwidth–delay product in MB, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　1.23 MB

**詳解**　One-way path $= 37{,}500 + 36{,}500 = 74{,}000$ km. One-way delay $= 7.4 \times 10^7/(3 \times 10^8) = 0.24667$ s. RTT $= 0.49333$ s. BDP $= 2 \times 10^7 \times 0.49333 = 9.8667 \times 10^6$ bit $= 1.2333 \times 10^6$ bytes $= 1.23$ MB.

</details>

**3.4.5S032**　Determine the one-way delay of a 1500-byte packet from the cell site to the gateway, from the first bit sent to the last bit received, in milliseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　247.27 ms

**詳解**　Transmission $= 12{,}000/(2 \times 10^7) = 0.6$ ms. Propagation $= 246.667$ ms (bent pipe: no second transmission). Total $= 246.667 + 0.6 = 247.27$ ms.

</details>

**3.4.5S033**　The operator considers a LEO constellation with about 20 ms RTT at the same 20 Mbit/s. How do the BDP and the dominant delay component change?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　The BDP falls in proportion to the RTT (about 25 times smaller, roughly 0.05 MB), and propagation stops dominating so strongly; transmission, processing and queueing become relatively more important.

**詳解**　BDP $= R \times \text{RTT}$: $2 \times 10^7 \times 0.02 = 4 \times 10^5$ bit $= 0.05$ MB, compared with 1.23 MB. A smaller BDP means default TCP windows can fill the link and loss recovery is faster. Propagation still exists but is much smaller, so on-board switching, inter-satellite routing and queueing delays become a visible share of the total.

**出處**　IETF RFC 7323, TCP Extensions for High Performance (bandwidth–delay product); Maral & Bousquet, Satellite Communications Systems

**3.4.5S034**　Why does a new TCP connection take a long time to reach 20 Mbit/s over this GEO link even when the window limit is large enough?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　TCP slow start grows the congestion window by roughly a factor of two per RTT, and each RTT is about 0.49 s, so reaching a window of 1.23 MB from a few segments takes many seconds.

**詳解**　Starting from a small initial window (a few segments of about 1.5 kB), the window must double about $\log_2(1.23 \times 10^6/(10 \times 1500)) \approx 6.4$ times, i.e. about 7 round trips, roughly 3.5 s, before the link is full. **3.4.5S043**　With a 124-frame window, how many sequence-number bits are needed for Go-Back-N and for Selective Repeat? Explain the difference.

#### 情境題：Bottleneck throughput

**Scenario | Microwave chain versus fibre route for backhaul**

An operator compares two backhaul routes between a hub site and an aggregation node. Route A is a microwave chain of 4 hops, 120 km in total (radio propagation speed $3 \times 10^8$ m/s), where each hop adds 60 µs of modem and switching delay. Route B is a fibre route of 165 km (propagation speed $2 \times 10^8$ m/s) with optical amplifiers only, whose delay is negligible. Packet transmission delays are ignored for this comparison.

**3.4.5S051**　Determine how much lower the one-way delay of the microwave route is than that of the fibre route, in microseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　185.00 µs

**詳解**　Route A: propagation $= 1.2 \times 10^5/(3 \times 10^8) = 400$ µs; equipment $= 4 \times 60 = 240$ µs; total $= 640$ µs. Route B: $1.65 \times 10^5/(2 \times 10^8) = 825$ µs. Difference $= 825 - 640 = 185.00$ µs.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (propagation delay); radio propagation at the speed of light

</details>

**3.4.5S052**　Why does radio over air travel faster per kilometre than light in fibre, and roughly by how much?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　In air the refractive index is about 1, so radio waves travel at nearly $c$ (about 3.3 µs/km); in fibre the group index is about 1.5, so light travels at about $2 \times 10^8$ m/s (about 5 µs/km), roughly 1.5 times slower.

**詳解**　Propagation speed is $c/n$. Air has $n \approx 1.0003$, glass about 1.47, so fibre delay is about 50 % larger per kilometre. In addition, radio paths are close to straight lines while fibre follows roads and ducts, which usually makes fibre routes longer. This is why latency-sensitive links (for example between trading venues) sometimes use microwave.

**出處**　Agrawal, Fiber-Optic Communication Systems (group index of silica fibre)

</details>

**3.4.5S053**　The microwave chain runs at 1 Gbit/s and the fibre at 10 Gbit/s. Which route is better for a bulk transfer of many gigabytes, and which for a small latency-critical control message? Explain.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Fibre for bulk transfer (throughput is set by the 10 Gbit/s rate); microwave for a small control message (its delay is dominated by propagation and equipment delay, where the microwave route is 185 µs faster).

**詳解**　For bulk data, transfer time ≈ size/rate, so the 10 Gbit/s fibre finishes about ten times sooner; the 185 µs propagation difference is negligible. For a small message, transmission time is tiny on both (a 100-byte message takes 0.8 µs at 1 Gbit/s), so the end-to-end delay is decided by propagation and equipment delay, where the microwave route wins. Latency and throughput are separate properties.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (delay vs throughput)

</details>

#### 情境題：Latency budgets and measurement

**Scenario | Placing a server for an AR application**

An augmented-reality application needs the network round trip (excluding rendering) to stay within 20 ms. The radio access contributes 8 ms of round-trip delay, and transport and core equipment (processing and queueing, both directions) contribute 3 ms. The rest of the budget is available for round-trip fibre propagation at $2 \times 10^8$ m/s over a route of the same length in each direction. The current server is in a central data centre with a fibre route length of 1200 km.

**3.4.5S056**　Determine the maximum fibre route length (one way) from the users to the server that meets the budget, in km, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　900.00 km

**詳解**　Propagation budget (round trip) $= 20 - 8 - 3 = 9$ ms. One way $= 4.5$ ms. $d = 4.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^8 = 9 \times 10^5$ m $= 900.00$ km.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (propagation delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S057**　Moving the server to an edge site 40 km away saves most of the propagation delay. Why can it not bring the network round trip below about 11 ms?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Because 8 ms of the round trip is in the radio access and 3 ms in equipment processing and queueing; server placement only reduces the fibre propagation part.

**詳解**　With the edge site, round-trip propagation $= 2 \times 40{,}000/(2 \times 10^8) = 0.4$ ms, so the total becomes $8 + 3 + 0.4 = 11.4$ ms. To go lower, the radio part (scheduling, HARQ, frame structure) or the equipment delays must be reduced; distance is no longer the main term.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (delay components)

</details>

**3.4.5S058**　The 900 km limit is a fibre route length. Why can a data centre whose straight-line distance is 700 km still fail the budget?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Because fibre follows roads, railways and ducts, and the route can be considerably longer than the straight line; the delay is set by the actual route length (and any detours via other cities), which may exceed 900 km.

**詳解**　Propagation delay depends on the length of the physical path. Terrestrial fibre routes are typically longer than the geodesic distance, sometimes by a large factor where the network detours through hubs. A 700 km straight-line distance can therefore correspond to more than 900 km of fibre, i.e. more than 9 ms of round-trip propagation.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (propagation delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S059**　A planner computes the reach:
1. Budget left for propagation $= 20 - 8 = 12$ ms (round trip).
2. One way $= 6$ ms.
3. $d = 6 \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^8 = 1200$ km, so the central data centre is acceptable.

Which step is wrong, and what is the correct reach?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Step 1 is wrong: it omits the 3 ms of transport and core equipment delay. The propagation budget is 9 ms round trip, the reach is 900 km, and the 1200 km data centre fails.

**詳解**　$20 - 8 - 3 = 9$ ms; one way 4.5 ms; $d = 4.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^8 = 900$ km $< 1200$ km.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (end-to-end delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S060**　Another planner checks the central data centre directly:
1. One-way propagation $= 1.2 \times 10^6/(2 \times 10^8) = 6$ ms.
2. Network round trip $= 8 + 3 + 6 = 17$ ms.
3. $17 < 20$, so the central data centre is acceptable.

Which step is wrong, and what is the correct round trip?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Step 2 is wrong: the propagation must be counted in both directions (12 ms), because the budget is a round trip. Correct round trip: 23 ms, which exceeds 20 ms.

**詳解**　Round-trip propagation $= 2 \times 6 = 12$ ms. Total $= 8 + 3 + 12 = 23$ ms $> 20$ ms, so the central data centre fails.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (round-trip time)

**Scenario | Decomposing a ping measurement**

A monitoring probe pings a server whose fibre route is 300 km long in each direction (propagation speed $2 \times 10^8$ m/s). All links on the path are at least 1 Gbit/s and the ICMP packets are 64 bytes. Over one hour, the minimum RTT measured is 3.4 ms and the average RTT is 5.9 ms.

**3.4.5S061**　Taking the minimum RTT as the delay without queueing, estimate the average round-trip queueing delay, in milliseconds, rounded to two decimal places.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　2.50 ms

**詳解**　Average queueing (round trip) ≈ average RTT − minimum RTT $= 5.9 - 3.4 = 2.50$ ms.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (queueing delay; traceroute/ping measurements)

</details>

**3.4.5S062**　Why is the minimum RTT, rather than the average, used as the estimate of the fixed (non-queueing) part of the delay?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Because propagation, transmission and processing are (nearly) the same for every ping, while queueing varies and is never negative; the smallest sample is the one that met the least queueing, so it approaches the fixed part.

**詳解**　RTT = fixed part + queueing, with queueing ≥ 0 and random. Over many samples some packets find almost empty queues, so the minimum converges to the fixed part. The average includes the mean queueing, and the difference average − minimum estimates it.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (queueing delay variability)

</details>

**3.4.5S063**　The probe repeats the test with 1500-byte packets. Would the minimum RTT change noticeably here, and what would the slope of minimum RTT versus packet size tell you on a slow access link?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Hardly: at ≥ 1 Gbit/s a 1500-byte packet adds only about 12 µs per link per direction. On a slow link the slope of minimum RTT versus packet size equals the sum of $1/R$ over the store-and-forward links in both directions, which reveals the link rates.

**詳解**　Transmission delay $L/R$ is the only fixed term that depends on size. At 1 Gbit/s, 1500 bytes take 12 µs per link, small compared with the 3 ms propagation. On a 2 Mbit/s access link, the same packet takes 6 ms each way, so the minimum RTT rises clearly with size; tools such as pathchar use exactly this slope to estimate per-link rates.

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (transmission delay)

</details>

**3.4.5S064**　An analyst bounds the server distance from the measurement:
1. Minimum RTT $= 3.4$ ms.
2. Maximum distance $= 3.4 \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^8 = 680$ km.
3. The server cannot be more than 680 km (route length) away.

Which step is wrong, and what is the correct bound?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Step 2 is wrong: the RTT covers the path twice, so the one-way time is 1.7 ms. Correct upper bound: 340 km of fibre route.

**詳解**　One-way bound $= 3.4/2 = 1.7$ ms. $d \le 1.7 \times 10^{-3} \times 2 \times 10^8 = 340$ km. (This is consistent with the 300 km route; the remaining 0.4 ms is processing and transmission.)

**出處**　Kurose & Ross, Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach (round-trip time)

</details>

**3.4.5S065**　An analyst estimates the processing delay along the path:
1. Round-trip propagation $= 2 \times 300{,}000/(3 \times 10^8) = 2$ ms.
2. Fixed delay not due to propagation $= 3.4 - 2 = 1.4$ ms.
3. Processing and transmission add 1.4 ms per round trip.

Which step is wrong, and what is the correct value?

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

**答案**　Step 1 is wrong: the scenario states $2 \times 10^8$ m/s in fibre, not $3 \times 10^8$ m/s. Round-trip propagation is 3 ms, so processing and transmission add 0.40 ms.

**詳解**　Round-trip propagation $= 6 \times 10^5/(2 \times 10^8) = 3$ ms. Remaining fixed delay $= 3.4 - 3 = 0.40$ ms.
