## 正文

In modern wireless and wired networks engineers constantly exchange power levels, gains, and losses across many devices – transmitters, amplifiers, cables, antennas, and receivers. Because these quantities can span many orders of magnitude (from femtowatts at a receiver front‑end to kilowatts at a base‑station transmitter), a logarithmic representation is indispensable. The *decibel* (dB) provides a compact, additive way to describe ratios of power, voltage, or current, and it is the lingua‑franca of link‑budget calculations, path‑loss models, and RF component specifications.

---

### 1. Definition of the decibel

For a **power ratio** $R_P = \dfrac{P_2}{P_1}$

$$
L_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(R_P\right) \quad\text{[dB]}
\tag{1}
$$

where  

* $P_1, P_2$ are powers expressed in the **same** unit (W, mW, …).  
* The base‑10 logarithm is used; the factor 10 follows from the definition of the *bel* (1 B = 10 dB).

For a **voltage or current ratio** $R_V = \dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$ (or $I_2/I_1$) across a **constant impedance** $Z$,

$$
L_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(R_V\right) \quad\text{[dB]}
\tag{2}
$$

because power is proportional to the square of voltage (or current): $P \propto V^{2}$. Equation (2) is therefore valid only when the two voltages are measured across the same resistance (most RF work assumes a reference impedance of 50 Ω or 75 Ω).

---

### 2. Reference‑level decibel units

The decibel itself is dimensionless; to give an absolute level we attach a **reference power** (or voltage). The most common references are:

| Symbol | Reference | Meaning |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **dBm** | $P_{\text{ref}} = 1\ \text{mW}$ | Power expressed relative to 1 mW. |
| **dBW** | $P_{\text{ref}} = 1\ \text{W}$ | Power expressed relative to 1 W. |
| **dBi** | $G_{\text{ref}} = 1$ (isotropic radiator) | Antenna gain relative to an isotropic antenna. |
| **dBd** | $G_{\text{ref}} = 2.15\ \text{dBi}$ | Antenna gain relative to a half‑wave dipole. |

Conversion between linear power (W) and dBm:

$$
P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{W}}}{1\ \text{mW}}\right)
               = 10\log_{10}\!\left(P_{\text{W}}\times10^{3}\right)
\tag{3}
$$

$$
P_{\text{W}} = 10^{\frac{P_{\text{dBm}}}{10}} \times 10^{-3}
\tag{4}
$$

Similarly, dBW and watts:

$$
P_{\text{dBW}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(P_{\text{W}}\right)
\qquad
P_{\text{W}} = 10^{\frac{P_{\text{dBW}}}{10}}
\tag{5}
$$

The relationship between dBm and dBW follows directly from the 30‑dB difference between 1 W and 1 mW:

$$
P_{\text{dBW}} = P_{\text{dBm}} - 30
\qquad
P_{\text{dBm}} = P_{\text{dBW}} + 30
\tag{6}
$$

---

### 3. Adding gains and losses – the link‑budget rule

Because the decibel converts multiplication into addition, a cascade of components can be handled by **summing** their individual dB values:

$$
L_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i} L_i
\tag{7}
$$

where each $L_i$ is positive for a gain (e.g., an amplifier) and negative for a loss (e.g., a cable). This rule underlies the classic link‑budget equation:

$$
P_{\text{rx}}(\text{dBm}) = P_{\text{tx}}(\text{dBm}) 
                           + G_{\text{tx}}(\text{dBi}) 
                           - L_{\text{path}}(\text{dB}) 
                           - L_{\text{cable}}(\text{dB}) 
                           + G_{\text{rx}}(\text{dBi}) 
                           - L_{\text{misc}}(\text{dB})
\tag{8}
$$

All terms must be expressed in the same reference (dBm for power, dBi for antenna gain, dB for pure ratios).

---

### 4. Worked example – a simple link budget

A small‑cell base station transmits with **30 dBm** output power. The RF feed line introduces a **3 dB** loss, and the antenna has a **15 dBi** gain. Compute:

1. Power delivered to the antenna terminals (in dBm and watts).  
2. Effective isotropic radiated power (EIRP) in dBm.

**Solution**

1. **Power at antenna input**  

   $$
   P_{\text{ant}} = P_{\text{tx}} - L_{\text{feed}} 
                  = 30\ \text{dBm} - 3\ \text{dB} = 27\ \text{dBm}
   $$

   Convert 27 dBm to watts using (4):

   $$
   P_{\text{W}} = 10^{\frac{27}{10}} \times 10^{-3}
               = 10^{2.7} \times 10^{-3}
               \approx 501.2 \times 10^{-3}
               = 0.501\ \text{W}
   $$

2. **EIRP**  

   $$
   \text{EIRP} = P_{\text{ant}} + G_{\text{ant}} 
               = 27\ \text{dBm} + 15\ \text{dBi} = 42\ \text{dBm}
   $$

   In watts:

   $$
   P_{\text{EIRP,W}} = 10^{\frac{42}{10}} \times 10^{-3}
                     = 10^{4.2} \times 10^{-3}
                     \approx 15848.9 \times 10^{-3}
                     = 15.85\ \text{W}
   $$

Thus the antenna sees 0.5 W of input power and radiates an isotropic equivalent of about 15.9 W.

---

### 5. Common pitfalls and practical notes

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Correct practice |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| **Using 10 log₁₀ for voltage ratios** | Confusing power‑ratio formula (1) with voltage‑ratio formula (2). | Remember that voltage (or current) ratios require the factor 20 because power ∝ V². |
| **Mixing dBi and dBd without conversion** | dBd is referenced to a dipole (2.15 dBi). | Convert: $G_{\text{dBi}} = G_{\text{dBd}} + 2.15$. |
| **Neglecting the reference impedance** | dB is dimensionless; the same voltage dB value corresponds to different power dB values if impedances differ. | Keep the system impedance consistent (usually 50 Ω) when moving between voltage and power. |
| **Treating “−3 dB” as “‑3 dBm”** | A loss of 3 dB reduces the power ratio by half, but the absolute level must still be expressed in dBm or dBW. | Apply the loss to the existing dBm value, then convert if needed. |
| **Rounding early** | Decibel arithmetic is additive; premature rounding can accumulate error, especially in long cascades. | Keep at least two decimal places until the final result, then round to the required precision. |

**Practical tip:** When drafting a link‑budget spreadsheet, keep a column for each component’s dB contribution and a running total. This visual addition makes it easy to spot missing losses (e.g., connector attenuation) and to verify that the final received power meets the receiver sensitivity requirement.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following expressions correctly converts a pure power ratio of 5 to decibels?

- A. $10\log_{10}(5)$
- B. $20\log_{10}(5)$
- C. $5\times10$
- D. $5\times20$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。Decibels for power ratios use the factor 10, per Equation (1).

</details>

**2.** A voltage ratio of 2 across the same impedance corresponds to how many decibels?

- A. 3.01 dB
- B. 6.02 dB
- C. 2 dB
- D. 10 dB

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。Use Equation (2): $20\log_{10}(2) \approx 6.02$ dB.

</details>

**3.** Convert –85 dBm to watts (W). Provide the answer in watts.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Use Equation (4): $P_{\text{W}} = 10^{\frac{P_{\text{dBm}}}{10}} \times 10^{-3}$.
2. Insert $P_{\text{dBm}} = -85$: $10^{\frac{-85}{10}} = 10^{-8.5}$.
3. Multiply by $10^{-3}$: $P_{\text{W}} = 10^{-8.5} \times 10^{-3} = 10^{-11.5}$ W.
4. Evaluate $10^{-11.5} = 3.1622776601683795\times10^{-12}$ W. 
$\boxed{3.1622776601683795e-12}$

</details>

**4.** A link budget has the following items: transmitter power = 20 dBm, cable loss = 2 dB, connector loss = 0.5 dB, antenna gain = 12 dBi. What is the resulting EIRP expressed in dBm?


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Subtract all losses from the transmit power: $P_{\text{after losses}} = 20\ \text{dBm} - 2\ \text{dB} - 0.5\ \text{dB} = 17.5\ \text{dBm}$.
2. Add the antenna gain: $\text{EIRP} = 17.5\ \text{dBm} + 12\ \text{dBi} = 29.5\ \text{dBm}$.
$\boxed{29.5}$

</details>
