## 正文

In modern telecommunication systems the information carried by a waveform is almost always processed in the frequency domain. Antenna design, channel equalisation, interference management, and spectrum regulation all rely on a precise description of *how much power a signal occupies at each frequency*. This description is provided by the **Fourier transform**, the **power spectral density (PSD)**, and the **autocorrelation function**. Understanding the relationships among these three concepts allows engineers to:

* predict the impact of a signal on adjacent channels (spectral leakage, out‑of‑band emissions);
* design filters that shape the spectrum to meet regulatory masks;
* evaluate the noise performance of a receiver through the Wiener‑Khinchin theorem;
* compute link budgets that involve time‑varying (random) signals such as fading waveforms.

Below we present the definitions that are universally used in the literature, the key formulas, and a short worked example that ties them together.

---

### 1. Continuous‑time Fourier Transform (CTFT)

For a deterministic, absolutely integrable signal $x(t)$ (unit: **V** for voltage or **W** for power), the **forward transform** is  

$$
\boxed{X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f t}\,dt}
\tag{1}
$$

where  

* $f$ – frequency in **hertz (Hz)**,  
* $j=\sqrt{-1}$.

The **inverse transform** reconstructs the time‑domain signal  

$$
\boxed{x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\,e^{j2\pi f t}\,df}
\tag{2}
$$

A common source of confusion is the use of angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ (rad/s). Equation (1) uses the ordinary frequency $f$; if $\omega$ is preferred, replace $2\pi f$ by $\omega$ and adjust the integration variable accordingly.

---

### 2. Power Spectral Density (PSD)

The PSD describes how the **average power** of a signal is distributed over frequency. For a deterministic signal observed over a finite interval of length $T$ seconds, the **periodogram** definition is  

$$
\boxed{S_{x}(f)=\frac{|X_T(f)|^{2}}{T}}
\tag{3}
$$

where  

* $X_T(f)$ is the Fourier transform of the *windowed* signal $x_T(t)=x(t)w_T(t)$ with a rectangular window $w_T(t)=1$ for $0\le t\le T$ and zero elsewhere;  
* the unit of $S_x(f)$ is **V$^2$/Hz** (or **W/Hz** for power signals).

For a *wide‑sense stationary (WSS) random process* $X(t)$ the PSD is defined as the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation (see next section):

$$
\boxed{S_{X}(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} R_X(\tau)\,e^{-j2\pi f \tau}\,d\tau}
\tag{4}
$$

Equation (4) is the **Wiener–Khinchin theorem**. The total average power equals the integral of the PSD over all frequencies:

$$
\boxed{P_{\text{avg}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} S_X(f)\,df}
\tag{5}
$$

---

### 3. Autocorrelation

For a deterministic signal the (non‑normalised) autocorrelation is  

$$
\boxed{R_x(\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\,x(t+\tau)\,dt}
\tag{6}
$$

Its value at zero lag, $R_x(0)$, equals the **energy** of the signal (units V$^2$·s).  

For a WSS random process $X(t)$ the **autocorrelation function** is the statistical expectation  

$$
\boxed{R_X(\tau)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[ X(t)\,X(t+\tau) \right]}
\tag{7}
$$

with units **V$^2$** (or **W**) – the same as the average power.  

The Wiener–Khinchin theorem (4) guarantees that the PSD and autocorrelation are a Fourier‑transform pair.

---

### 4. Worked Example

Consider a single‑tone voltage signal  

$$
x(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_0 t),\qquad A=2\ \text{V},\; f_0=1\ \text{MHz}.
$$

We will (i) compute its Fourier transform, (ii) obtain the two‑sided PSD using (3) for an observation window $T=10\ \text{µs}$, and (iii) verify the Wiener–Khinchin relationship.

#### 4.1 Fourier Transform

Using the identity $\cos(2\pi f_0 t)=\tfrac12\big(e^{j2\pi f_0 t}+e^{-j2\pi f_0 t}\big)$,

$$
X(f)=\frac{A}{2}\big[\delta(f-f_0)+\delta(f+f_0)\big],
\tag{8}
$$

where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac delta. The spectrum consists of two impulses of equal weight at $\pm f_0$.

#### 4.2 Power Spectral Density (periodogram)

Windowing the cosine with a rectangular window of length $T$ multiplies the spectrum by a sinc function:

$$
X_T(f)=\frac{A}{2}T\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\big[T(f-f_0)\big]+
\frac{A}{2}T\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\big[T(f+f_0)\big],
$$

where $\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}$.

Applying (3) and evaluating at the peak $f=f_0$ (where $\operatorname{sinc}(0)=1$):

$$
S_x(f_0)=\frac{\big|\frac{A}{2}T\big|^{2}}{T}
          =\frac{A^{2}T}{4}
          =\frac{(2)^2\,(10\times10^{-6})}{4}
          =\boxed{1.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{V}^2\!/\text{Hz}}.
$$

The same value appears at $-f_0$; all other frequencies contain zero power (ideal case).

#### 4.3 Autocorrelation and Wiener–Khinchin Check

For a deterministic cosine, the autocorrelation from (6) is

$$
R_x(\tau)=\frac{A^{2}}{2}\cos(2\pi f_0 \tau).
\tag{9}
$$

Taking the Fourier transform of (9) yields exactly the PSD in (8) multiplied by the factor $A^{2}/4$, which after division by $T$ reproduces the periodogram result (4.2). This confirms the Wiener–Khinchin theorem for a simple tone.

---

### 5. Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | Correct practice |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| **Confusing $f$ and $\omega$** | Some textbooks use angular frequency $\omega$ (rad/s) while others use ordinary frequency $f$ (Hz). | Always check the definition of the transform pair you are using; convert with $\omega=2\pi f$ when necessary. |
| **Missing the factor ½ in one‑sided PSD** | The total power of a real sinusoid is $A^{2}/2$. When presenting a *one‑sided* PSD (only $f\ge 0$), the Dirac weight must be doubled. | Remember: two‑sided PSD gives $A^{2}/4$ at each of $\pm f_0$; one‑sided PSD gives $A^{2}/2$ at $+f_0$. |
| **Treating periodogram as an unbiased estimator** | The periodogram has a variance that does not vanish as $T\to\infty$; averaging (Welch’s method) is required for a reliable estimate. | Use windowing and averaging to reduce bias and variance when estimating PSD from measured data. |
| **Neglecting units** | PSD units are often omitted, leading to confusion between power (W) and power density (W/Hz). | Keep track: $S_x(f)$ – V$^2$/Hz (or W/Hz); $R_x(0)$ – V$^2$ (or W). |
| **Assuming autocorrelation equals power for any signal** | Only the zero‑lag value $R_x(0)$ equals average power for *wide‑sense stationary* processes; for deterministic finite‑energy signals it equals total energy. | Distinguish energy signals (finite support) from power signals (infinite support) before applying (5). |

With these definitions, formulas, and cautions in hand, you can move confidently between the time domain, frequency domain, and statistical descriptions of signals encountered in modern telecom networks.
