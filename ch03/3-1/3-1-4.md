## 正文

In a practical wireless link the transmitted electromagnetic wave seldom travels along a single straight line from the transmitter (Tx) to the receiver (Rx). Buildings, trees, the ground and even moving objects reflect, diffract and scatter the wave. The superposition of several delayed and attenuated copies of the same signal is called *multipath propagation*. Multipath creates two important phenomena that dominate the performance of cellular, Wi‑Fi, satellite and emerging mmWave systems:

* **Frequency‑selective fading** – when the signal bandwidth exceeds the *coherence bandwidth* of the channel, different frequency components experience different gains, producing inter‑symbol interference (ISI).
* **Small‑scale (fast) fading** – rapid fluctuations of the received signal envelope caused by constructive and destructive interference of the multipath components. This is the main source of outage in mobile links.

Understanding and modelling these effects is essential for link‑budget calculations, the design of equalizers, OFDM guard intervals, diversity schemes, and for specifying fading margins in standards such as 3GPP TS 36.101.

---

### 1. Channel Impulse Response and Power Delay Profile  

A linear time‑varying wireless channel can be described by its **impulse response**  

$$
h(t,\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i(t)\, \delta(\tau-\tau_i),
$$

where  

* $t$ – observation time (seconds).  
* $\tau$ – excess delay of the $i$‑th path (seconds).  
* $a_i(t)$ – complex gain (amplitude × phase) of the $i$‑th path, possibly time‑varying.  
* $N$ – number of resolvable multipath components.  

The **power delay profile (PDP)** is the average received power versus excess delay  

$$
P(\tau)=\mathbb{E}\{|h(t,\tau)|^{2}\},
$$

with unit W (or dBm). The PDP is often measured in the field and used to compute the **root‑mean‑square (RMS) delay spread**  

$$
\tau_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum\limits_{i} P_i (\tau_i-\bar{\tau})^{2}}{\sum\limits_{i} P_i}},
\qquad
\bar{\tau}= \frac{\sum\limits_{i} P_i \tau_i}{\sum\limits_{i} P_i},
$$

where $P_i$ is the average power of the $i$‑th component.  

---

### 2. Coherence Bandwidth  

The **coherence bandwidth $B_c$** quantifies the frequency range over which the channel response is highly correlated. A common rule‑of‑thumb is  

$$
B_c \approx \frac{1}{5\,\tau_{\text{rms}}}\quad\text{(for 90 % correlation)},
$$
or alternatively  

$$
B_c \approx \frac{1}{2\pi \tau_{\text{rms}}}\quad\text{(for 50 % correlation)}.
$$

*If the signal bandwidth $W$ satisfies $W \ll B_c$, the channel appears *flat* (all frequencies see the same gain).  
If $W \gg B_c$, the channel is *frequency‑selective* and equalisation or multicarrier modulation is required.*

---

### 3. Doppler Spread and Coherence Time  

When either the Tx, Rx, or scatterers move, the path phases change with time, giving rise to a **Doppler shift**  

$$
f_{D,i}= \frac{v_i}{c} f_c \cos\theta_i,
$$

where  

* $v_i$ – relative velocity of the $i$‑th scatterer (m s$^{-1}$).  
* $c=3\times10^{8}$ m s$^{-1}$ – speed of light.  
* $f_c$ – carrier frequency (Hz).  
* $\theta_i$ – angle between velocity vector and wave propagation direction.

The **maximum Doppler frequency** (worst‑case) is  

$$
f_{D,\max}= \frac{v}{c}\,f_c,
$$

with $v$ the speed of the mobile terminal. The **Doppler spread $B_D$** is roughly $2f_{D,\max}$.  

The **coherence time $T_c$**, i.e., the time interval over which the channel can be assumed constant, is often approximated by  

$$
T_c \approx \frac{0.423}{f_{D,\max}} \quad\text{(Jakes’ model)}.
$$

If the symbol period $T_s \ll T_c$, the channel can be treated as *slow* fading; otherwise it is *fast* fading.

---

### 4. Small‑Scale Fading Distributions  

* **Rayleigh fading** – applicable when there is no dominant line‑of‑sight (LOS) component. The envelope $R$ follows  

$$
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^{2}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),\quad r\ge 0,
$$

with mean power $\mathbb{E}\{R^{2}\}=2\sigma^{2}$.  

* **Rician fading** – a LOS component of amplitude $A$ exists in addition to many scattered components. The **Rician K‑factor**  

$$
K = \frac{A^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{(ratio of deterministic to scattered power)},
$$

characterises the severity of fading. The envelope pdf is  

$$
p_R(r)=\frac{r}{\sigma^{2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{r^{2}+A^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right) I_0\!\left(\frac{Ar}{\sigma^{2}}\right),
$$

where $I_0(\cdot)$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind, order 0.

The **outage probability** for a Rayleigh channel, i.e., the probability that the instantaneous SNR $\gamma$ falls below a threshold $\gamma_{\text{th}}$, is  

$$
P_{\text{out}} = 1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{\gamma_{\text{th}}}{\bar{\gamma}}\right),
$$

with $\bar{\gamma}$ the average SNR.

---

### 5. Worked Example  

A mobile user moves at $v = 30\;\text{km h}^{-1}$ on a 2 GHz carrier. The measured RMS delay spread of the environment is $\tau_{\text{rms}} = 2\;\mu\text{s}$. An OFDM system uses subcarriers of bandwidth $W_{\text{sub}} = 200\;\text{kHz}$.

1. **Maximum Doppler frequency**  

$$
v = 30\;\text{km h}^{-1}=8.33\;\text{m s}^{-1},\qquad
f_{D,\max}= \frac{v}{c} f_c = \frac{8.33}{3\times10^{8}} \times 2\times10^{9}
           \approx 55.5\;\text{Hz}.
$$

2. **Coherence time**  

$$
T_c \approx \frac{0.423}{55.5}\approx 7.6\times10^{-3}\;\text{s}=7.6\;\text{ms}.
$$

Since the OFDM symbol duration (including CP) is typically $T_{\text{sym}} \approx 71\;\mu\text{s}$, we have $T_{\text{sym}} \ll T_c$, so the channel can be assumed *slow* for this link.

3. **Coherence bandwidth**  

$$
B_c \approx \frac{1}{5\tau_{\text{rms}}}= \frac{1}{5 \times 2\times10^{-6}}
      = 1\times10^{5}\;\text{Hz}=100\;\text{kHz}.
$$

4. **Flat or frequency‑selective?**  

Each subcarrier occupies $200\;\text{kHz} > B_c$, therefore the subcarrier experiences **frequency‑selective fading**. In practice, a guard interval (cyclic prefix) longer than the maximum excess delay (often a few microseconds) is inserted to mitigate ISI, but the subcarrier’s amplitude will still fluctuate according to the underlying small‑scale fading distribution.

---

### 6. Common Pitfalls & Practical Notes  

| Pitfall | Why it hurts | Remedy |
|---------|--------------|--------|
| **Using the maximum delay instead of RMS delay spread** when computing $B_c$ | Over‑estimates coherence bandwidth, leading to an optimistic flat‑fading assumption. | Always compute $\tau_{\text{rms}}$ from the PDP; for a quick check use the 5‑times rule. |
| **Neglecting the Doppler shift in high‑speed scenarios** (e.g., vehicular or train) | Symbol timing and channel estimation become outdated, causing severe performance loss. | Compute $T_c$ and ensure pilot spacing in time is well within $0.5\,T_c$. |
| **Mixing units** (µs vs ms, km/h vs m/s) | Leads to orders‑of‑magnitude errors in $f_D$ and $B_c$. | Keep a unit‑conversion checklist; most calculators accept SI units. |
| **Assuming Rayleigh fading for a strong LOS link** | Under‑estimates the received power and over‑estimates outage. | Estimate the Rician $K$‑factor from measurements; use the Rician pdf when $K>0\,$dB. |
| **Designing OFDM subcarrier spacing solely on $B_c$ without considering CP length** | ISI can still appear if the cyclic prefix is shorter than the channel’s maximum excess delay. | Choose CP ≥ $ \max(\tau_i)$ (often a few µs) and verify with the measured PDP. |

By correctly modelling multipath delay spread, Doppler spread, and the associated fading statistics, engineers can dimension link budgets, select appropriate modulation and coding schemes, and design robust equalisation or diversity mechanisms that meet the reliability targets of modern wireless standards.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following expressions best approximates the *coherence bandwidth* for a 90 % correlation level?

- A. $B_c \approx \dfrac{1}{2\pi\,\tau_{\text{rms}}}$
- B. $B_c \approx \dfrac{1}{5\,\tau_{\text{rms}}}$
- C. $B_c \approx \dfrac{1}{\tau_{\text{rms}}}$
- D. $B_c \approx \dfrac{5}{\tau_{\text{rms}}}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。For 90 % correlation the rule‑of‑thumb is $B_c \approx 1/(5\,\tau_{\text{rms}})$; the $1/(2\pi\tau_{\text{rms}})$ form corresponds to 50 % correlation.

</details>

**2.** Which condition leads to a *Rayleigh* fading model for the received envelope?

- A. A strong line‑of‑sight (LOS) component dominates the received power.
- B. The LOS component is comparable in power to the scattered components.
- C. No dominant LOS component exists; the signal consists only of many scattered paths.
- D. The channel exhibits a very large Rician K‑factor (>10 dB).

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。Rayleigh fading assumes the received signal is the sum of many independent scattered components with no deterministic LOS term.

</details>

**3.** An indoor office environment has an RMS delay spread $\tau_{\text{rms}} = 0.8\;\mu\text{s}$. Compute the 90 % coherence bandwidth $B_c$ using the rule‑of‑thumb $B_c \approx 1/(5\,\tau_{\text{rms}})$. Give the answer in kilohertz (kHz).


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Convert $\tau_{\text{rms}}$ to seconds: $0.8\;\mu\text{s}=0.8\times10^{-6}\;\text{s}$.  
2. Apply the formula:  
$$
B_c = \frac{1}{5\tau_{\text{rms}}}
      = \frac{1}{5 \times 0.8\times10^{-6}}
      = \frac{1}{4\times10^{-6}}
      = 2.5\times10^{5}\;\text{Hz}.
$$  
3. Convert Hz to kHz: $2.5\times10^{5}\;\text{Hz}=250\;\text{kHz}$.  
$$
\boxed{250}
$$

</details>

**4.** A vehicle travels at $v = 120\;\text{km h}^{-1}$ on a carrier frequency $f_c = 3.5\;\text{GHz}$.  
Calculate the maximum Doppler frequency $f_{D,\max}$ and the corresponding coherence time $T_c$ using Jakes’ approximation $T_c \approx 0.423/f_{D,\max}$. Report the coherence time in milliseconds (ms) rounded to two decimal places.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Convert speed to meters per second:  
$$
v = 120\;\text{km h}^{-1}= \frac{120\,000}{3600}\;\text{m s}^{-1}=33.333\;\text{m s}^{-1}.
$$  
2. Compute maximum Doppler frequency:  
$$
f_{D,\max}= \frac{v}{c}\,f_c
          = \frac{33.333}{3\times10^{8}}\times 3.5\times10^{9}
          \approx 388.9\;\text{Hz}.
$$  
3. Compute coherence time:  
$$
T_c = \frac{0.423}{f_{D,\max}}
    = \frac{0.423}{388.9}\;\text{s}
    \approx 1.088\times10^{-3}\;\text{s}.
$$  
4. Convert to milliseconds: $1.088\times10^{-3}\;\text{s}=1.09\;\text{ms}$.  
$$
\boxed{1.09}
$$

</details>
