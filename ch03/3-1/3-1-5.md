## 正文

In any practical radio link the received signal is always accompanied by unwanted random fluctuations called *thermal noise* (or *Johnson‑Nyquist noise*). Even if the transmitter were ideal, the antenna and the subsequent receiver front‑end generate a baseline power that limits the smallest detectable signal. Consequently, the concepts of **noise power**, **noise figure (NF)**, **noise temperature**, and the way they combine when several components are connected in series are fundamental for link‑budget calculations, system design, and the evaluation of regulatory compliance.

---

### 1. Thermal noise power  

For a resistor at absolute temperature *T* (Kelvin) that is observed over a bandwidth *B* (Hz), the available noise power is  

$$
P_{\mathrm{N}} = k\,T\,B \quad\text{[W]},
$$

where  

* $k = 1.380\,649\times10^{-23}\,\text{J·K}^{-1}$ is Boltzmann’s constant,  
* $T$ is the **noise temperature** of the device or of the surrounding environment,  
* $B$ is the **single‑sided** noise bandwidth (the factor 2 for double‑sided spectra is already included in the definition of *B* used by most telecom standards).

When the temperature is expressed relative to the reference temperature $T_{0}=290\;\text{K}$ (the standard ambient temperature adopted by 3GPP, ITU‑R, etc.), the corresponding *reference noise power* is  

$$
P_{N0}=k\,T_{0}\,B .
$$

It is common to quote noise power in **dBm**:

$$
P_{\mathrm{N(dBm)}} = 10\log_{10}\!\bigl(P_{\mathrm{N}}/1\;\text{mW}\bigr)
                     = 10\log_{10}(kTB) + 30 .
$$

---

### 2. Noise Figure (NF) and Noise Factor (F)  

The **noise factor** $F$ is a dimensionless ratio that quantifies how much a component degrades the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR):

$$
F = \frac{(\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{N})_{\text{in}}}{(\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{N})_{\text{out}}}\;,
\qquad
\text{NF (dB)} = 10\log_{10}F .
$$

A *perfect* noiseless device would have $F=1$ ($\text{NF}=0\;\text{dB}$). Real amplifiers, mixers, filters, etc., have $F>1$.

Because the input SNR is limited by the thermal noise at temperature $T_{0}$, the **effective noise temperature** $T_{\!e}$ of a component is defined as  

$$
F = 1 + \frac{T_{\!e}}{T_{0}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T_{\!e}= (F-1)T_{0} .
$$

Thus NF and noise temperature are interchangeable descriptors; engineers often prefer the one that matches the available data sheet.

---

### 3. Linear vs. dB representation  

When cascading several stages, all gains and noise factors must be expressed **linearly**:

$$
G_{\text{lin}} = 10^{G_{\text{dB}}/10}\;,
\qquad
F = 10^{\text{NF (dB)}/10}\; .
$$

A *loss* is simply a gain smaller than 1 (negative dB). Forgetting this conversion is a frequent source of error.

---

### 4. Friis cascade formula  

For a chain of *N* two‑port networks (amplifiers, mixers, filters, etc.) the overall noise factor $F_{\text{tot}}$ is given by the **Friis formula**:

$$
\boxed{
F_{\text{tot}} = F_{1}
                 + \frac{F_{2}-1}{G_{1}}
                 + \frac{F_{3}-1}{G_{1}G_{2}}
                 + \dots
                 + \frac{F_{N}-1}{G_{1}G_{2}\dots G_{N-1}}
               }
$$

where  

* $F_{i}$ is the noise factor of the *i*‑th stage,  
* $G_{i}$ is the **linear power gain** of the *i*‑th stage (a loss has $G_{i}<1$).  

The formula shows that the **first stage dominates** the total NF; a high gain in the front‑end quickly suppresses the contribution of later, noisier stages.

---

### 5. Worked example  

A typical receiver front‑end consists of three cascaded blocks:

| Stage | NF (dB) | Gain (dB) |
|------|--------|-----------|
| LNA  | 1.0    | 15        |
| Mixer| 6.0    | 0         |
| Filter| 3.0   | –2        |

**Step 1 – Convert to linear values**

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{1}&=10^{0.1}=1.2589, &
G_{1}&=10^{1.5}=31.62,\\
F_{2}&=10^{0.6}=3.981, &
G_{2}&=10^{0}=1,\\
F_{3}&=10^{0.3}=1.995, &
G_{3}&=10^{-0.2}=0.63096\;(\text{loss}).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 2 – Apply Friis**

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{\text{tot}} &= F_{1}
               + \frac{F_{2}-1}{G_{1}}
               + \frac{F_{3}-1}{G_{1}G_{2}} \\[2mm]
               &= 1.2589
               + \frac{3.981-1}{31.62}
               + \frac{1.995-1}{31.62\times1} \\[2mm]
               &= 1.2589 + 0.0943 + 0.0315 \\[2mm]
               &= 1.3847 .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Step 3 – Convert back to dB**

$$
\text{NF}_{\text{tot}} = 10\log_{10}(1.3847) \approx 1.41\;\text{dB}.
$$

Even though the mixer alone has a relatively poor NF (6 dB), the high gain of the LNA reduces its impact dramatically. If the LNA gain were reduced to 5 dB, the overall NF would increase to about 3 dB, illustrating the importance of a low‑noise, high‑gain first stage.

---

### 6. Common pitfalls and practical notes  

| Pitfall | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Using dB values directly in Friis** | The formula requires linear ratios; inserting dB numbers yields nonsense. | Convert every gain and NF to linear before substitution. |
| **Ignoring losses (negative gain)** | A filter or cable with –3 dB loss actually *reduces* the denominator in the Friis terms, worsening NF. | Treat losses as gains < 1 (e.g., –3 dB → $G=10^{-0.3}=0.501$). |
| **Confusing noise figure with noise factor** | NF (dB) and F (linear) are not interchangeable without the log conversion. | Remember $F = 10^{\text{NF(dB)}/10}$. |
| **Using 300 K instead of the standard 290 K** | The reference temperature appears in the conversion between NF and noise temperature; a 10 K error can shift NF by ≈0.15 dB. | Adopt $T_{0}=290\;\text{K}$ consistently. |
| **Assuming NF adds linearly** | Only the first stage’s NF matters if its gain is very high; later stages are weighted by preceding gains. | Apply Friis, not simple addition. |

When building a link budget, start by selecting an LNA with the smallest possible NF and at least 10–15 dB of gain. Then verify that the cumulative NF after all subsequent stages still satisfies the system‑level requirement (often expressed as a maximum allowable *receiver noise figure*).  

---  

**Key formulas for quick reference**

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\!N}&=kTB \quad[\text{W}],\\
F      &=\frac{(\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{N})_{\text{in}}}{(\mathrm{S}/\mathrm{N})_{\text{out}}},\qquad
\text{NF}=10\log_{10}F\;[\text{dB}],\\
T_{\!e}&=(F-1)T_{0},\\
G_{\text{lin}}&=10^{G_{\text{dB}}/10},\qquad
F=10^{\text{NF(dB)}/10},\\
F_{\text{tot}}&=F_{1}+\frac{F_{2}-1}{G_{1}}+\frac{F_{3}-1}{G_{1}G_{2}}+\dots .
\end{aligned}
$$

Understanding and applying these relations enables accurate prediction of link performance, proper component selection, and compliance with standards that limit out‑of‑band emissions and required receiver sensitivity.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following expressions correctly defines the **noise figure (NF)** of a component?

- A. $\displaystyle \text{NF}=10\log_{10}\!\bigl(\frac{S/N_{\text{out}}}{S/N_{\text{in}}}\bigr)$
- B. $\displaystyle \text{NF}=10\log_{10}\!\bigl(\frac{S/N_{\text{in}}}{S/N_{\text{out}}}\bigr)$
- C. $\displaystyle \text{NF}=kTB$
- D. $\displaystyle \text{NF}= \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。NF is defined as the ratio of input to output SNR; the logarithmic version uses 10 log₁₀ of that ratio.

</details>

**2.** In a two‑stage receiver, Stage 1 has NF = 3 dB and gain = 0 dB, while Stage 2 has NF = 0.5 dB and gain = 20 dB. Which stage dominates the overall noise figure?

- A. Stage 1, because its low gain gives little attenuation of its own noise.
- B. Stage 2, because it has the lower NF.
- C. Both stages contribute equally.
- D. Neither; the overall NF is simply the average of the two.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。The Friis formula shows the first stage’s NF is weighted by 1, while the second stage’s contribution is divided by the gain of Stage 1 (here 1, i.e., 0 dB).

</details>

**3.** The effective noise temperature $T_e$ of a component is related to its noise factor $F$ by which equation?

- A. $T_e = (F-1)T_0$
- B. $F = 1 + \frac{T_0}{T_e}$
- C. $T_e = F\,T_0$
- D. $F = \frac{T_e}{T_0}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。Rearranging $F = 1 + T_e/T_0$ gives $T_e = (F-1)T_0$.

</details>

**4.** A receiver front‑end consists of three cascaded stages:  

- Stage 1: NF = 0.5 dB, gain = 20 dB  
- Stage 2: NF = 4 dB,   gain = 10 dB  
- Stage 3: NF = 2 dB,   gain = –1 dB (loss)  

Calculate the overall noise figure of the chain in dB.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Convert NF to linear noise factors:  

$$
F_1 = 10^{0.5/10}=1.1220,\;
F_2 = 10^{4/10}=2.5119,\;
F_3 = 10^{2/10}=1.5849.
$$

2. Convert gains to linear values:  

$$
G_1 = 10^{20/10}=100,\;
G_2 = 10^{10/10}=10,\;
G_3 = 10^{-1/10}=0.7943\;(\text{not needed in Friis}).
$$

3. Apply Friis formula (only up to stage 3):  

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{\text{tot}} &= F_1
               + \frac{F_2-1}{G_1}
               + \frac{F_3-1}{G_1 G_2} \\[1mm]
               &= 1.1220
               + \frac{2.5119-1}{100}
               + \frac{1.5849-1}{100\times10} \\[1mm]
               &= 1.1220 + 0.015119 + 0.0005849 \\[1mm]
               &= 1.137704 .
\end{aligned}
$$

4. Convert back to dB:  

$$
\text{NF}_{\text{tot}} = 10\log_{10}(1.137704) \approx 0.56\;\text{dB}.
$$

$$
$\boxed{0.56}$
$$

</details>

**5.** A receiver operates over a bandwidth of 5 MHz and has a total system noise temperature of 400 K. Compute the thermal noise power at the input of the receiver in dBm.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Use $P_N = kTB$.  

$k = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\;\text{J/K}$  

$T = 400\;\text{K}$  

$B = 5\;\text{MHz} = 5\times10^{6}\;\text{Hz}$

$$
P_N = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\times 400 \times 5\times10^{6}
    = 2.761298\times10^{-14}\;\text{W}.
$$

2. Convert to dBm:  

$$
P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}\!\bigl(P_N/1\;\text{mW}\bigr)
               = 10\log_{10}(2.761298\times10^{-14}) + 30.
$$

$$
\log_{10}(2.761298\times10^{-14}) = \log_{10}(2.761298) - 14
                                   \approx 0.4409 - 14 = -13.5591.
$$

$$
P_{\text{dBm}} = 10(-13.5591) + 30 \approx -105.591\;\text{dBm}.
$$

$$
$\boxed{-105.59}$
$$

</details>
