## 正文

In a real cellular or wireless‑wide‑area network the radio signal does not travel along a single, perfectly straight line. Buildings, hills, trees, and even the occasional vehicle block or diffract the wave, producing *large‑scale* variations that are much slower than the rapid fluctuations caused by multipath (small‑scale fading).  
The large‑scale component is usually captured by a **propagation (or path‑loss) model** together with a random term called **shadow fading** (or log‑normal shadowing). Accurate modelling of these effects is essential for:

* **Network planning** – estimating coverage, required base‑station density, and cell radius.  
* **Link budget calculations** – determining whether a given transmit power and antenna configuration can meet a target received‑signal level.  
* **Interference analysis** – predicting average interference levels between cells.  

---

### 1. Log‑Distance Path‑Loss Model  

The most widely used analytical model is the *log‑distance* model, which expresses the mean (median) path loss as a logarithmic function of distance:

$$
\boxed{ PL(d) = PL(d_0) + 10\,n \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{d_0}\right) }   \tag{1}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $PL(d)$ | Path loss at distance $d$ (ratio of transmitted to received power) | dB |
| $PL(d_0)$ | Path loss at a reference distance $d_0$ (usually 1 m or 100 m) | dB |
| $d$ | Separation between transmitter and receiver | m |
| $d_0$ | Reference distance, chosen where the model is known to be valid | m |
| $n$ | Path‑loss exponent, environment‑dependent (free‑space $n=2$; urban $2.7\!-\!3.5$; dense indoor $3\!-\!5$) | — |
| $\log_{10}$ | Base‑10 logarithm | — |

The reference loss $PL(d_0)$ can be obtained from the **Friis free‑space equation**:

$$
PL(d_0) = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d_0 f}{c}\right)   \tag{2}
$$

where $f$ is the carrier frequency (Hz) and $c = 3\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$ is the speed of light.

---

### 2. Shadow Fading (Log‑Normal Shadowing)

Even after accounting for the distance‑dependent loss, measurements show a residual variation that is well described by a zero‑mean Gaussian random variable in dB:

$$
\boxed{ PL_{\text{total}}(d) = PL(d) + X_{\sigma} }   \tag{3}
$$

* $X_{\sigma}$  – Shadow‑fading term, $X_{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})$ (dB).  
* $\sigma$ – Standard deviation of the shadowing, typically $4\!-\!12$ dB for outdoor macro‑cellular environments and up to $12\!-\!16$ dB for indoor office scenarios.  

Because the dB domain is logarithmic, adding $X_{\sigma}$ in (3) corresponds to multiplying the linear power ratio by a log‑normal factor. Hence the name **log‑normal shadowing**.

---

### 3. Worked Example  

**Problem:**  
A macro‑cell operates at $f = 900$ MHz. The reference distance is $d_0 = 100$ m, and the measured loss at that distance is $PL(d_0)=100$ dB. The environment has a path‑loss exponent $n=3.5$ and a shadow‑fading standard deviation $\sigma = 8$ dB.  

* (a) Compute the *median* path loss at $d = 2$ km.  
* (b) If a particular location experiences a shadowing value of $+5$ dB, what is the *actual* path loss there?

**Solution:**  

1. Compute the distance ratio  

$$
\frac{d}{d_0}= \frac{2000\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{m}} = 20 .
$$

2. Evaluate the log‑distance term  

$$
10 n \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{d_0}\right) = 10 \times 3.5 \times \log_{10}(20)
      = 35 \times 1.3010 \approx 45.54\ \text{dB}.
$$

3. Median loss (no shadowing)  

$$
PL_{\text{median}}(2\text{ km}) = PL(d_0) + 45.54 = 100 + 45.54 = 145.54\ \text{dB}.
$$

4. Including a concrete shadowing realization $X_{\sigma}=+5$ dB  

$$
PL_{\text{actual}} = 145.54 + 5 = 150.54\ \text{dB}.
$$

Thus the median loss at 2 km is **≈ 145.5 dB**, and the specific site with +5 dB shadowing suffers **≈ 150.5 dB** loss.

---

### 4. Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls  

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Correct practice |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| **Mixing linear and dB quantities** | Adding a dB term to a linear power ratio is meaningless. | Always keep all terms in the same domain; use (1) and (3) in dB, or convert to linear before multiplication. |
| **Using the wrong reference distance** | Some standards define $d_0=1$ m, others $d_0=100$ m. | Verify the reference distance used for the given $PL(d_0)$ value; keep units consistent. |
| **Treating $\sigma$ as a deterministic loss** | $\sigma$ is a *standard deviation*; a single simulation should draw a random sample, not add $\sigma$ directly. | Generate $X_{\sigma}$ from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})$ when Monte‑Carlo simulating; for worst‑case planning use a multiple of $\sigma$ (e.g., $+2\sigma$). |
| **Neglecting frequency dependence of $PL(d_0)$** | Equation (2) shows that the reference loss grows with frequency. | Re‑calculate $PL(d_0)$ whenever the carrier frequency changes, unless a measured value is supplied. |
| **Assuming a single exponent for all distances** | In many environments the exponent changes after a “breakpoint” (e.g., from line‑of‑sight to non‑LOS). | Use a *dual‑slope* model if a breakpoint distance is known; otherwise acknowledge the added modelling error. |

By respecting these conventions, engineers can reliably predict coverage, size cells, and perform realistic system‑level simulations.

## 練習題

**1.** For an urban macro‑cell environment, which range most accurately describes a typical path‑loss exponent $n$?

- A. 0.5 – 1.0
- B. 1.5 – 2.0
- C. 2.7 – 3.5
- D. 5.0 – 6.0

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。Urban macro‑cells usually exhibit $n$ between about 2.7 and 3.5, higher than free‑space ($n=2$).

</details>

**2.** The shadow‑fading variable $X_{\sigma}$ is modeled as a zero‑mean Gaussian in dB. In the linear (power) domain this corresponds to which distribution?

- A. Uniform distribution
- B. Exponential distribution
- C. Log‑normal distribution
- D. Rayleigh distribution

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。A Gaussian in dB (logarithmic) translates to a log‑normal distribution in the linear power ratio.

</details>

**3.** A site uses a reference distance $d_0=100$ m with $PL(d_0)=60$ dB. The path‑loss exponent is $n=2.7$ and the shadow‑fading standard deviation is $\sigma=6$ dB. For a user located $d=500$ m away, assume a particular shadowing realization of $-3$ dB. Compute the total path loss $PL_{\text{total}}(d)$ in dB.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Distance ratio: $d/d_0 = 500/100 = 5$.  
2. Log‑distance term: $10 n \log_{10}(5) = 10 \times 2.7 \times \log_{10}(5) = 27 \times 0.69897 \approx 18.872$ dB.  
3. Median loss: $PL_{\text{median}} = 60 + 18.872 = 78.872$ dB.  
4. Add shadowing: $PL_{\text{total}} = 78.872 + (-3) = 75.872$ dB.  
$$\boxed{75.872}$$

</details>

**4.** A base station transmits at $P_t = 20$ dBm with antenna gain $G_t = 2$ dBi. A mobile handset has antenna gain $G_r = 2$ dBi and measures a received power of $-85$ dBm on a 2 GHz carrier. Using a log‑distance model with reference distance $d_0 = 1$ m, path‑loss exponent $n = 3$, and **no** shadowing, determine the distance $d$ (in meters) between the base station and the handset.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Compute the required path loss:  
   $PL(d) = P_t + G_t + G_r - P_r = 20 + 2 + 2 - (-85) = 109$ dB.  
2. Reference loss at 1 m (free‑space):  
   $PL(d_0) = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d_0 f}{c}\right)$  
   $= 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 1 \times 2\times10^{9}}{3\times10^{8}}\right)$  
   $= 20\log_{10}(83.776) \approx 38.46$ dB.  
3. Apply the log‑distance model:  
   $PL(d) = PL(d_0) + 10 n \log_{10}(d/d_0)$ →  
   $109 = 38.46 + 30\log_{10}(d)$.  
4. Solve for $\log_{10}(d)$:  
   $\log_{10}(d) = (109 - 38.46)/30 = 70.54/30 \approx 2.3513$.  
5. Convert to linear distance:  
   $d = 10^{2.3513} \approx 224.2$ m.  
$$\boxed{224.2}$$

</details>
