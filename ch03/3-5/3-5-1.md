# 3.5.1 基本電學：功率、阻抗匹配與傳輸線衰減

所屬：[3.5 基礎數理](../3-5.md)

## 正文

In modern wired and wireless networks the link budget is built from three elementary building blocks:

1. **Power** – the amount of electromagnetic energy that can be generated, transferred, or received.
2. **Impedance matching** – the condition that maximises the power that actually reaches the load, avoiding reflections on transmission lines.
3. **Attenuation of a transmission line** – the exponential decay of the travelling wave caused by conductor, dielectric, and radiation losses.

Understanding these concepts is essential for designing base‑station front‑ends, microwave interconnects, fiber‑to‑the‑home distribution, and any system where the signal must travel over a physical medium. The following sections give precise definitions, the governing formulas, and a short worked example that ties the three ideas together.

---

### 1. Power in Linear Networks  

For a time‑varying voltage $v(t)$ and current $i(t)$ the **instantaneous power** is  

$$
p(t)=v(t)i(t)\quad\text{[W]}. 
$$

In sinusoidal steady‑state we usually work with **root‑mean‑square (RMS)** quantities  

$$
V_{\text{rms}}=\frac{V_{\text{peak}}}{\sqrt{2}},\qquad 
I_{\text{rms}}=\frac{I_{\text{peak}}}{\sqrt{2}} .
$$

If the load impedance is $Z=R+jX$ and the source voltage is $V_{\text{rms}}$, the **average (real) power** delivered to the load is  

$$
P = V_{\text{rms}} I_{\text{rms}} \cos\phi
   = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^{2}}{|Z|}\cos\phi
   = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}^{2}R}{R^{2}+X^{2}}\quad\text{[W]}, 
$$

where $\phi = \arg(Z)$ is the phase angle between voltage and current, $\cos\phi$ is the **power factor**, and $R=\Re\{Z\}$ is the resistive part that actually dissipates power.

---

### 2. Impedance Matching  

When a source with internal impedance $Z_{\mathrm{s}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}+jX_{\mathrm{s}}$ drives a load $Z_{\mathrm{l}} = R_{\mathrm{l}}+jX_{\mathrm{l}}$, the **Maximum Power Transfer Theorem** states that the load receives the greatest possible real power when  

$$
Z_{\mathrm{l}} = Z_{\mathrm{s}}^{*} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 
R_{\mathrm{l}} = R_{\mathrm{s}},\; X_{\mathrm{l}} = -X_{\mathrm{s}} .
$$

In the special case of purely resistive source and load ($X_{\mathrm{s}}=X_{\mathrm{l}}=0$), the condition reduces to $R_{\mathrm{l}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}$.

For high‑frequency interconnects the source and load are often separated by a **transmission line** with characteristic impedance $Z_{0}$. A lossless line has  

$$
Z_{0}= \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}\quad\text{[Ω]},
$$

where $L$ (H/m) and $C$ (F/m) are the per‑unit‑length inductance and capacitance. If the line is terminated by a load that does not equal $Z_{0}$, part of the wave is reflected, reducing the net power that reaches the load. A common broadband solution is a **quarter‑wave transformer** whose characteristic impedance is  

$$
Z_{\text{qt}} = \sqrt{Z_{\mathrm{s}} Z_{\mathrm{l}}}\quad\text{[Ω]} .
$$

A line of length $\lambda/4$ (quarter wavelength) with this impedance transforms the load to appear as $Z_{\mathrm{s}}$ at the source, eliminating reflections.

---

### 3. Transmission‑Line Attenuation  

Real lines exhibit loss, described by the **complex propagation constant**  

$$
\gamma = \alpha + j\beta\quad\text{[Np/m + rad/m]},
$$

where  

* $\alpha$ – **attenuation constant** (nepers per metre, Np/m).  
* $\beta$ – **phase constant** (radians per metre, rad/m).

The voltage (and current) magnitude decays as  

$$
|V(z)| = |V(0)| e^{-\alpha z},
$$

while the **power** decays twice as fast because it is proportional to the square of the voltage:  

$$
P(z) = P(0)\,e^{-2\alpha z}\quad\text{[W]} .
$$

Attenuation is often expressed in **decibels per unit length** using  

$$
\alpha_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10} e\; \alpha \approx 8.686\,\alpha\quad\text{[dB/m]} .
$$

For a low‑loss line ( $R \ll \omega L$ and $G \ll \omega C$ ) the attenuation constant can be approximated by  

$$
\alpha \approx \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} + \frac{G}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}\quad\text{[Np/m]},
$$

with $R$ (Ω/m) the series resistance and $G$ (S/m) the shunt conductance.

---

### 4. Worked Example  

A base‑station transmitter delivers a **10 V$_{\text{rms}}$** sinusoid into a source resistance $R_{\mathrm{s}}=50\;\Omega$. The antenna feed is a 200‑Ω resistive load located 100 m away through a coaxial cable with  

* characteristic impedance $Z_{0}=50\;\Omega$,  
* attenuation constant $\alpha = 0.02\;\text{Np/m}$ (≈ 0.174 dB/m).

**(a) Power without matching**  

The total series resistance seen by the source is $R_{\mathrm{s}}+R_{\mathrm{l}}=250\;\Omega$.  
$$
P_{\text{load}} = \frac{V_{\text{s}}^{2} R_{\mathrm{l}}}{(R_{\mathrm{s}}+R_{\mathrm{l}})^{2}}
               = \frac{10^{2}\times200}{250^{2}}
               = 0.32\;\text{W}=320\;\text{mW}.
$$

**(b) Adding a quarter‑wave transformer**  

The required transformer impedance is  

$$
Z_{\text{qt}} = \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{s}}R_{\mathrm{l}}}= \sqrt{50\times200}=100\;\Omega .
$$

When the transformer is inserted, the source sees an effective load of 50 Ω, so the voltage division disappears and the full source voltage appears across the 50‑Ω equivalent load. The power at the transformer output (i.e., before the line) becomes  

$$
P_{\text{qt-out}} = \frac{V_{\text{s}}^{2}}{4R_{\mathrm{s}}}= \frac{10^{2}}{4\times 50}=0.5\;\text{W}.
$$

**(c) Accounting for line attenuation**  

The line length is $L=100\;\text{m}$. Power at the antenna is  

$$
P_{\text{ant}} = P_{\text{qt-out}} \, e^{-2\alpha L}
               = 0.5 \, e^{-2\times0.02\times100}
               = 0.5 \, e^{-4}
               \approx 0.5 \times 0.0183 = 9.2\;\text{mW}.
$$

Thus, despite the matching network increasing the delivered power to the line, the severe attenuation reduces the received power to only a few milliwatts. This illustrates why matching and low‑loss distribution are both indispensable.

---

### 5. Common Mistakes & Practical Notes  

| Pitfall | Why it hurts | Remedy |
|---------|--------------|--------|
| **Ignoring the reactive part** of source or load impedance. | A mismatched reactance creates reflections even if resistances are equal. | Use a conjugate match (add series/parallel L‑C networks) or a broadband transformer that also cancels $X$. |
| **Using the voltage‑divider formula with RMS values but forgetting to square** when computing power. | Leads to a factor‑of‑2 error. | Remember $P = V_{\text{rms}}^{2} / R$ (or $V^{2}R/|Z|^{2}$). |
| **Confusing nepers and decibels**. | 1 Np ≈ 8.686 dB, not 10 dB. | Convert with the factor 8.686 when the problem statement gives $\alpha$ in Np/m. |
| **Applying the quarter‑wave transformer at frequencies where the line is not electrically long**. | The $\lambda/4$ condition fails, causing residual mismatch. | Verify that the physical length is within ±5 % of $\lambda/4$ at the operating frequency. |
| **Assuming attenuation is linear with length**. | Power decays exponentially; a 10 % loss per 10 m does **not** become 20 % after 20 m. | Use the exponential law $P(z)=P_{0}e^{-2\alpha z}$. |

When designing high‑frequency links, always compute the **link budget** in three stages: (1) source power, (2) matching efficiency, (3) line loss. Only then can you allocate margin for fading, antenna gain, and regulatory limits.

## 練習題

**1.** What condition on the load impedance maximises the real power transferred from a source with internal impedance $Z_{\mathrm{s}}$?

- A. $Z_{\mathrm{l}} = Z_{\mathrm{s}}$
- B. $Z_{\mathrm{l}} = Z_{\mathrm{s}}^{*}$
- C. $|Z_{\mathrm{l}}| = |Z_{\mathrm{s}}|$
- D. $Z_{\mathrm{l}} = 1/Z_{\mathrm{s}}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。Maximum power transfer requires the load to be the complex conjugate of the source impedance, cancelling the reactive part and equalising resistances.

</details>

**2.** For a loss‑less transmission line, the characteristic impedance $Z_{0}$ is given by which expression?

- A. $Z_{0}= \sqrt{L C}$
- B. $Z_{0}= \sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$
- C. $Z_{0}= \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{L C}}$
- D. $Z_{0}= \dfrac{L}{C}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。In a TEM line $Z_{0}= \sqrt{L/C}$; the product $LC$ gives the propagation velocity, not the impedance.

</details>

**3.** A sinusoidal source delivers $V_{\text{s}} = 10\;\text{V}_{\text{rms}}$ into a source resistance $R_{\mathrm{s}} = 50\;\Omega$. The load is a purely resistive $R_{\mathrm{l}} = 200\;\Omega$. Compute the average power dissipated in the load (in watts) without any matching network.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

Total series resistance $R_{\mathrm{tot}} = R_{\mathrm{s}} + R_{\mathrm{l}} = 250\;\Omega$.  
Load voltage $V_{\mathrm{l}} = V_{\text{s}}\frac{R_{\mathrm{l}}}{R_{\mathrm{tot}}}=10\frac{200}{250}=8\;\text{V}_{\text{rms}}$.  
Power $P = V_{\mathrm{l}}^{2}/R_{\mathrm{l}} = 8^{2}/200 = 64/200 = 0.32\;\text{W}$.  
$\boxed{0.32}$

</details>

**4.** A coaxial cable has an attenuation constant $\alpha = 0.017\;\text{Np/m}$. The line length is $L = 150\;\text{m}$. If the input power is $P_{\text{in}} = 1\;\text{W}$, what is the output power at the far end (in watts)?


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

Power decays as $P_{\text{out}} = P_{\text{in}} e^{-2\alpha L}$.  
Compute exponent: $2\alpha L = 2 \times 0.017 \times 150 = 5.1$.  
Thus $P_{\text{out}} = 1 \times e^{-5.1}$.  
$e^{-5.1} \approx 0.006074$.  
$\boxed{0.0061}$

</details>
