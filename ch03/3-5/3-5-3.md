## 正文

In modern communication systems the performance of a link, a receiver, or a network controller is seldom known a‑priori. Parameters such as channel gain, noise power, traffic intensity, or packet error probability must be inferred from noisy measurements. **Statistical estimation** provides the mathematical framework for turning finite, random observations into usable numerical values (estimates) and for quantifying the reliability of those values. Accurate estimates enable adaptive modulation, power control, link‑layer retransmission policies, and many other algorithms that keep a cellular or packet‑switched network operating efficiently.

---

### 1. Basic concepts

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $x_1,\dots ,x_N$ | Independent observations (samples) | depends on measurement (e.g., volts, bits) |
| $\theta$ | True but unknown parameter (e.g., mean signal power) | same as $x_i$ |
| $\hat\theta = g(x_1,\dots ,x_N)$ | **Estimator** – a deterministic function of the data | same as $\theta$ |
| $\hat\theta_{\text{obs}}$ | **Estimate** – numerical value obtained after data are realized | same as $\theta$ |
| $N$ | Sample size (dimensionless) | – |
| $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ | Expectation operator (average over the sampling distribution) | – |
| $\operatorname{Var}[\cdot]$ | Variance | $(\text{unit})^2$ |

An estimator is a random variable because it depends on random samples. Its **bias** and **variance** are the two most common measures of quality:

$$
\text{Bias}(\hat\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]-\theta,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}\!\big[(\hat\theta-\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta])^{2}\big].
$$

The **mean‑squared error** (MSE) combines both:

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) \triangleq \mathbb{E}\!\big[(\hat\theta-\theta)^{2}\big]
= \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)+\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)^{2}.
$$

An estimator with zero bias is called **unbiased**. Unbiasedness is desirable but not sufficient; a low‑variance estimator is also needed.

---

### 2. Common estimation methods

1. **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).**  
   Given a likelihood function $L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}f(x_i;\theta)$ (where $f$ is the pdf/pmf), the MLE $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$ solves  

   $$
   \hat\theta_{\text{ML}} = \underset{\theta}{\arg\max}\; L(\theta)
   \quad\text{or}\quad
   \hat\theta_{\text{ML}} = \underset{\theta}{\arg\max}\; \ln L(\theta).
   $$

2. **Method of Moments (MoM).**  
   Equate sample moments $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i^{k}$ to the corresponding theoretical moments $m_k(\theta)$ and solve for $\theta$.

3. **Least‑Squares / Minimum‑Mean‑Square‑Error (MMSE).**  
   For linear models $\mathbf{y}= \mathbf{H}\boldsymbol{\theta}+\mathbf{w}$, the MMSE estimator is  

   $$
   \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MMSE}} = 
   (\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{y},
   $$

   assuming $\mathbf{w}$ is zero‑mean Gaussian with known covariance.

---

### 3. Performance limits

The **Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)** gives a fundamental limit on the variance of any unbiased estimator:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \ge \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)},
$$

where the **Fisher information** is  

$$
\mathcal{I}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln f(X;\theta)\right)^{2}
\right].
$$

If an unbiased estimator attains the bound, it is called **efficient**; the MLE often reaches the CRLB asymptotically (as $N\to\infty$).

---

### 4. Worked example – estimating the mean of a Gaussian

Assume $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^{2})$ i.i.d., with known variance $\sigma^{2}=4$ V$^2$. We collect $N=10$ samples and use the sample mean

$$
\hat\mu = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i
$$

as estimator.

*Bias:*  
$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat\mu]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[x_i]=\mu \;\Rightarrow\;
\text{Bias}=0.
$$

*Variance:*  
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Var}(x_i)
= \frac{1}{N^{2}} N\sigma^{2}= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}
= \frac{4}{10}=0.4\;\text{V}^{2}.
$$

*CRLB:* For a Gaussian mean, $\mathcal{I}(\mu)=N/\sigma^{2}=10/4=2.5$, hence  

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) \ge \frac{1}{2.5}=0.4\;\text{V}^{2},
$$

so the sample mean is **efficient** (it meets the bound).

If the observed samples are $\{5.2,\,4.8,\,5.5,\,5.0,\,4.9,\,5.1,\,5.3,\,4.7,\,5.4,\,5.0\}$ V, the numerical **estimate** is  

$$
\hat\mu_{\text{obs}} = \frac{1}{10}\sum x_i = 5.09\;\text{V}.
$$

The MSE equals the variance (0.4 V$^2$) because the estimator is unbiased.

---

### 5. Practical notes & common pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Confusing estimator and estimate** | The estimator is a random function; the estimate is a single number after data are realized. | Keep the notation $\hat\theta$ (estimator) vs. $\hat\theta_{\text{obs}}$ (realized value). |
| **Using sample variance with denominator $N$** | Leads to a biased variance estimator; the unbiased version uses $N-1$. | Apply Bessel’s correction: $s^{2}= \frac{1}{N-1}\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^{2}$. |
| **Assuming unbiasedness without proof** | Some popular estimators (e.g., ML for variance of a Gaussian) are biased for finite $N$. | Derive bias analytically or use bias‑corrected formulas. |
| **Misinterpreting confidence intervals** | A 95 % confidence interval does *not* guarantee the true parameter lies inside with 95 % probability after the interval is fixed. | View the interval as a random set that contains the true parameter in 95 % of repeated experiments. |
| **Neglecting independence** | Many variance formulas (e.g., $\sigma^{2}/N$) rely on i.i.d. samples. Correlated measurements inflate variance. | Model correlation (e.g., via covariance matrix) and use the generalized CRLB. |

Understanding these concepts equips telecom engineers to design adaptive receivers, schedule resources, and evaluate network KPIs with statistically sound rigor.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following definitions correctly describes the bias of an estimator $\hat\theta$?

- A. $\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]-\theta$
- B. $\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)$
- C. $\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\big[(\hat\theta-\theta)^{2}\big]}$
- D. $\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\hat\theta-\theta$ (after the data are observed)

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。By definition bias is the difference between the estimator’s expected value and the true parameter.

</details>

**2.** For i.i.d. Gaussian samples $x_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$ with known variance $\sigma^{2}$, the Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB) for estimating the mean $\mu$ is

- A. $\displaystyle \frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}$
- B. $\displaystyle \sigma^{2}$
- C. $\displaystyle \frac{N}{\sigma^{2}}$
- D. $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}N}$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。The Fisher information for the mean is $N/\sigma^{2}$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu)\ge 1/(N/\sigma^{2})=\sigma^{2}/N$.

</details>

**3.** You collect $N=25$ independent samples from a Gaussian distribution with unknown mean $\mu$ and known variance $\sigma^{2}=9\;\text{V}^{2}$. What is the variance (in V$^2$) of the unbiased sample‑mean estimator $\hat\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum x_i$?


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

The variance of the sample mean for i.i.d. Gaussian data is  
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}.
$$  
Insert the given numbers:  
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu)=\frac{9\;\text{V}^{2}}{25}=0.36\;\text{V}^{2}.
$$  
$\boxed{0.36}$

</details>

**4.** Eight i.i.d. observations from an exponential distribution are $\{0.5,\,0.8,\,0.3,\,0.6,\,0.9,\,0.4,\,0.7,\,0.2\}$ (units: seconds). Using the method of moments, estimate the rate parameter $\lambda$ (units: s$^{-1}$).


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

For an exponential distribution the theoretical mean is $1/\lambda$.  
1. Compute the sample mean:  
$$
\bar{x}= \frac{0.5+0.8+0.3+0.6+0.9+0.4+0.7+0.2}{8}
      = \frac{4.4}{8}=0.55\ \text{s}.
$$  
2. Equate the sample mean to the theoretical mean: $ \bar{x}=1/\lambda$.  
3. Solve for $\lambda$: $\lambda = 1/\bar{x}=1/0.55\approx1.8181818\ \text{s}^{-1}$.  
$\boxed{1.8181818}$

</details>
