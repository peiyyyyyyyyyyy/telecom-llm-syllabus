# 3.5.4 隨機過程：卜瓦松過程、馬可夫鏈、高斯過程

所屬：[3.5 基礎數理](../3-5.md)

## 正文

Random processes are mathematical models for quantities that evolve unpredictably over time or space. In telecommunications they appear everywhere: packet arrivals at a router, hand‑over states of a mobile user, fading envelopes of a wireless channel, and noise samples in a receiver. A solid grasp of the three canonical processes—Poisson, Markov, and Gaussian—allows engineers to design queueing systems, predict reliability, and apply optimal estimators.

### Poisson Process

A **Poisson process** $\{N(t),t\ge 0\}$ is a counting process that satisfies  

1. **$N(0)=0$**.  
2. **Independent increments**: for any $0\le t_0<t_1<\dots<t_k$, the random variables $N(t_1)-N(t_0),\dots,N(t_k)-N(t_{k-1})$ are mutually independent.  
3. **Stationary increments**: the distribution of $N(t+s)-N(s)$ depends only on the length $t$, not on $s$.  

If the **rate** (average arrivals per unit time) is $\lambda\;[\text{events·s}^{-1}]$, then for any $t\ge0$  

$$
\Pr\{N(t)=k\}= \frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!}\,e^{-\lambda t},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots
\tag{1}
$$

The **inter‑arrival time** $T_i$ between successive events is exponential:

$$
f_{T}(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t},\;t\ge0,\qquad
\mathbb{E}[T]=\frac{1}{\lambda}.
\tag{2}
$$

### Discrete‑Time Markov Chain (DTMC)

A **Markov chain** is a sequence of random variables $\{X_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ taking values in a finite or countable state set $\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,M\}$ such that

$$
\Pr\{X_{n+1}=j \mid X_n=i, X_{n-1}=i_{n-1},\dots\}= \Pr\{X_{n+1}=j \mid X_n=i\}=p_{ij},
\tag{3}
$$

where $p_{ij}$ are the **one‑step transition probabilities**. Collecting them yields the **transition matrix**  

$$
\mathbf{P}= \begin{bmatrix}
p_{11}&\dots&p_{1M}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{M1}&\dots&p_{MM}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
p_{ij}\ge0,\;\sum_{j=1}^{M}p_{ij}=1.
\tag{4}
$$

The probability of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ in exactly $n$ steps is the $(i,j)$ element of $\mathbf{P}^n$, denoted $p^{(n)}_{ij}$.

A **stationary (steady‑state) distribution** $\boldsymbol{\pi}=[\pi_1,\dots,\pi_M]$ satisfies  

$$
\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{P}= \boldsymbol{\pi},\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{M}\pi_i=1.
\tag{5}
$$

If the chain is **irreducible** and **aperiodic**, $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ is unique and $\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}^n$ has each row equal to $\boldsymbol{\pi}$.

### Gaussian Process

A stochastic process $\{X(t),t\in\mathcal{T}\}$ is **Gaussian** if every finite collection $\bigl(X(t_1),\dots,X(t_n)\bigr)$ is jointly normally distributed. It is completely characterized by  

* **Mean function** $m(t)=\mathbb{E}[X(t)]$ (unit of $X$).  
* **Covariance function** $k(t,s)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(X(t)-m(t))(X(s)-m(s))\bigr]$ (unit$^2$).

Thus  

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
X(t_1)\\\vdots\\ X(t_n)
\end{pmatrix}
\sim \mathcal{N}\!\Bigl(\,\mathbf{m},\;\mathbf{K}\,\Bigr),
\qquad
\mathbf{m}=[m(t_1),\dots,m(t_n)]^{\!\top},
\;
\mathbf{K}_{ij}=k(t_i,t_j).
\tag{6}
$$

A widely used special case is **Brownian motion (Wiener process)** with $m(t)=0$ and  

$$
k(t,s)=\sigma^2\min(t,s),
\tag{7}
$$

where $\sigma^2$ is the variance growth rate (unit$^2\,$·s$^{-1}$).

### Worked Example – Poisson Arrival in a Packet Scheduler

A base‑station receives uplink packets according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=2.5\;$packets·s$^{-1}$.  

**Question:** What is the probability that exactly three packets arrive during a 1‑second scheduling interval?

**Solution:** Use (1) with $t=1$ s and $k=3$.

$$
\Pr\{N(1)=3\}= \frac{(2.5\cdot1)^3}{3!}\,e^{-2.5}
= \frac{15.625}{6}\,e^{-2.5}
\approx 2.6042\,\times\,0.08208
\approx 0.213.
$$

So the scheduler sees three packets with probability **21.3 %**.

### Practical Notes & Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it occurs | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Treating any arrival stream as Poisson | Real traffic often exhibits burstiness (self‑similarity) → variance ≠ mean | Perform a **dispersion test**; if variance ≫ mean, consider a **Markov‑modulated Poisson** or **Pareto** model. |
| Forgetting to normalize the stationary vector | Solving $\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\pi}$ yields eigenvectors; missing $\sum\pi_i=1$ leads to non‑probabilistic values | After solving the linear equations, divide by the sum of components. |
| Assuming a Gaussian process is *wide‑sense stationary* automatically | Stationarity requires $m(t)=\text{const}$ and $k(t,s)=k(t-s)$. Many channel models (e.g., fading with Doppler) are non‑stationary over long intervals. | Verify the two conditions explicitly; if not satisfied, work with the *non‑stationary* covariance. |
| Mixing units in rate $\lambda$ and time $t$ | Using $\lambda$ in packets·min$^{-1}$ with $t$ in seconds yields wrong exponent in (1). | Convert both to the same time unit before substitution. |

Understanding these three processes equips telecom engineers to model packet flows, predict state evolution of mobility management, and design optimal filters for Gaussian‑distributed noise and fading. The same mathematical tools—probability mass functions, matrix powers, and multivariate normals—appear repeatedly across the discipline, making mastery of this subsection a cornerstone of modern communications theory.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following statements is *always* true for a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$?

- A. The inter‑arrival times are identically distributed but not independent.
- B. The number of arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent.
- C. The variance of $N(t)$ equals $\lambda t^2$.
- D. The probability of zero arrivals in a time $t$ is $1-\lambda t$.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。Independent increments are a defining property; the other statements are false (inter‑arrival times are independent, variance equals mean, and $P\{N(t)=0\}=e^{-\lambda t}$).

</details>

**2.** For a discrete‑time Markov chain with transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$, the stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ must satisfy which additional condition besides $\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\pi}$?

- A. $\sum_i \pi_i = 0$
- B. $\pi_i \ge 0$ for all $i$
- C. $\pi_i \le 1$ for all $i$
- D. $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：D。A probability vector must sum to one; non‑negativity follows automatically from the linear equations for an irreducible chain.

</details>

**3.** A Gaussian process $X(t)$ has mean $m(t)=0$ and covariance $k(t,s)=\sigma^2\min(t,s)$. Which classic process does this describe?

- A. Poisson counting process
- B. Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck process
- C. Wiener (Brownian motion) process
- D. Rayleigh fading process

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。The covariance $\sigma^2\min(t,s)$ is the defining kernel of Wiener (Brownian) motion.

</details>

**4.** A Poisson process has rate $\lambda = 4\;$events·s$^{-1}$. Compute the probability that exactly five events occur in a 0.8 s interval. (Answer as a decimal.)


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

Mean number of events: $\mu = \lambda t = 4 \times 0.8 = 3.2$.  
Use Poisson pmf: $P(N=5)=\frac{\mu^{5}}{5!}e^{-\mu}$.  
Compute $\mu^{5}=3.2^{5}=335.54432$.  
$5! = 120$.  
So $P = \frac{335.54432}{120}e^{-3.2}=2.7962027\,e^{-3.2}$.  
$e^{-3.2}\approx 0.0407622$.  
Thus $P \approx 2.7962027 \times 0.0407622 = 0.1139$.  
$\boxed{0.1139}$

</details>

**5.** A two‑state Markov chain has transition matrix  
$$
\mathbf{P}= \begin{bmatrix}
0.7 & 0.3\\
0.2 & 0.8
\end{bmatrix}.
$$  
Find the probability of being in state 2 after two steps, given that the chain starts in state 1 at step 0. (Answer as a decimal.)


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

The two‑step transition matrix is $\mathbf{P}^2 = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}$.  
Compute:  

Row 1:  
$(0.7)(0.7)+(0.3)(0.2)=0.49+0.06=0.55$ (to state 1)  
$(0.7)(0.3)+(0.3)(0.8)=0.21+0.24=0.45$ (to state 2)  

Row 2: not needed.  

Thus $p^{(2)}_{12}=0.45$.  
$\boxed{0.45}$

</details>
