## 正文

In a wireless link the *receiver sensitivity* (also called **minimum detectable signal**, MDS) is the lowest input power at the antenna terminals that still yields an acceptable quality of service. It directly determines how far a transmitter can be placed, how much transmit power is needed, and which modulation and coding schemes (MCS) are viable. In practical networks the sensitivity figure appears in equipment datasheets and is used by link‑budget calculators to predict coverage, capacity, and interference margins.

---

### 1. Fundamental definition  

The sensitivity is defined as the minimum received power, $P_{\text{rx,min}}$, that satisfies a required signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) for the target MCS:

$$
P_{\text{rx,min}} = N_0 \, B \; \times \; \text{NF}_{\text{lin}} \; \times \; \text{SNR}_{\text{req,lin}}
\tag{1}
$$

where  

* $N_0 = k T$ – *thermal noise power spectral density* (W/Hz)  
  * $k = 1.380\,649\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ (Boltzmann constant)  
  * $T$ – system noise temperature in kelvin (standard reference: $T_0 = 290\ \text{K}$)  

* $B$ – receiver *effective bandwidth* in hertz (Hz). This is the bandwidth over which the signal is processed (e.g., the channel bandwidth).  

* $\text{NF}_{\text{lin}}$ – *noise figure* expressed as a linear ratio (dimensionless). It quantifies the degradation of the signal‑to‑noise ratio caused by the receiver front‑end. In decibels, $\text{NF}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(\text{NF}_{\text{lin}})$.  

* $\text{SNR}_{\text{req,lin}}$ – the *minimum required* linear SNR for the chosen MCS. In dB, $\text{SNR}_{\text{req,dB}} = 10\log_{10}(\text{SNR}_{\text{req,lin}})$.  

Equation (1) is often expressed in dBm, which is convenient because most link‑budget terms are already in dB:

$$
\boxed{
P_{\text{rx,min}}[\text{dBm}] = -174\ \text{dBm} \;+\; 10\log_{10} B[\text{Hz}]
\;+\; \text{NF}_{\text{dB}} \;+\; \text{SNR}_{\text{req,dB}}
}
\tag{2}
$$

* The constant $-174\ \text{dBm}$ is the thermal‑noise floor at $T_0=290\ \text{K}$ in a 1‑Hz bandwidth: $10\log_{10}(kT_0\;1\text{Hz}) = -174.0\ \text{dBm}$.  

* All terms are additive because the dB scale turns multiplication into addition.

---

### 2. Relating to $E_b/N_0$

When the data rate $R$ (bits s$^{-1}$) is known, the required SNR can be expressed through the energy‑per‑bit to noise‑density ratio $E_b/N_0$:

$$
\text{SNR}_{\text{req,lin}} = \frac{E_b}{N_0}\;\frac{R}{B}
\tag{3}
$$

Thus, for a given $E_b/N_0$ requirement (specified by the modulation and coding), the bandwidth factor $R/B$ (spectral efficiency) scales the SNR. Substituting (3) into (1) yields an alternative sensitivity expression:

$$
P_{\text{rx,min}} = k T \, B \; \text{NF}_{\text{lin}} \; \frac{E_b}{N_0}\;\frac{R}{B}
= k T \, \text{NF}_{\text{lin}} \; \frac{E_b}{N_0} \; R
\tag{4}
$$

Equation (4) shows that, for a fixed data rate, the sensitivity is *independent of the channel bandwidth*: widening the bandwidth raises the noise floor but also reduces the required SNR proportionally.

---

### 3. Worked example  

Consider an LTE downlink operating on a 10 MHz channel ($B = 10\,\text{MHz}=1.0\times10^{7}\ \text{Hz}$). The receiver has a noise figure of $5\ \text{dB}$, and the chosen modulation (16‑QAM) with the target coding rate needs a minimum SNR of $6\ \text{dB}$.

1. **Convert all quantities to dB**  

   * $10\log_{10} B = 10\log_{10}(1.0\times10^{7}) = 70\ \text{dB}$  
   * $\text{NF}_{\text{dB}} = 5\ \text{dB}$  
   * $\text{SNR}_{\text{req,dB}} = 6\ \text{dB}$  

2. **Apply the dBm formula (2)**  

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\text{rx,min}}[\text{dBm}] &= -174\ \text{dBm} + 70\ \text{dB} + 5\ \text{dB} + 6\ \text{dB} \\
&= -93\ \text{dBm}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence the receiver must see at least **–93 dBm** to reliably demodulate 16‑QAM on a 10 MHz LTE carrier.

If the same device were used on a narrow 200 kHz narrowband IoT channel, the bandwidth term becomes $10\log_{10}(2\times10^{5}) = 53\ \text{dB}$, and the sensitivity improves to $-174 + 53 + 5 + 6 = -110\ \text{dBm}$. This illustrates why low‑rate, narrowband systems can achieve dramatically larger coverage.

---

### 4. Practical notes and common pitfalls  

| Pitfall | Why it matters | Correct practice |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| **Omitting the noise figure** | NF can add several dB to the noise floor; ignoring it yields an overly optimistic sensitivity. | Always add the NF (in dB) to the thermal‑noise term. |
| **Using the *channel* bandwidth instead of the *receiver* (filter) bandwidth** | A receiver may filter a wider spectrum (e.g., adjacent‑channel leakage) than the nominal channel width, raising the effective $B$. | Use the actual equivalent noise bandwidth (ENB) of the front‑end filter. |
| **Mixing linear and dB units** | Adding a linear ratio to a dB value is mathematically invalid and leads to large errors. | Convert all multiplicative terms to dB before addition, or keep everything linear and multiply. |
| **Assuming a fixed SNR requirement for all MCS** | Higher‑order constellations need higher SNR; using a generic value underestimates the required power. | Look up the SNR (or $E_b/N_0$) threshold for the specific modulation and coding rate. |
| **Neglecting temperature variations** | $kT$ changes with ambient temperature; a hot environment raises the noise floor by ≈ 0.6 dB per 10 K. | For design margins, add a temperature margin (e.g., +10 K → +0.1 dB). |

Finally, remember that the sensitivity specified by a vendor is usually measured under *ideal* laboratory conditions (e.g., 0 dB input VSWR, perfectly matched impedance). Real deployments must allocate additional link margin (often 3–6 dB) to cover implementation loss, fading, and interference.

## 練習題

**1.** Which term in the receiver‑sensitivity equation accounts for the degradation introduced by the receiver’s own components?

- A. Thermal noise floor $-174\ \text{dBm}$
- B. Bandwidth term $10\log_{10}B$
- C. Noise figure (NF)
- D. Required SNR

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。NF quantifies the extra noise added by the receiver front‑end beyond the thermal noise.

</details>

**2.** Increasing the receiver noise figure from 3 dB to 8 dB will have which effect on the calculated sensitivity?

- A. Improve (more negative) by 5 dB
- B. Degrade (less negative) by 5 dB
- C. No effect, because NF is cancelled by SNR
- D. Degrade by 3 dB only

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。Sensitivity adds NF in dB; raising NF by 5 dB makes the required received power 5 dB higher (less negative).

</details>

**3.** A 2 GHz narrowband system uses a 200 kHz receiver bandwidth, a noise figure of 4 dB, and requires a minimum SNR of 9 dB. Compute the receiver sensitivity in dBm.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Thermal‑noise floor: $-174\ \text{dBm}$.  
2. Bandwidth term: $10\log_{10}(200\,000)=10\log_{10}(2\times10^{5})=10(\log_{10}2+5)=10(0.3010+5)=53.01\ \text{dB}$.  
3. Add NF: $4\ \text{dB}$.  
4. Add required SNR: $9\ \text{dB}$.  
5. Sum: $-174 + 53.01 + 4 + 9 = -107.99\ \text{dBm}$.  
Rounded to one decimal place: $\boxed{-108.0}$

</details>

**4.** A receiver must achieve a sensitivity of –95 dBm on a 15 MHz channel with a required SNR of 7 dB. Assuming a temperature of 290 K, what is the maximum allowable noise figure (in dB)?


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Compute the bandwidth contribution: $10\log_{10}(15\times10^{6}) = 10(\log_{10}15 + 6) = 10(1.1761+6)=71.761\ \text{dB}$.  
2. Rearrange (2): $\text{NF}_{\text{dB}} = P_{\text{rx,min}} - (-174) - 10\log_{10}B - \text{SNR}_{\text{req}}$  
   = $(-95) - (-174) - 71.761 - 7$  
   = $79 - 71.761 - 7$  
   = $0.239\ \text{dB}$.  
3. Therefore the NF must be ≤ 0.24 dB.  
Result: $\boxed{0.24}$

</details>
