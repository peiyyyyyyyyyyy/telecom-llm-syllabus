## 正文

In the design of circuit‑switched and packet‑switched access networks, engineers must know how likely a new call (or session request) will be denied because all resources are occupied. Two classical formulas—Erlang B and Erlang C—provide these probabilities under well‑defined assumptions. Erlang B gives the *blocking probability* of a **loss system** (no waiting room), while Erlang C yields the *waiting (or delay) probability* of a **queueing system** with an infinite buffer. Their results drive capacity planning for telephone exchanges, cellular base stations, and modern voice‑over‑IP (VoIP) gateways.

---

### 1. Traffic intensity (Erlangs)

The offered traffic is expressed in **Erlangs**, a dimensionless unit defined as  

$$
E = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}\;[\text{Erl}]
$$

where  

* $\lambda$ – mean arrival rate of calls (calls · s$^{-1}$).  
* $\mu$ – mean service rate of a single server (calls · s$^{-1}$).  

Equivalently, if the average call holding time is $H$ seconds, $\mu = 1/H$ and  

$$
E = \lambda H .
$$

---

### 2. Erlang B – Blocking probability

Erlang B models a **pure loss system** (often denoted M/M/$c$/$c$), where $c$ identical servers (or channels) are available and a call that finds all $c$ busy is *cleared* (lost) immediately. The blocking probability $B(E,c)$ is  

$$
\boxed{%
B(E,c)=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{E^{c}}{c!}}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{c}\frac{E^{k}}{k!}}
}
\tag{1}
$$

All symbols are dimensionless; $c$ is an integer $\ge 1$. Because the factorial terms grow rapidly, a numerically stable recursive form is preferred:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(E,1) &= \frac{E}{1+E},\\[4pt]
B(E,c) &= \frac{E\,B(E,c-1)}{c + E\,B(E,c-1)},\qquad c\ge 2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
$$

Equation (2) requires only the previous value and avoids large intermediate numbers.

*Interpretation*: $B(E,c)$ is the probability that an arriving call is blocked and therefore lost. The **grade of service (GoS)** commonly quoted by operators is simply $B$ expressed as a percentage.

---

### 3. Erlang C – Waiting probability

When the system permits an unlimited queue (M/M/$c$/∞), blocked calls are placed in a FIFO line. The probability that an arriving call **must wait** before service begins is the Erlang C probability $C(E,c)$:

$$
\boxed{%
C(E,c)=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{E^{c}}{c!}\,\frac{c}{c-E}}%
{\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{c-1}\frac{E^{k}}{k!}+\frac{E^{c}}{c!}\,\frac{c}{c-E}}
}
\qquad (E<c)
\tag{3}
$$

The condition $E<c$ (offered load less than the number of servers) guarantees a finite average waiting time; otherwise the queue would diverge.

A convenient relationship with Erlang B is  

$$
\boxed{%
C(E,c)=\frac{B(E,c)}{1-\frac{E}{c}\bigl(1-B(E,c)\bigr)}
}
\tag{4}
$$

Once $C$ is known, the **average waiting time in queue** $W_q$ (seconds) follows from the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula for the M/M/$c$ model:

$$
\boxed{%
W_q = \frac{C}{c\mu - \lambda}
}
\tag{5}
$$

The **probability of immediate service** is $1-C$.

---

### 4. Worked example

A small cellular sector handles an average of $\lambda = 0.15$ call attempts per second. The mean call duration is $H = 120\,$s, so $\mu = 1/H = 0.00833\,$s$^{-1}$. The sector provides $c = 10$ voice channels.

1. **Compute offered traffic**  

   $$
   E = \lambda H = 0.15 \times 120 = 18\;\text{Erlangs}.
   $$

2. **Blocking probability (Erlang B)** – using the recursion (2):

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   B_1 &= \frac{18}{1+18}=0.9474,\\
   B_2 &= \frac{18\,B_1}{2+18\,B_1}=0.8955,\\
   &\;\;\vdots\\
   B_{10}&\approx 0.494.
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Hence, about **49.4 %** of call attempts will be blocked.

3. **Waiting probability (Erlang C)** – first verify $E<c$; here $18>10$, so the infinite‑queue model is **not applicable**. If the traffic were reduced to $E=6$ Erlangs (e.g., by traffic shaping), the calculation would be:

   * Compute $B(6,8) \approx 0.122$ (using recursion).  
   * Apply (4):  

     $$
     C = \frac{0.122}{1-\frac{6}{8}(1-0.122)}\approx 0.358.
     $$

   Thus, **35.8 %** of calls would have to wait, and the average waiting time would be  

   $$
   W_q = \frac{0.358}{8\mu-\lambda}
        = \frac{0.358}{8(0.00833)-0.15}
        \approx 7.5\;\text{s}.
   $$

---

### 5. Common pitfalls and practical notes

| Pitfall | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Using Erlang B when a queue exists** | Erlang B assumes cleared calls; it underestimates delay and overestimates loss. | Verify system architecture; apply Erlang C if a buffer is present. |
| **Applying Erlang C with $E \ge c$** | The denominator in (3) becomes zero, implying infinite waiting time. | Reduce load (traffic shaping) or increase $c$ before using Erlang C. |
| **Neglecting unit consistency** | $E$ must be dimensionless; mixing seconds with minutes yields wrong traffic. | Convert holding time to seconds (or any consistent unit) before computing $E$. |
| **Rounding intermediate factorials** | Large $c$ leads to overflow; rounding early destroys accuracy. | Use the recursive formulation (2) or logarithmic implementations. |
| **Assuming Poisson arrivals for all traffic** | Erlang formulas rely on Poisson (memoryless) arrivals; bursty traffic violates this. | For highly variable traffic, consider Engset, Gaussian, or simulation models. |

In practice, network planners often pre‑compute Erlang B tables for a range of $E$ and $c$ values, then interpolate. Modern tools embed the recursion directly, guaranteeing precision even for $c>1000$.
