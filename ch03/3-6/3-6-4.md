## 正文

When solving telecommunications problems, the numerical result is only half the story. The way the answer is *presented*—its unit, number of significant figures, and the notation used—directly affects how the result can be interpreted, compared, and used in subsequent design steps. In a real network, a misplaced decimal point or an inconsistent number of significant digits can lead to under‑ or over‑provisioning of resources, causing costly performance degradations or unnecessary capital expenditure. This subsection explains the conventions that make answers both *accurate* and *communicable*.

---

### 1. Units and Notation  

| Quantity | Preferred Unit | Symbol | Typical Range in Practice |
|----------|----------------|--------|---------------------------|
| Power (linear) | Watt (W) | $P$ | $10^{-12}\,$W – $10^{3}\,$W |
| Power (log) | Decibel‑milliwatt (dBm) | $P_{\rm dBm}$ | $-150\,$dBm – $+50\,$dBm |
| Frequency | Hertz (Hz) | $f$ | $10^{6}\,$Hz – $10^{12}\,$Hz |
| Bandwidth | Hertz (Hz) | $B$ | $10^{3}\,$Hz – $10^{9}\,$Hz |
| Distance | Meter (m) | $d$ | $10^{-2}\,$m – $10^{7}\,$m |
| Gain (antenna) | Decibel‑isotropic (dBi) | $G$ | $-5$ – $30$ dBi |
| Loss (cable, filter, etc.) | Decibel (dB) | $L$ | $0$ – $30$ dB |
| Capacity | bit/s (bps) or Mbit/s (Mbps) | $C$ | $10^{3}$ – $10^{9}$ bps |

*Never mix linear and logarithmic units in the same expression.* Convert to the same domain first (e.g., all powers to dBm) and keep the unit symbol attached to the numeric value. For very large or very small numbers, scientific notation is preferred, e.g. $3.2\times10^{-9}\,$W.

---

### 2. Significant Figures (SF)  

A **significant figure** is any digit that contributes to the precision of a measurement. The rule of thumb in telecommunications calculations is:

1. **Identify the least‑precise input** (fewest SF).  
2. **Carry out the arithmetic with full internal precision** (use double‑precision floating point in a calculator or software).  
3. **Round the final answer** to the same number of SF as the least‑precise input *unless* the calculation involves a logarithmic conversion, in which case the SF rule is applied to the linear quantity *before* conversion.

**Examples of input precision**

* 10 W (two SF) → $P_{\rm t}=40\,$dBm (exact conversion, no loss of SF).  
* 2 dBi (one SF) → treat as $2.0$ dBi for rounding purposes.  
* 5 km (one SF) → the distance dominates the final SF.

When the result is expressed in dB, the convention is to keep **one decimal place** if any of the operands had a fractional dB component; otherwise, round to the nearest whole dB.

---

### 3. Rounding Rules  

| Operation | Rule |
|-----------|------|
| **Addition / Subtraction** | Result is rounded to the *least number of decimal places* of any term. |
| **Multiplication / Division** | Result is rounded to the *fewest significant figures* of any term. |
| **Logarithmic conversion** (e.g., $P_{\rm dBm}=10\log_{10}P_{\rm W}+30$) | Compute the linear value with full precision, then round the dB result according to the SF of the original linear quantity. |
| **Exponentiation** (e.g., $10^{x}$) | The exponent $x$ must be carried with adequate precision; the final linear result follows the multiplication rule. |

---

### 4. Worked Example  

**Problem:** A base station transmits $P_{\rm t}=10\,$W (two SF) with antenna gains $G_{\rm t}=G_{\rm r}=2\,$dBi (one SF each). The carrier frequency is $f=2.4\,$GHz (two SF) and the user equipment is $d=5\,$km away (one SF). System losses are $L_{\rm s}=3\,$dB (one SF). Compute the received power $P_{\rm r}$ in dBm and present the answer with the correct number of significant figures.

**Solution steps**

1. Convert transmit power to dBm:  
   $$P_{\rm t,dBm}=10\log_{10}(10\ {\rm W})+30=40\ {\rm dBm}$$  
   (exact because 10 W → 40 dBm).

2. Compute free‑space path loss (Friis):  
   $$L_{\rm p}=20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right)$$  
   where $c=3\times10^{8}\,$m/s.  

   Substituting the *exact* values (keep full precision):  
   $$\frac{4\pi d f}{c}= \frac{4\pi \times 5\times10^{3}\times2.4\times10^{9}}{3\times10^{8}}=5.03\times10^{5}$$  
   $$L_{\rm p}=20\log_{10}(5.03\times10^{5})=114.0\ {\rm dB}$$  
   (rounded to one decimal place because the logarithm yields a fractional dB).

3. Apply the Friis link budget:  
   $$P_{\rm r,dBm}=P_{\rm t,dBm}+G_{\rm t}+G_{\rm r}-L_{\rm p}-L_{\rm s}$$  
   $$=40+2+2-114.0-3 = -73.0\ {\rm dBm}$$  

4. **Determine the number of significant figures**  
   The least‑precise input is the distance ($5\,$km, one SF). Consequently, the final answer is limited to **one significant figure** in the linear domain. After conversion to dBm, the convention is to round to the nearest whole dB. Hence the answer is reported as  

   $$\boxed{-73\ {\rm dBm}}$$  

   If the answer were required in watts, we would first convert:  
   $$P_{\rm r,W}=10^{\frac{-73-30}{10}}=5.0\times10^{-11}\ {\rm W}$$  
   and keep **one SF**, i.e., $5\times10^{-11}\,$W.

---

### 5. Common Mistakes and Practical Tips  

| Mistake | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Mixing linear and dB units** (e.g., adding $10\,$W to $3\,$dB) | Produces mathematically meaningless results. | Convert *all* quantities to the same domain before arithmetic. |
| **Rounding intermediate results** | Propagates rounding error, often leading to an answer with too few SF. | Keep full calculator precision until the final step. |
| **Ignoring the least‑precise input** | Over‑states accuracy, which can mislead design margins. | Identify the input with the fewest SF and limit the final answer accordingly. |
| **Using too many decimal places in dB** | Gives a false impression of precision; most hardware specifications are quoted to 0.1 dB or 1 dB. | Round dB results to the appropriate decimal place (usually 0.1 dB if any operand had a fractional dB). |
| **Omitting the unit** | Ambiguity between linear (W) and logarithmic (dBm) values. | Always attach the unit symbol, and when writing tables, align units in a separate column. |

**Practical note:** In engineering reports and test logs, the *standard* format is “value ± uncertainty unit” (e.g., $-73 \pm 1\,$dBm). Even when the uncertainty is not explicitly calculated, stating the implied precision (through the number of SF) communicates the confidence level to the reader.

---

By adhering to these conventions, engineers ensure that numerical results are both *correct* and *communicable* across teams, tools, and standards bodies. This disciplined approach reduces misinterpretation and supports reliable network planning and optimization.

## 練習題

**1.** When the least‑precise input of a link‑budget problem has **one significant figure** and the final received power is expressed in dBm, how should the answer be rounded?

- A. Round to the nearest whole dB.
- B. Keep two decimal places in the dBm value.
- C. Keep the same number of significant figures as the linear power (one SF) in the dBm value.
- D. Express the result in scientific notation with three significant figures.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。With a single‑SF input the dBm result is limited to the nearest whole decibel; fractional dB would imply false precision.

</details>

**2.** Which rounding rule is applied to results of **multiplication or division** in telecommunications calculations?

- A. Round to the least number of decimal places of any term.
- B. Round to the fewest significant figures of any term.
- C. Always round to three significant figures.
- D. Keep all intermediate digits and never round.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。Multiplication and division propagate relative errors, so the result is limited by the operand with the fewest significant figures.

</details>

**3.** Which statement about mixing linear and logarithmic units in the same arithmetic expression is correct?

- A. It is permissible if a conversion factor (e.g., 10 log₁₀) is explicitly written.
- B. It is allowed when the final result is expressed in linear units.
- C. It is never allowed; all terms must be in the same domain before any arithmetic operation.
- D. It is allowed for antenna gain because dBi is a “relative” unit.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。Adding or subtracting a linear quantity to a dB quantity is mathematically meaningless; all terms must be converted to either linear or logarithmic form first.

</details>

**4.** A link operates at 5 GHz over a distance of 2 km. Compute the free‑space path loss (FSPL) in dB. Use $c = 3\times10^{8}$ m/s and round the final result according to the conventions of subsection 3.6.4.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Convert distance and frequency to SI units:  
   $d = 2\ \text{km} = 2.0\times10^{3}\ \text{m}$ (one SF)  
   $f = 5\ \text{GHz} = 5.0\times10^{9}\ \text{Hz}$ (one SF)  
2. Compute the argument of the logarithm:  
   $\displaystyle X = \frac{4\pi d f}{c}
      = \frac{4\pi \times 2.0\times10^{3} \times 5.0\times10^{9}}{3.0\times10^{8}}
      = 4.19\times10^{5}$ (full precision)  
3. Take the base‑10 logarithm:  
   $\log_{10}X = \log_{10}(4.19\times10^{5}) = 5.622$  
4. Apply the FSPL formula:  
   $\displaystyle L_{\!p}=20\log_{10}X = 20 \times 5.622 = 112.44\ \text{dB}$  
5. According to the rounding rule for dB results (no operand has a fractional dB), round to the nearest whole dB:  
   $L_{\!p}=112\ \text{dB}$  
\boxed{112}

</details>

**5.** A base station transmits $P_{\text{t}} = 0.5\ \text{W}$ (two SF) with antenna gains $G_{\text{t}} = G_{\text{r}} = 3\ \text{dBi}$ (one SF each). The carrier frequency is $f = 900\ \text{MHz}$ (two SF) and the user equipment is $d = 10\ \text{km}$ away (one SF). System losses are $L_{\text{s}} = 2\ \text{dB}$ (one SF). Using the Friis link‑budget equation, compute the **received power in watts**, expressed with the correct number of significant figures.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解：

1. Convert transmit power to dBm (full precision):  
   $P_{\text{t,dBm}} = 10\log_{10}(0.5) + 30 = 26.9897\ \text{dBm}$  
2. Compute the free‑space path loss:  
   $d = 10\ \text{km} = 1.0\times10^{4}\ \text{m}$ (one SF)  
   $f = 900\ \text{MHz} = 9.0\times10^{8}\ \text{Hz}$  
   $X = \frac{4\pi d f}{c}
        = \frac{4\pi \times 1.0\times10^{4} \times 9.0\times10^{8}}{3.0\times10^{8}}
        = 3.77\times10^{5}$  
   $\log_{10}X = 5.576$  
   $L_{\!p}=20\log_{10}X = 111.52\ \text{dB}$  
3. Apply the Friis link budget (all terms in dB):  
   $P_{\text{r,dBm}} = P_{\text{t,dBm}} + G_{\text{t}} + G_{\text{r}} - L_{\!p} - L_{\text{s}}$  
   $= 26.9897 + 3 + 3 - 111.52 - 2 = -80.5303\ \text{dBm}$  
4. Round according to the least‑precise input (distance, gains, loss → one SF).  
   In dBm this means rounding to the nearest whole dB: $-81\ \text{dBm}$.  
5. Convert back to watts:  
   $P_{\text{r,W}} = 10^{\frac{-81-30}{10}} = 10^{-11.1} = 7.94\times10^{-12}\ \text{W}$  
6. Keep one significant figure (the limiting precision):  
   $P_{\text{r,W}} = 8\times10^{-12}\ \text{W}$  
\boxed{8e-12}

</details>
