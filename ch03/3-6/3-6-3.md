## 正文

In telecommunications engineering every numerical result is only as trustworthy as the units that were used to obtain it. A formula such as Shannon’s capacity law, Friis’ transmission equation, or the thermal‑noise expression $N=kTB$ is dimensionally correct only when each variable is expressed in its International System of Units (SI) counterpart. In real networks—cellular, satellite, or optical—mixing megahertz with kilohertz, decibel‑relative units with linear ratios, or kilometres with metres leads to errors that can be orders of magnitude, jeopardising link‑budget calculations, spectrum‑allocation studies, and system‑level simulations.

---

### Key definitions  

| Symbol | Quantity | SI unit | Typical engineering unit | Note |
|--------|----------|--------|--------------------------|------|
| $P$ | Power | watt (W) | milliwatt (mW), dBm | $P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}(P/1\text{ mW})$ |
| $G$ | Antenna gain | dimensionless (linear) | dBi | $G_{\text{dBi}} = 10\log_{10}G$ |
| $L$ | Path loss | dimensionless (linear) | dB | $L_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}L$ |
| $B$ | Bandwidth | hertz (Hz) | kilohertz (kHz), megahertz (MHz) | |
| $f$ | Frequency | hertz (Hz) | GHz, MHz | |
| $\lambda$ | Wavelength | metre (m) | cm, mm | $\lambda = v/f$, $v\approx 3\times10^8\,$m/s (free space) |
| $k$ | Boltzmann constant | joule per kelvin (J K$^{-1}$) | $1.380649\times10^{-23}$ J K$^{-1}$ | |
| $T$ | Absolute temperature | kelvin (K) | 290 K (standard) | |
| $N$ | Thermal noise power | watt (W) | dBm | $N=kTB$ |
| $\text{SNR}$ | Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio | dimensionless (linear) | dB | $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\text{SNR}$ |
| $C$ | Channel capacity | bit per second (bit/s) | Mbps, Gbps | $C = B\log_{2}(1+\text{SNR})$ |

**Fundamental formulas**  

1. **Thermal noise power**  
   $$ N = kTB $$  
   where $k = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\text{J K}^{-1}$, $T$ in kelvin, $B$ in hertz.  

2. **Shannon capacity** (bits per second)  
   $$ C = B\log_{2}\!\bigl(1+\text{SNR}\bigr) $$  

3. **Conversion between linear and logarithmic ratios**  
   $$ X_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}X_{\text{lin}} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X_{\text{lin}} = 10^{X_{\text{dB}}/10} $$  

All logarithmic quantities (dB, dBm, dBi) are *relative*; they must be converted to linear scale before being inserted into a formula that expects a dimensionless ratio.

---

### Worked example  

*Problem*: An LTE carrier occupies $B = 20\,$MHz and the measured signal‑to‑noise ratio is $\text{SNR}=15\,$dB. Compute the theoretical maximum data rate $C$ in megabits per second (Mbps).

*Solution*  

1. **Convert bandwidth**  
   $$ B = 20\;\text{MHz}=20\times10^{6}\;\text{Hz}. $$  

2. **Convert SNR from dB to linear**  
   $$ \text{SNR}_{\text{lin}} = 10^{15/10}=10^{1.5}=31.6228. $$  

3. **Apply Shannon’s formula**  
   $$
   C = B\log_{2}\!\bigl(1+\text{SNR}_{\text{lin}}\bigr)
     = 20\times10^{6}\;\log_{2}(1+31.6228).
   $$  
   Compute the logarithm: $\log_{2}(32.6228) \approx 5.036$.  

4. **Result in bits per second**  
   $$
   C \approx 20\times10^{6}\times5.036 = 1.0072\times10^{8}\;\text{bit/s}.
   $$  

5. **Convert to Mbps**  
   $$
   C_{\text{Mbps}} = \frac{1.0072\times10^{8}}{10^{6}} \approx 100.7\;\text{Mbps}.
   $$

$$
\boxed{100.7\ \text{Mbps}}
$$

Key observations: the bandwidth was first expressed in hertz, the SNR was converted from decibels to a pure number, and only then was the logarithm evaluated. Any deviation—e.g., using MHz directly—would have yielded a result off by a factor of $10^{6}$.

---

### Common mistakes and practical notes  

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| Adding dB values directly to linear quantities (e.g., $P_{\text{dBm}}+G_{\text{dBi}}-L_{\text{dB}}$ and then treating the result as a watt) | Decibel arithmetic is valid only when *all* terms are expressed in dB. | Keep the whole link‑budget in dB, then convert the final result to watts with $P_{\text{W}} = 10^{(P_{\text{dBm}}-30)/10}$. |
| Forgetting to convert MHz → Hz before using $C = B\log_{2}(1+\text{SNR})$ | The logarithm expects a pure number; the bandwidth factor must be in hertz. | Write a personal checklist: “Convert all frequencies and bandwidths to Hz”. |
| Using $k = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K but plugging $B$ in kHz without conversion | $kTB$ yields watts only when $B$ is in hertz. | Multiply $B$ by $10^{3}$ (kHz→Hz) or $10^{6}$ (MHz→Hz) before the multiplication. |
| Mixing temperature units (°C vs K) in the noise formula | $T$ must be absolute temperature in kelvin. | Convert $°\text{C}$ to $K$ by adding $273.15$. |
| Rounding intermediate results too early (e.g., truncating $\text{SNR}_{\text{lin}}$ to 32) | Logarithmic functions are sensitive; early rounding propagates error. | Keep at least four significant figures throughout calculations; round only the final answer according to the required precision. |

**Practical tips**

* **Unit table** – Keep a small table of common prefixes (k, M, G) and their powers of ten.  
* **Calculator with unit conversion** – Many engineering calculators allow you to type “20 MHz → Hz” directly; use it to avoid manual mistakes.  
* **Consistent significant figures** – Align the number of sig‑figs with the least‑precise input; for link‑budget work, three sig‑figs are usually sufficient.  
* **Verification step** – After obtaining a result, perform a quick sanity check: does the magnitude make sense given typical network values (e.g., LTE channel capacity ≈ 100 Mbps for a 20 MHz carrier with moderate SNR)?  

By rigorously enforcing unit consistency, engineers eliminate a class of errors that are otherwise hard to trace, especially in multi‑step problems where each stage introduces its own conversion factor.

## 練習題

**1.** Which of the following is the correct conversion of a power level of **30 dBm** to watts?

- A. 1 W
- B. 0.001 W
- C. 0.01 W
- D. 10 W

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。0 dBm = 1 mW, so 30 dB higher is $10^{30/10}=10^{3}=1000$ mW = 1 W.

</details>

**2.** In a link‑budget calculation, which statement is *always* true?

- A. Adding a gain expressed in dBi to a loss expressed in dB yields a linear ratio.
- B. The sum of quantities expressed in dB can be directly interpreted as a power in watts.
- C. Converting all quantities to linear units before performing arithmetic avoids unit‑consistency errors.
- D. Path loss expressed in dB must be multiplied by antenna gain in dBi to obtain the net loss.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：C。Performing arithmetic on linear (ratio) values guarantees dimensionally correct results; dB quantities are only additive when all terms share the same logarithmic reference.

</details>

**3.** When applying Shannon’s capacity formula $C = B\log_{2}(1+\text{SNR})$, the bandwidth $B$ must be expressed in:

- A. Hertz (Hz)
- B. Kilohertz (kHz)
- C. Megahertz (MHz)
- D. Any of the above, as long as the same unit is used for $B$ and $C$

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。The formula yields bits per second; therefore $B$ must be in hertz so that the product has units of bit/s.

</details>

**4.** A communication channel has a bandwidth of **10 MHz** and an SNR of **20 dB**. Compute the theoretical Shannon capacity in **Mbps**. (Assume ideal conditions.)


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Convert bandwidth: $B = 10\;\text{MHz}=10\times10^{6}\;\text{Hz}$.  
2. Convert SNR to linear: $\text{SNR}_{\text{lin}} = 10^{20/10}=10^{2}=100$.  
3. Apply Shannon: $C = B\log_{2}(1+100) = 10\times10^{6}\,\log_{2}(101)$.  
4. $\log_{2}(101)=\frac{\ln 101}{\ln 2}\approx\frac{4.6151205}{0.69314718}=6.658211$.  
5. $C = 10\times10^{6}\times6.658211 = 66.58211\times10^{6}\;\text{bit/s}$.  
6. Convert to Mbps: $C_{\text{Mbps}} = 66.58211\;\text{Mbps}$.  
$\boxed{66.58211}$

</details>

**5.** In a wireless link, the transmitter outputs **5 W**, the antenna gain is **15 dBi**, the free‑space path loss is **120 dB**, the receiver bandwidth is **2 MHz**, the receiver noise figure is **5 dB**, and the ambient temperature is **290 K**. Determine the received SNR in **dB**.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Convert transmit power to dBm: $P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}(5\;\text{W}/1\text{ mW}) = 10\log_{10}(5000)=10\times3.69897=36.9897\;\text{dBm}\approx 37\;\text{dBm}$.  
2. Add antenna gain: $P_{\text{dBm,\,ant}} = 37 + 15 = 52\;\text{dBm}$.  
3. Subtract path loss: $P_{\text{rx,dBm}} = 52 - 120 = -68\;\text{dBm}$.  
4. Compute thermal noise power: $N = kTB = (1.380649\times10^{-23})(290)(2\times10^{6}) = 8.004\times10^{-15}\;\text{W}$.  
5. Convert noise power to dBm: $N_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}(8.004\times10^{-15}/1\times10^{-3}) = 10\log_{10}(8.004\times10^{-12})$.  
   $\log_{10}(8.004\times10^{-12}) = \log_{10}(8.004) -12 = 0.90309 -12 = -11.09691$.  
   $N_{\text{dBm}} = 10\times(-11.09691) = -110.9691\;\text{dBm}\approx -111\;\text{dBm}$.  
6. Include receiver noise figure: $N_{\text{total,dBm}} = -111 + 5 = -106\;\text{dBm}$.  
7. SNR (dB) = $P_{\text{rx,dBm}} - N_{\text{total,dBm}} = -68 - (-106) = 38\;\text{dB}$.  
$\boxed{38}$

</details>
