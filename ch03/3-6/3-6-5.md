## 正文

In telecommunications problem solving, verification – the act of checking that a derived numerical result is consistent with the original statement – is as important as the algebraic manipulation itself. Real‑world networks are built on margins; a single misplaced decimal or a sign error can lead to under‑‑ or over‑‑provisioned equipment, causing coverage gaps, interference, or unnecessary cost. Verification therefore serves three practical purposes:

1. **Error detection** – catching arithmetic slips, sign mistakes, or mis‑applied formulas before the result is used in design.  
2. **Physical plausibility** – ensuring that the answer lies within the range that the underlying physics permits (e.g., a received power cannot be higher than the transmitted power in a passive link).  
3. **Documentation integrity** – providing a reproducible trail that reviewers or automated grading tools can follow.

---

### Key Definitions  

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|--------|---------|------|
| $P_{\text{tx}}$ | Transmit power (reference to 1 mW) | dBm |
| $G_t$, $G_r$ | Transmit and receive antenna gains (reference to isotropic) | dBi |
| $L_p$ | Path‑loss (reference to 1) | dB |
| $P_{\text{rx}}$ | Received power (reference to 1 mW) | dBm |
| $d$ | Separation distance between antennas | m |
| $f$ | Carrier frequency | Hz |
| $\lambda$ | Wavelength, $\displaystyle \lambda = \frac{c}{f}$ | m |
| $c$ | Speed of light, $c = 3.0\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ | m/s |
| $R$ | Residual error after substitution, $R = \text{LHS} - \text{RHS}$ | same as the quantity compared |
| $\epsilon$ | Acceptable tolerance (often expressed in dB) | dB |

The **link‑budget equation** for a free‑space link is  

$$
P_{\text{rx}} = P_{\text{tx}} + G_t + G_r - L_p \tag{1}
$$

with the free‑space path‑loss given by  

$$
L_p = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right) \quad \text{[dB]} \tag{2}
$$

---

### Verification Techniques  

| Technique | How to Apply | Typical Tolerance |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Back‑substitution** | Insert the computed $P_{\text{rx}}$ (or any intermediate variable) back into the original equation (1) and evaluate the residual $R$. | $|R| \le \epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ = 0.1–1 dB for link‑budget work. |
| **Unit consistency check** | Verify that every term in (1) is expressed in dB (or dBm) and that (2) produces a dimensionless dB value. | No unit mismatch allowed. |
| **Sanity (order‑of‑magnitude) check** | Estimate $L_p$ using $L_p \approx 32.4 + 20\log_{10}(d\,[\text{km}]) + 20\log_{10}(f\,[\text{MHz}])$ and see whether the detailed calculation is within ±3 dB. | ±3 dB is a common rule of thumb. |
| **Alternative method** | Compute the same quantity with a different formula (e.g., Friis equation in linear scale) and compare results after conversion to dB. | Results should agree within the chosen $\epsilon$. |
| **Boundary‑condition test** | Set $d\to0$ (or $f\to0$) and confirm that the equation reduces to the expected physical limit (e.g., $L_p \to 0$). | Exact limit behavior must be satisfied. |

The *residual* after back‑substitution is  

$$
R = \bigl(P_{\text{tx}} + G_t + G_r - L_p\bigr)_{\text{calc}} - P_{\text{rx,\,computed}} \tag{3}
$$

A small $|R|$ indicates a correct algebraic manipulation and arithmetic execution.

---

### Worked Example  

**Problem:** A base station transmits $P_{\text{tx}} = 15\ \text{dBm}$ at $f = 1800\ \text{MHz}$ toward a handset $d = 1.5\ \text{km}$ away. The base‑station antenna gain is $G_t = 8\ \text{dBi}$ and the handset antenna gain is $G_r = 0\ \text{dBi}$. Compute the received power $P_{\text{rx}}$ and verify the result.

**Solution**

1. **Compute wavelength**  

$$
\lambda = \frac{c}{f}= \frac{3.0\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}{1.8\times10^{9}\ \text{Hz}} = 0.1667\ \text{m}
$$

2. **Free‑space path‑loss** (using (2))  

$$
L_p = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\right)
     = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi \times 1500}{0.1667}\right)
$$

$$
\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}= \frac{4\pi \times1500}{0.1667}\approx 1.13\times10^{5}
$$

$$
L_p = 20\log_{10}(1.13\times10^{5}) \approx 20 \times 5.053 = 101.1\ \text{dB}
$$

3. **Link‑budget (1)**  

$$
P_{\text{rx}} = 15\ \text{dBm} + 8\ \text{dBi} + 0\ \text{dBi} - 101.1\ \text{dB}
               = -78.1\ \text{dBm}
$$

4. **Verification – back‑substitution**  

Insert $P_{\text{rx}} = -78.1\ \text{dBm}$ into (1):

$$
\text{LHS}= -78.1\ \text{dBm}
$$
$$
\text{RHS}= 15 + 8 + 0 - 101.1 = -78.1\ \text{dBm}
$$

$$
R = \text{LHS} - \text{RHS} = 0\ \text{dB}
$$

Residual $R=0$ dB (well within any practical $\epsilon$), confirming the calculation.

5. **Sanity check** – using the simplified rule  

$$
L_p \approx 32.4 + 20\log_{10}(1.5) + 20\log_{10}(1800) 
      = 32.4 + 3.52 + 65.1 \approx 101.0\ \text{dB}
$$

The estimate (101.0 dB) matches the detailed value (101.1 dB) within 0.1 dB, providing additional confidence.

---

### Common Mistakes and Practical Notes  

| Mistake | Why it Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|----------------|--------------|
| **Neglecting the sign of $L_p$** | Treating loss as a gain in (1). | Remember that loss *subtracts* from the power budget. |
| **Mixing linear and logarithmic units** | Using watts for $P_{\text{tx}}$ while $L_p$ is in dB. | Convert all quantities to the same logarithmic reference before applying (1). |
| **Rounding too early** | Rounding $L_p$ to the nearest dB before the final subtraction inflates error. | Keep at least two decimal places through intermediate steps; round only for the final answer. |
| **Using the wrong frequency unit** | Plugging $f$ in GHz into (2) which expects Hz. | Verify that $f$ is expressed in Hz; if you use MHz, adjust the constant accordingly. |
| **Skipping the sanity check** | Accepting a result that is mathematically correct but physically impossible (e.g., $P_{\text{rx}} > P_{\text{tx}}$). | Always ask “Does this make sense given the distance and frequency?” and compare with typical link‑budget ranges. |

Finally, when documenting verification in a report or a training dataset for a language model, explicitly show the residual $R$ and state the tolerance used. This makes the reasoning transparent and enables automated graders to award partial credit for a correct verification even if the final numeric answer is slightly off due to rounding.

**1.** Which of the following is *not* a valid verification technique for a telecommunications calculation?

- A. Substituting the computed result back into the original equation

- D. Comparing the result with a known special‑case value

正解：C。Ignoring rounding errors can hide significant discrepancies; proper verification must account for them.

- D. ±10 dB

正解：C。Engineering practice usually tolerates residuals up to about ±1 dB for link‑budget verification.

**4.** A point‑to‑point microwave link operates at $f = 12\ \text{GHz}$ over a distance $d = 15\ \text{km}$. The transmitter power is $P_{\text{tx}} = 20\ \text{dBm}$, the antenna gains are $G_t = 30\ \text{dBi}$ and $G_r = 35\ \text{dBi}$. Compute the received power $P_{\text{rx}}$ in dBm using the free‑space path‑loss model, then verify by back‑substitution. Report the verified $P_{\text{rx}}$ (rounded to one decimal place).


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Compute wavelength: $\lambda = c/f = 3.0\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s} / 12\times10^{9}\ \text{Hz} = 0.025\ \text{m}$.  
2. Compute $L_p = 20\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)$.  
   $4\pi d/\lambda = 4\pi \times 15\,000\ \text{m} / 0.025\ \text{m} = 7.54\times10^{6}$.  
   $L_p = 20\log_{10}(7.54\times10^{6}) = 20 \times 6.877 = 137.5\ \text{dB}$.  
3. Link‑budget: $P_{\text{rx}} = 20 + 30 + 35 - 137.5 = -52.5\ \text{dBm}$.  
4. Verification: RHS = $20 + 30 + 35 - 137.5 = -52.5\ \text{dBm}$, same as computed, so residual $R = 0\ \text{dB}$.  
$\boxed{-52.5}$

</details>
