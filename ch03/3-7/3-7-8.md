## 正文

In packet‑switched and circuit‑switched networks, the physical and link layers must guarantee that the bits received are identical to the bits transmitted, or at least be able to detect when a corruption has occurred. The Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC) is the most widely deployed error‑detection mechanism in Ethernet, Wi‑Fi, LTE, 5G NR, satellite links, and many storage interfaces. Its popularity stems from three properties:

1. **Very high detection probability for common error patterns** (single‑bit flips, burst errors up to a prescribed length, odd‑numbered bit errors).  
2. **Simple hardware implementation** using linear‑feedback shift registers (LFSRs).  
3. **Deterministic, algebraic formulation** that can be analysed with polynomial algebra over the binary field $\mathbb{F}_2$.

---

### 1. Algebraic model

A binary message of $k$ bits is interpreted as a polynomial
$$
M(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} m_i x^{i},
\qquad m_i\in\{0,1\},
$$
where the least‑significant bit corresponds to the constant term.  

A *generator polynomial* $G(x)$ of degree $r$ (hence $r$ parity bits) is agreed upon by both transmitter and receiver. $G(x)$ must be *monic* (its highest‑order coefficient equals 1) and is usually chosen to be primitive or to contain the factor $(x+1)$ to guarantee detection of all odd‑weight errors.

The transmitted *codeword* is obtained by appending $r$ zero bits to $M(x)$, dividing the resulting polynomial by $G(x)$ using modulo‑2 arithmetic, and subtracting the remainder $R(x)$:

$$
\underbrace{M(x) x^{r}}_{\text{message shifted}} = Q(x) G(x) + R(x), \qquad \deg(R)<r .
$$

The transmitted frame is
$$
C(x)=M(x) x^{r} - R(x)=M(x) x^{r}+R(x),
$$
because subtraction and addition are identical in $\mathbb{F}_2$.

At the receiver, the received polynomial $\hat C(x)$ (ideally equal to $C(x)$) is divided by $G(x)$. If the remainder is **zero**, the frame is accepted; any non‑zero remainder signals a detected error.

---

### 2. Detection capability

Let the error pattern be $E(x)$, i.e. the received codeword is $C'(x)=C(x)+E(x)$. The receiver computes

$$
C'(x) \bmod G(x)=\bigl(C(x)+E(x)\bigr)\bmod G(x)=E(x)\bmod G(x).
$$

Thus an error goes *undetected* iff $G(x)$ divides $E(x)$. From this observation the following guarantees can be derived (see any textbook on coding theory):

| Property of $G(x)$ | Undetected error pattern |
|--------------------|--------------------------|
| $G(x)$ contains factor $(x+1)$ | All odd‑weight errors are detected. |
| $\deg(G)=r$ | All burst errors of length $\le r$ are detected. |
| $G(x)$ not divisible by $x$ | All single‑bit errors are detected. |

Consequently, a well‑chosen CRC (e.g., CRC‑32 with polynomial $0x04C11DB7$) will detect any single‑bit error, any double‑bit error, any odd‑weight error, and any burst up to 32 bits, with a residual undetected‑error probability of roughly $2^{-r}$ for random errors.

---

### 3. Worked example (CRC‑4)

Consider a 7‑bit data word $M = 1101011$ and the CRC‑4 generator polynomial

$$
G(x)=x^{4}+x^{3}+1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 11001_2 .
$$

**Step 1 – Append zeros:**  
$M(x) x^{4}=1101011\,0000$.

**Step 2 – Polynomial long division (mod‑2):**  

```
11010110000 ÷ 11001
   11001
   -----
    00111 (bring down next bit)
    00000 (since leading 0, no subtraction)
    00111
    11001
    -----
    11110
    11001
    -----
    00111
    11001
    -----
    11110
    11001
    -----
    00111 (remainder after last bit)
```

The remainder $R(x)=1110$ (four bits).  

**Step 3 – Form codeword:**  
Transmit $C = 1101011\,1110$.

**Verification at receiver:**  

Dividing $C$ by $G(x)$ yields a zero remainder, confirming correctness. If a single‑bit error flips the third bit (producing $1100011\,1110$), the division leaves a non‑zero remainder, and the error is detected.

---

### 4. Practical notes and common pitfalls

| Issue | Why it matters | Remedy |
|-------|----------------|--------|
| **Polynomial selection** | Using a generator that lacks the factor $(x+1)$ fails to detect all odd‑weight errors (e.g., many burst errors). | Adopt standardized CRC polynomials (CRC‑8, CRC‑16‑CCITT, CRC‑32‑IEEE). |
| **Bit ordering** | Some standards treat the most‑significant bit as the polynomial’s highest‑order term, others the opposite. | Consistently follow the ordering defined in the protocol specification; document the convention in the implementation. |
| **Initial/final XOR (pre‑/post‑conditioning)** | Many protocols XOR the message with a preset value before division and/or invert the remainder after division (e.g., Ethernet CRC‑32 uses initial value $0xFFFFFFFF$ and final XOR $0xFFFFFFFF$). | Implement the exact pre‑ and post‑conditioning steps; neglecting them changes the effective generator polynomial. |
| **Length mismatch** | The transmitter and receiver must agree on the same $r$ (number of CRC bits). A mismatch yields a non‑zero remainder for every frame. | Encode the CRC length in the protocol header or fix it by specification. |
| **Hardware vs. software implementation** | LFSR implementations must shift in the *same* direction as the polynomial representation; a reversed LFSR will compute a different remainder. | Verify the LFSR taps against the polynomial’s coefficients; unit‑test with known vectors. |

When these details are respected, CRCs provide a deterministic, low‑latency error‑detection layer that can be combined with retransmission protocols (ARQ, HARQ) to achieve very low residual error rates in modern telecommunications systems.

## 練習題

**1.** Which property of the generator polynomial guarantees detection of all odd‑weight error patterns?

- A. It contains the factor $(x+1)$.
- B. Its degree equals the number of CRC bits.
- C. It is not divisible by $x$.
- D. It is a primitive polynomial.

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。The factor $(x+1)$ forces the polynomial to evaluate to 1 for any odd‑weight error, so the remainder cannot be zero.

</details>

**2.** A CRC with $r = 12$ parity bits is used. What is the longest burst error length that is guaranteed to be detected?

- A. 10 bits
- B. 12 bits
- C. 13 bits
- D. 24 bits

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：B。A CRC of degree $r$ detects every burst error whose length is ≤ $r$; thus a 12‑bit CRC catches all bursts up to 12 bits.

</details>

**3.** In a hardware implementation of a CRC, which structure is most commonly employed to compute the remainder efficiently?

- A. Linear‑feedback shift register (LFSR) with XOR taps
- B. Binary counter
- C. FIFO buffer
- D. Multiplexer tree

<details><summary>看答案</summary>

正解：A。An LFSR performs the modulo‑2 division in a single pass using shift operations and XOR feedback, matching the polynomial algebra of CRCs.

</details>

**4.** Given the 7‑bit data word $M = 1011001$ and the generator polynomial $G(x)=x^{3}+x+1$ (binary representation 1011), compute the CRC remainder (3 bits) that must be appended to the data.  Provide the remainder as a decimal integer.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

1. Append three zeros to the data: $1011001\,000$.  
2. Perform modulo‑2 long division by the divisor 1011:  

   - Align divisor with the leftmost 1 and XOR → first four bits become 0000.  
   - Continue shifting; the only other XOR occurs when the leading 1 reaches position 6, producing the pattern …0011 at the end.  
3. After the last possible subtraction (position 6) the remaining three bits are the remainder: **011**.  
4. Convert binary 011 to decimal → $3$.  

$\boxed{3}$

</details>

**5.** A CRC‑16 is used on a link where bit errors are independent and uniformly random. Approximate the probability that a random error pattern is *not* detected (i.e., the undetected‑error probability).  Express the answer as a decimal number in scientific notation.


<details><summary>看答案</summary>

詳解（式號指本頁正文的公式）：

For a CRC of $r$ bits, the set of detectable error patterns is all but the $2^{r}$ multiples of the generator. With random errors the chance that the error polynomial is a multiple of the generator is $2^{-r}$.  
Here $r = 16$:  

$$
P_{\text{undetected}} = 2^{-16}= \frac{1}{65536}\approx 1.525878906\times10^{-5}.
$$

$\boxed{1.525878906e-05}$

</details>
