# 2.2.3 規格的章節組成：範圍、參考、定義、縮寫

所屬：[2.2 規格文件的結構](../2-2.md)

## 正文

In every 3GPP Technical Specification (TS) or Technical Report (TR) the reader must be able to locate, understand, and reuse the essential building blocks of the document. Four of those blocks—**Scope**, **References**, **Definition of terms, symbols and abbreviations**, and **Abbreviation list**—appear in virtually every specification and form the logical “front‑matter” that frames the technical content. Their consistent placement and content enable engineers to quickly assess whether a document covers the required network functions, to trace the origin of external standards, and to avoid ambiguous terminology when implementing protocols in real‑world networks.  

### Required versus optional elements
The 3GPP editorial rules distinguish **required** elements (shown in bold type in the skeleton) from **optional** ones (normal weight). Required elements **shall not be removed** from the skeleton TS/TR, whereas optional elements may be omitted if they are not needed for the particular subject matter [TS 21.801 §5.1.3]. This rule guarantees that every specification contains at least the minimal set of front‑matter needed for interoperability.  

### Scope
The **Scope** clause defines the boundary of the specification: it states *what* is covered, *what* is excluded, and often provides a high‑level diagram of the functional split. According to the official template, the Scope element may contain **text, figures, tables, notes** [TS 21.801 §5.1.3]. No normative requirements belong to the Scope; it is purely informative, serving as a contract between the author and the implementer.  

### Example – counting items in Scope
Assume a new TS describes a radio access network feature and includes three figures and two tables inside the Scope. The total number of “content items” in the Scope is  

$$
N_{\text{Scope}} = N_{\text{text}} + N_{\text{fig}} + N_{\text{tbl}} + N_{\text{notes}} = 1 + 3 + 2 + 0 = 6
$$

where we count the introductory paragraph as a single text item. This simple count helps a reviewer verify that the Scope is not overloaded with detailed design, which should be moved to later clauses.  

### References
The **References** clause lists every external document that the current TS/TR cites. Its permitted content is **stock text followed by numbered references of documents specifically cited in the TS/TR** [TS 21.801 §5.1.3]. The numbering is strict: each reference appears once and is used throughout the document via the notation “[ref‑x]”. Because the list is generated from the citations, it guarantees traceability of every normative or informative claim back to its source.  

### Example – building a reference list
A specification cites three external standards: 3GPP TS 36.300, ITU‑R M.3010, and IEEE 802.11ax. The References clause will therefore contain three numbered entries:

1. 3GPP TS 36.300 – “E‑UTRA and E‑UTRAN; Overall description”.
2. ITU‑R M.3010 – “Framework for IMT‑2020”.
3. IEEE 802.11ax – “High‑Efficiency WLAN”.

The total number of reference entries, $N_{\text{ref}} = 3$, matches the number of distinct citations in the text.  

### Definition of terms, symbols and abbreviations
The **Definition of terms, symbols and abbreviations** element provides a controlled vocabulary for the whole document. Its content consists of **stock text plus specific definitions** (note 4) and may also include explanatory notes [TS 21.801 §5.1.3]. By centralising terminology, the specification avoids contradictory interpretations across clauses and ensures that all implementers share the same meaning for key concepts such as “UE”, “gNB”, or “QoS”.  

### Example – extracting a definition
Suppose the spec introduces the term **“Logical Channel”**. The entry would read:

*Logical Channel*: A transport channel that carries data of a specific type between the UE and the gNB.  

If the spec also defines the symbol **$L_{\text{CH}}$** for the logical channel identifier, the same element records that mapping.  

### Abbreviation list (often merged with definitions)
Although the reference table does not list a separate “Abbreviation” element, the **Definition of terms, symbols and abbreviations** clause typically contains an abbreviation sub‑section. Each abbreviation is expanded once, e.g., **“RRC – Radio Resource Control”**. This practice eliminates the need for readers to guess the meaning of acronyms that appear throughout the document.  

### Common mistakes and practical notes

| Mistake | Why it matters | Remedy |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| **Omitting a required element** (e.g., leaving out Scope) | Violates the skeleton rule; downstream tools may fail to parse the document. | Verify the bold‑type checklist before finalising the draft. |
| **Placing normative language (shall/should) inside an informative element** | Informative elements **shall not contain normative content** [TS 21.801 §5.1.3]; this can cause ambiguous compliance testing. | Keep all “shall” statements inside the normative main body (clauses). |
| **Citing a document in the text but not listing it in References** | Breaks traceability; reviewers cannot locate the source. | Use the automated citation generator that populates the References list. |
| **Defining the same term twice with different meanings** | Leads to contradictory implementations. | Consolidate all definitions in the single “Definition of terms, symbols and abbreviations” element. |
| **Mixing stock text with custom content in References** | The template requires *stock text* followed by the numbered list; extra prose may be rejected. | Follow the exact wording “stock text followed by numbered references…”. |

By adhering to the prescribed structure, authors ensure that every 3GPP document is both human‑readable and machine‑processable, which is essential for large‑scale network roll‑outs and for training domain‑specific language models that rely on consistent section headings and terminology.
